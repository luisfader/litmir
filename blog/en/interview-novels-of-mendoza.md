# "The acequias are the city's veins": Robert Fader on his Mendoza novels

*2 November 2026 · Literature · Interview*

**Foreign queens, foxes who know too much, water running backwards and a province turned into the nation's capital.** We spoke with Robert Fader about the Mendoza of his novels, including *El misterio de los Fader*, *La reina de la Vendimia* and *Anomalía*, stories written far from home.

First, the author explains where he is looking from. "I don't feel like a true-born Mendocino. I look at Mendoza from a distance. When you're there, a poplar is a poplar and a weeping willow is just a weeping willow. But distance can give you a thousand ideas. What was routine for me has become a forgotten aesthetic reference that makes me nostalgic. That's why I combine Mendoza with magic: it really is a magical place, but you hardly think about that while you're there."

**Natalia, Jana and Natasha are all foreigners competing in your Vendimia, Mendoza's great grape harvest festival. Why does your Vendimia always crown a foreigner?**

What a good question. I hadn't thought about it. Maybe it's because one day I'd like to go back with my wife, who is a foreigner. Perhaps I have an unconscious fantasy of seeing her shine on that stage, because I think she's beautiful and I'm sure she would deserve the title of National Vendimia Queen. And maybe there's another reason: I can't see myself with a Mendocina. But I can see myself with a foreign woman who admires Mendoza and, thanks to it, falls even more in love with me.

**A briefcase of mead floats down your acequias, Mendoza's irrigation channels, and in *La reina de la Vendimia* the water runs backwards. What is the acequia in your writing?**

It's something I want to show everyone, because it's very hard to explain. For me it was part of every day: watching the water run, even getting into it in summer when I was a child. Pure mountain water. I couldn't imagine not having it. But now I live in a metropolis with no acequias and no mountains nearby, so the acequia has become something romantic, something that gives life. It's like the veins that carry the city's blood.

**You turned the engineer Carlos Fader into Karl, immortal and guilty of selling the elixir to General Roca. What was it like to take on a real figure and your own surname in that way?**

My origin is a great mystery that, even at my age, I want to solve. Since that will take time, for now I fantasise and give it a touch of magical realism. And why not with historical figures? Karl Fader, who later became the engineer Carlos Fader, really is my ancestor. My grandfather was also called Carlos Fader. And to me, they are both a great mystery.

**Luis shouts "Mendoza is Huarpe, not Mapuche!" and in *La reina de la Vendimia* you awaken the Mulánima beneath the ruins of San Francisco. What are you looking for in this mythology and in such a disputed history?**

It's quite a contradictory thing in me. I've never felt part of a specific nationality or of an Indigenous people. The Huarpe line served to highlight what I, at least, studied at school: that they are our ancestors, not the Mapuche. I'm sure a Mapuche person won't like that at all, but I want to calm things down a little: I'm quite ignorant on this subject. It was a literary device to give that moment an epic feel.

While researching Slavic mythology, which is closest to me now, I realised I had absolutely no idea about the mythology of my own province. I won't rewrite those passages. If I ever feel like doing something new, I may research the Huarpe more seriously, although there really is very little material.

**There's the fox who may be Roca in *El misterio de los Fader* and the talking wooden fox in *Anomalía*. Why a fox?**

It's very curious, because the fox isn't a typical animal in our mythology. In Russian literature, though, the fox is very cunning and always looks for ways to profit at others' expense. In "Kolobok", a children's tale, she tricks a little round bun and ends up eating it. And it so happens that Roca was nicknamed "the Fox", so I decided to mix the two. No doubt also because he was the one who led the Conquest of the Desert. In *Anomalía*, I simply felt like using a fox again, this time a wooden one. There are mountain foxes roaming around Mendoza too, although I never came across one myself.

**Mendoza as capital, Sergio signing decrees under the grape arbour, a Republic of Mendoza with Irina. Is it a joke, a wish or political criticism?**

I think all three. Of course it's a joke to imagine my dad becoming president of Argentina; it's a joke even to him. But Mendoza has its own character. It's an oasis in the middle of a desert, and Mendocinos live, or at least I lived, as if on an island cut off from national reality, in our own world, in our imagination. That doesn't make us think we're better than anyone. And I find it funny because, although it's a fairly quiet, peaceful place, Mendoza is always politicised, and sooner or later that has an impact on the whole country.

**Sergio appears in almost all your novels, and in *Ladro di cuori 3* there's a Don Sergio whose son lives in Russia and writes down his stories. How much of your father is there in this?**

What I most want to highlight is the admiration I feel for him. I always give him an important role: the owner of a vineyard or a wine business, or even an apparent mafioso. But in the end the reader realises he's good. I think that reflects the affection he has for me and the respect I have for him. It's a mutual relationship.

**In the prologue to *El misterio de los Fader* you say you haven't been back and that one day you and Mendoza will meet again. Are these novels your way of going back? And what would happen if you really did return?**

Of course they are. They're ways of returning to Mendoza, of respecting it, and of giving thanks for the childhood and youth I spent there. Mendoza made me a romantic, a dreamer and a thinker. It made me think about the world in a majestic way: with the mountains to the west, the water in the acequias, the occasional annoying Zonda wind, the rare layer of snow that lasts five hours, the morning frosts. All of that made me who I am and prepared me to discover the world in a very special way.

And if I do go back, the first thing I'll do is visit all the places I describe in my novels and relive them right there.
