### The Moscow Metro · 0:45

That night, they couldn't go home. The police were waiting outside Alex and Olga's building, and a woman from the third floor had set up a camping chair at the entrance with a thermos and a pair of binoculars. So they decided to spend the night in the one place that never really sleeps: the Moscow metro.

The Moscow metro is the kind of place that tourists photograph more than museums. Some stations have marble walls, bronze statues, and chandeliers that look as if they were stolen from a palace. Komsomolskaya, which is the station where three railway stations meet, has a yellow ceiling with mosaics. Mayakovskaya, where they got off first, has a ceiling covered in small pictures of the sky.

“In 3024,” said Alex, looking up, “the metro is a grey tube. It's so fast that you can't see anything. Nobody has ever looked at the ceiling.”

“Then look now,” said Olga. She took his hand, and for a few minutes they stood together under the mosaics, two people that the whole city was searching for, looking at a painted sky.

Lucy, who was standing a little way behind them, quietly took out her sketchbook. Then, for once, she put it away again without drawing anything.

Kennet bought tickets for everyone from a machine that talked to him in Russian. He answered it politely in English. The machine and Kennet didn't understand each other at all, but they both seemed satisfied.

The metro closes at one in the morning, so they had about an hour. They rode the Circle Line, which is the line that goes round and round the city centre and never ends. It was the perfect hiding place: they could sit in the last carriage, go round and round, and nobody would notice.

But the metro at night is full of interesting people.

At Kurskaya, a man who was carrying a double bass got on. He sat next to Kennet and asked him if he was a basketball player. Kennet said he wasn't. The man asked if he was a bodyguard. Kennet said he wasn't. The man asked if he was an alien. Kennet looked at him calmly and said, “Only on Tuesdays.” The man laughed for three stations.

At Taganskaya, a group of students got on. They were the kind of students who carry guitars everywhere. They were wearing foil hats, but they had decorated them with stickers and flowers, so they looked more like party hats.

“We're not afraid of aliens,” one of them explained to Lucy. “We just like the hats. It's a new fashion.”

At Paveletskaya, a busker got on — an old man with an accordion and a face that had seen a lot of metro stations. He started to play a sad, beautiful waltz, and for a moment the whole carriage was quiet.

Then the train stopped between stations.

The lights flickered. The waltz stopped. And through the window, in the dark tunnel, Alex saw a grey shape moving slowly along the tracks. It was a small maintenance train — and on its roof, turning slowly, was a vacuum.

“No,” whispered Olga. “Not here too.”

The vacuum turned towards their carriage. Silvery mist began to float out of the passengers — out of the students, out of the man with the double bass, out of the busker. The students' smiles disappeared. The man with the double bass frowned at his instrument as if it had insulted him. The busker put his accordion down and stared at the floor.

Alex stood up. He didn't know why. He just knew that he couldn't sit there and watch.

“Excuse me,” he said loudly to the whole carriage. “Can I tell you a joke?”

Everybody looked at him. A few people recognized him. Somebody gasped.

“It's the alien!”

“Yes,” said Alex. “Well — no. But listen. A man from the future goes into a café. He orders a coffee. The woman says, ‘That's two hundred roubles.’ He gives her his wrist.”

Silence.

“It's a true story,” he added. “It was me.”

Nobody laughed. The vacuum outside kept turning. Alex's face went red. He looked at Olga desperately.

And then the busker, the old man with the accordion, started to laugh. Not a big laugh. A small, rusty, surprised laugh, as if he had found an old coin in his pocket. The silver mist above his head stopped floating away. It went back into him, like a cat going back into a warm house.

The students looked at the busker. One of them giggled. Then another. Then the man with the double bass, who said, “His *wrist*!” and slapped his knee.

The vacuum outside began to shake, as if it couldn't decide which way to turn. Then, with an angry hiss, the maintenance train moved away down the tunnel.

The lights came back. The train started to move. The busker picked up his accordion and played something fast and cheerful.

Olga looked at her husband with an expression that he had never seen before.

“Alex,” she said slowly. “Do you understand what just happened?”

“Nobody laughed at my joke?”

“Somebody laughed *with* you,” said Olga. “And the machine couldn't take it.”

### Относительные придаточные

Who — для людей, which — для предметов и животных, that — для людей и предметов (в неформальной речи), where — для мест. Who/which/that можно опустить, если после них стоит подлежащее: the station (that) we liked. Соедините предложения с помощью who, which или where.

1. Komsomolskaya is a station. Three railway stations meet there. → Komsomolskaya is the station …

2. A man got on at Kurskaya. He was carrying a double bass. → A man …

3. They rode the Circle Line. It goes round and round the centre. → They rode the Circle Line, …

4. The students wore foil hats. They had decorated them with stickers. → The students wore foil hats …

5. The busker had a face. It had seen a lot of stations. → The busker had a face …

6. This is the carriage. Alex told his joke here. → This is the carriage …
