### Danilovsky Market

The next morning, they hid in a market.

Danilovsky Market was a huge round building with a roof like a circus tent made of concrete. Inside, it was loud, colourful, and smelled of everything at once: fresh bread, smoked fish, coriander, cheese, coffee, flowers, and something spicy from Uzbekistan that made Kennet's eyes water. There were rows and rows of stalls. Mountains of tomatoes. Towers of honey jars. Fish lying on ice with their mouths open, looking as surprised as Alex felt most of the time.

“Nobody will look for an alien here,” said Olga. “Everybody here is looking at prices.”

She was right. In the market, people were too busy arguing about cucumbers to watch television. At one stall, a man was weighing potatoes as if they were gold. At another, two old ladies were tasting pickled cabbage and disagreeing about it very loudly.

Nina had a friend who sold fish there — a large, cheerful woman called Zoya, who used to work with Nina in a school kitchen thirty years ago.

“You want to hide him?” said Zoya, looking at Alex from head to toe. “Easy. Put this on.”

She gave him a white apron, a pair of rubber gloves, and a hat with a picture of a fish on it.

“Nobody looks at a fishmonger's face,” she explained. “They only look at the fish.”

So for the whole morning, Alex was a fishmonger.

It was harder than repairing a time machine. Customers asked him questions he couldn't answer. *Is this salmon fresh? Where was this trout caught? Can you clean it for me?* Alex didn't know what “clean” meant for a fish. When he tried, the fish slipped out of his hands, flew over the counter, and landed in a woman's shopping bag. The woman looked into her bag and said, “I didn't order that one.” Then she looked again and said, “But it looks good. How much?”

Zoya laughed so hard she had to sit down.

“When I was young,” she told him, wiping her eyes, “I used to drop fish all the time. My boss would shout at me every morning. But I always sold the fish that I dropped. People think a fish that flies is a happy fish.”

Meanwhile, Lucy was painting the market. She had set up her easel between the honey stall and the flower stall, and a small crowd had gathered around her. Market people loved her. The honey seller gave her a jar of honey for her tea. The flower seller put a rose behind her ear. An old man who sold walnuts told her that she looked like his first love, who used to sing in a choir in 1965.

Olga, who was helping Zoya with the scales, watched all this with mixed feelings.

“Everybody loves her,” she said to Nina.

“She's a guest,” said Nina. “And she's polite. And she's beautiful. People always give honey to beautiful, polite guests.”

“Nobody ever gave *me* honey.”

Nina looked at her niece for a long moment.

“Olga,” she said. “When you were seven, you used to come to this market with me every Saturday. The honey seller's father would give you a piece of honeycomb every single time. You would eat it all and get it in your hair. Every Saturday.”

Olga blinked. She had completely forgotten.

“So people *did* give me honey.”

“For years,” said Nina. “You just don't need it anymore. You've got a husband who looks at you like you're the only woman in history.” She paused. “Which, to be fair, for a while you were.”

Olga laughed in spite of herself.

At noon, there was trouble. A group of men with foil hats came into the market, carrying printed photos of Alex and asking every stallholder the same question: “Have you seen this alien?” One of them was a very thin man in a grey coat who didn't look like the others. He wasn't looking at the photos. He was looking at the people. Especially at the ones who were laughing.

Kennet, who had been sitting by the coffee stall, stood up slowly.

“That one isn't a neighbour,” he said quietly. “He works for the trucks. Look at his shoes. They're covered in the same grey dust I saw at Ostankino.”

The thin man was walking towards the fish stall.

Zoya didn't panic. She calmly picked up the biggest fish on the counter — a huge silver salmon — and put it straight into Alex's arms.

“Hold it,” she ordered. “Hold it up. High. In front of your face.”

Alex held up the fish. The thin man stopped in front of the stall. He looked at the photo. He looked at the fish. The fish looked back at him with its mouth open.

“Excuse me,” said the man. “Have you seen this alien?”

“No,” said the fish, in Alex's voice.

The man stared at the salmon for a very long time. Then, without a word, he turned and left the market.

### 2 Грамматика

### Used to и would

Used to + глагол описывает привычки и состояния в прошлом, которых больше нет: Zoya used to work in a school kitchen. Would + глагол — только повторяющиеся действия (не состояния): My boss would shout at me every morning. Для состояний (be, have, like, know) would не используется. Заполните пропуски: used to или would. Иногда возможны оба варианта.

1. Zoya _______ work with Nina in a school kitchen.

2. When she was young, Zoya _______ drop fish all the time.

3. Her boss _______ shout at her every morning.

4. Olga _______ come to the market every Saturday.

5. The honey seller's father _______ give her a piece of honeycomb.

6. The walnut seller's first love _______ sing in a choir.
