### The State Tretyakov Gallery

The Tretyakov Gallery was built more than a hundred years ago, and it looked like something from a fairy tale: a red and white building with a decorated entrance, as if a giant had baked it like a cake. Inside, the rooms were quiet and warm, and the walls were covered with paintings — forests, rivers, princes, bears, and serious people in serious hats.

“Perfect,” whispered Lucy as they bought their tickets. “Nobody in a museum is looking at other visitors. They're all pretending to understand art.”

But Alex had a problem. His photo had been shown on every channel the night before, and the woman at the ticket desk was looking at him a little too long.

“You need a disguise,” said Lucy. She took off her cream-coloured scarf and wrapped it around Alex's neck. Then she gave him her round glasses and her sketchbook. “There. Now you're an art student. Art students are invisible. Nobody wants to talk to them, because they might explain their feelings.”

Olga looked at her husband: in Lucy's scarf, with Lucy's glasses, holding Lucy's sketchbook.

“Lovely,” she said. “Very… Lucy.”

They walked through the rooms in a group. Lucy explained the paintings in a soft, excited voice. This one was painted in 1871. That one was bought by Pavel Tretyakov himself, the man who had started the collection. This forest was painted by Shishkin, and the bears in it were added by another artist, because Shishkin wasn't very good at bears.

“Really?” said Alex. “Two artists for one picture?”

“Teamwork,” said Lucy, smiling at him.

Olga, walking behind them, studied a painting of a woman in a dark carriage. The woman looked proud, cold, and slightly annoyed. Olga felt that they understood each other perfectly.

For half an hour, everything was fine. Alex was even beginning to enjoy himself. Then they came to a large hall, and a guide was standing in the middle of it with a group of tourists — and all the tourists were wearing aluminium foil hats.

“And here,” the guide was saying, “we see a classic Russian landscape. The fields are painted in warm colours. The sky is… wait.” She stopped and pointed. “Is that the alien?”

Twenty foil hats turned towards Alex.

For one terrible second, nobody moved. Then Lucy did something very quick and very clever. She grabbed an empty picture frame that was leaning against the wall — it had been taken down for cleaning — and held it up in front of Alex's face.

“Don't move,” she whispered. “You're art now.”

Alex froze. Lucy stepped back and spoke to the tourists in a loud, confident voice.

“Ladies and gentlemen, this is a living installation. It's called *The Man Who Is Suspected*. It was created by a young British artist to show how paranoia is spread by television. Please don't touch the exhibit.”

The tourists looked at Alex in the frame. Alex tried very hard to look like art. He chose an expression that was mysterious, sad, and a bit Shishkin.

“It's very realistic,” said one tourist.

“The ears are especially well done,” said another.

“Is it for sale?” asked a third.

Olga stood in a corner with her arms crossed, watching her husband being admired by twenty tourists while a beautiful Englishwoman described him as a masterpiece.

“Is it for sale?” she repeated under her breath. “It's married.”

The guide moved her group on to the next room, and Alex finally breathed. Lucy lowered the frame. Her cheeks were pink, and she was laughing.

“You were brilliant,” she told him. “Honestly. You were born to be a painting.”

“Thank you,” said Alex, laughing too.

“Can we go now?” said Olga.

But at that moment, Kennet, who had stayed by the window the whole time, spoke without turning around.

“Look at this,” he said.

They joined him. Outside, on the street in front of the gallery, a grey truck had stopped. *MOSCOW FRESH AIR.* The big vacuum on its roof was slowly turning, like a head looking for something.

At the entrance of the gallery, a family was coming out. The little girl was laughing and pointing at the pigeons. Her father was smiling. Then the vacuum turned towards them, and a thin, silvery mist floated out of the people and up into the machine. It was hard to see — like breath on a cold morning.

The little girl stopped laughing. Her father checked his phone, sighed, and said something sharp to his wife.

“Did you see that?” whispered Olga.

“I saw it,” said Kennet. “It's taking something from them.”

Lucy was already drawing it — fast, without looking at the page. Olga, for once, didn't mind.

“We have to find out where those trucks go,” said Olga. “Kennet — can you follow one?”

Kennet looked at her calmly.

“I followed a deer for three days in Scotland once,” he said. “A truck is much louder.”

### 2 Грамматика

### Страдательный залог (Passive)

Passive используется, когда важнее действие или предмет, а не тот, кто действует: be + третья форма глагола. The gallery was built more than a hundred years ago. The bears were added by another artist. Исполнителя можно назвать с by. Перепишите предложения в страдательном залоге.

1. Somebody built the gallery more than a hundred years ago. → The gallery …

2. Pavel Tretyakov bought this painting. → This painting …

3. Another artist added the bears. → The bears …

4. Someone took the frame down for cleaning. → The frame …

5. Television spreads paranoia. → Paranoia …

6. The truck took the people's good mood. → The people's good mood …
