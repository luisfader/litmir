### Nina's Café · One Month Later

A month later, Moscow was almost back to normal.

Almost, because some things had changed for the better. The aluminium foil was back in the shops, and people were using it for baking again. Nobody checked their neighbours' ears any more, except as a joke. Sergeant Popov had sent Alex a letter of apology on official police paper, with a small drawing of a smiling alien at the bottom. And the Moscow Fresh Air trucks had been repainted in bright colours and given to the city's parks, where they now really did clean the air.

Doctor Gennady Grumov's programme had been cancelled. The day after the show, he had resigned from the television centre. The police had interviewed him for nine hours. In the end, they decided that he had committed many small crimes but one big kindness: on the night of the release, he had laughed for the first time, and every person in Moscow had seen it. That, said the judge, was punishment and therapy at the same time.

He now had a new job. He would be performing every Thursday night at a small comedy club on Pushkin Street — the same club where nobody had laughed twenty years ago.

“His jokes are still terrible,” said Nina, reading the review in the newspaper. “But listen to this: *‘Mr Grumov tells the worst jokes in Moscow. However, he laughs at them so happily that the whole audience laughs with him. It is the best bad evening in the city.’*”

“Will you go?” asked Olga.

“I'm going this Thursday,” said Nina. “I've already bought tickets for everyone.”

***

In the café, everybody was getting ready for a party.

Lucy's exhibition was opening that evening at a gallery near the Tretyakov. It was called *Moscow, Hiding*, and it was twenty paintings: the market, the metro, the planetarium dome, Gorky Park, a giraffe licking a man's ear, a steam room full of pink people in felt hats. The biggest painting was called *The Man Who Is Suspected*. It showed Alex in an empty gold frame in the middle of a museum, trying very hard to look like art.

“Everybody's going to be there,” said Lucy. “Zoya. The zookeeper. Gleb and Vera. Rimma has promised to do everyone's hair.”

“Kennet is going to carry the paintings, isn't he?” asked Olga with a smile.

“No,” said Lucy happily. “I'm carrying them. Kennet's going to hold my hand.”

Kennet, sitting at the table with a cup of tea that looked tiny in his hand, went pink. He and Lucy had decided to stay in Moscow for the summer. Lucy wanted to paint more. Kennet had found a job at the zoo, helping the old zookeeper. Lyuba the giraffe had decided that she liked him almost as much as Gleb.

***

There was one more painting at the exhibition that nobody had seen yet. Lucy had painted it secretly, and she gave it to Olga before they left the café.

It was a portrait of Olga and Alex sitting at the window of Nina's café. Alex was looking at Olga. Olga was drawing. And somewhere in the picture, if you looked carefully, there was a tiny ginger cat, hiding behind the coffee machine.

Olga looked at it for a long time. Her eyes were wet.

“Lucy,” she said. “I'm so sorry I was jealous of you.”

“I'm so glad I was jealous of you,” said Lucy. “It meant I found a friend.”

They hugged. Kennet and Alex looked at each other over their heads, the way men do when they understand that something important is happening and that the best thing they can do is be quiet.

***

That night, after the exhibition, after the speeches and the music and Fedya's pancakes and Rimma doing the zookeeper's beard, Alex and Olga walked home along the river.

It was a warm spring night. The city was full of noise — music from a bar, a group of students singing, a dog barking happily at a boat. Somebody, somewhere, was laughing at a joke that wasn't funny.

“What will you be doing this time next year?” asked Olga.

Alex thought about it.

“I'll probably be repairing computers,” he said. “I'll be making terrible coffee. And I'll definitely still be explaining to people that I'm not an alien.”

“And by this time next year,” said Olga, “I'll have finished my new book.”

“What's it about?”

Olga smiled.

“It's about a man from the future,” she said, “who comes to a city where everyone is afraid. He's so bad at hiding that the whole city ends up helping him. And there's a cat.”

“There's always a cat.”

“There's always a cat,” Olga agreed. She took his arm. “What do you think? Will people believe it?”

Alex looked at the city — the lights on the river, the people laughing on the bridges, the tower at Ostankino blinking calmly in the distance.

“No,” he said. “But they'll enjoy it.”

**THE END**

### 2 Грамматика

### Будущее время: повторение

Will — решения в момент речи и предсказания: They'll enjoy it. Be going to — планы и намерения: Kennet's going to hold my hand. Present Continuous — договорённости: I'm going this Thursday. Future Continuous (will be + -ing) — действие в процессе в определённый момент будущего: What will you be doing this time next year? Future Perfect (will have + третья форма) — действие, которое закончится к моменту в будущем: I'll have finished my book. Выберите правильный вариант.

1. Nina has bought the tickets. She (will go / is going) to the club this Thursday.

2. This time next year, Alex (will be repairing / will repair) computers.

3. By next spring, Olga (will finish / will have finished) her new book.

4. “Kennet (is going to / will have) hold my hand,” said Lucy.

5. Grumov (will be performing / will have performed) every Thursday night.

6. “Will people believe it?” — “No, but they (will enjoy / are enjoying) it.”
