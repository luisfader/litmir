### The Banya

The banya was in an old brick building near the river, with a wooden door, a sign with a picture of a birch leaf, and a man at the entrance who was asleep in a chair. Zoya had given them the address. “Nobody will look for you there,” she had said. “In a banya, everybody looks the same. Pink.”

The women went to the women's side with Nina, who announced that she had not been to a banya for thirty years and intended to stay all day. The men — Alex and Kennet — went to the men's side.

A banya is a Russian steam bath, and it is a serious place. First, you get undressed and put on a felt hat that looks like a small bell. The hat protects your head from the heat. Then you go into the steam room, which is very, very hot. You sit on a wooden bench. Someone throws water onto the hot stones, and a wave of steam hits you like a warm wall. Then, if you're brave, someone hits you gently with a bunch of birch branches, which is called a *venik*. And then — this is the most important part — you run outside and jump into cold water.

“This is a strange tradition, isn't it?” said Alex, sitting on the top bench in his felt hat, red as a tomato.

“It's good for you, isn't it?” said Kennet, who was sitting next to him and seemed completely comfortable. “In Finland they do the same. In Scotland we just stand in the rain. It's similar.”

An old man on the lower bench, with a huge white beard and a venik in his hand, looked up at them.

“You're not from here, are you?” he said.

“No,” said Kennet. “London.”

“And you?” He looked at Alex.

“From… further away,” said Alex.

The old man studied Alex's face for a long moment. Alex held his breath. Then the old man shrugged.

“In the banya,” he said, “nobody is from anywhere. We're all just people who are too hot.” He threw a ladle of water on the stones, and the steam rose up so strongly that Alex couldn't see anything for ten seconds.

When they were sitting outside afterwards, wrapped in towels and drinking hot tea with honey on a wooden terrace, Alex finally felt calm for the first time in days.

Kennet was quiet. He was looking at the river.

“You've known Lucy for a long time, haven't you?” said Alex.

“Six years,” said Kennet. “We met at an art school in London. I was the model. They needed someone big to draw. She was the only student who drew my face. The others only drew my shoulders.”

“That's nice, isn't it?”

“It is.” Kennet was silent for a while. “I've been her friend for six years. I carry her easel. I carry her paints. I carry her suitcase up the stairs in every hotel in Europe. She calls me ‘my bear’.”

Alex looked at him. Then, slowly, he understood.

“You're in love with her, aren't you?”

Kennet didn't answer. He didn't need to. His ears had gone even redder than the rest of him, which was difficult after a banya.

“Why don't you tell her?” asked Alex.

“Look at her,” said Kennet. “She's the most beautiful woman I've ever seen. She's clever and talented. She's going to be a famous artist. And I'm… a big man who carries things.” He shrugged. “Women like her don't fall in love with the man who carries things.”

Alex thought about it. A year ago, he had known nothing about women. Now he knew a little. Not much. But a little.

“Kennet,” he said. “In 3024, I thought love was about finding the most perfect woman. Like choosing the best computer in a shop. I was wrong. Love is about finding the person who notices you. Who sees you.” He took a sip of tea. “She drew your face, didn't she? When everybody else only drew your shoulders.”

Kennet turned slowly and looked at him.

“And another thing,” Alex added. “She doesn't need you to carry the easel. She could buy a lighter one. She's had six years to buy a lighter one.”

Kennet opened his mouth. Then he closed it. Then he laughed — a deep, surprised laugh that made the birds fly off the roof.

“You're cleverer than you look, aren't you?”

“Everybody says that,” said Alex. “It's not really a compliment, is it?”

At that moment, the door to the terrace opened, and the old man with the white beard came out, holding his phone.

“Young man,” he said to Alex. “You're on television again.”

He showed them the screen. It was Doctor Grumov, standing in his studio, looking even more serious than usual.

*“Citizens! The alien is hiding among us! But he will not hide for long. This Friday at nine o'clock, in a special live edition of The Hour of Truth, I will reveal his final secret — and I will show you how we can catch him. Do not miss it. Your future depends on it.”*

The old man put his phone away.

“I don't believe a word that man says,” he said. “He told my wife it would rain every day for three years when he was a weatherman. She still carries an umbrella in July.” He looked at Alex. “Finish your tea, alien. You've got work to do, haven't you?”

### Разделительные вопросы (question tags)

Question tag — короткий вопрос в конце предложения: «не так ли?». После утверждения — отрицательный хвостик, после отрицания — положительный. Вспомогательный глагол берётся из предложения: You've known her for a long time, haven't you? It's good for you, isn't it? She drew your face, didn't she? It's not a compliment, is it?
