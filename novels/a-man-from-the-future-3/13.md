### The Moscow Planetarium

In the evening, the library closed, and they had to move again. Gleb suggested the planetarium, because it was next door to the zoo, it was dark inside, and there was a special evening show that week.

When they saw the poster, they almost turned back.

**ALIENS AMONG US? A Journey Through the Universe. Special Show. Tonight 19:00.**

“This is the worst place in Moscow for me,” said Alex.

“Or the best,” said Lucy. “Who would look for an alien at a show about aliens? It's like hiding a book in a library.”

The Moscow Planetarium has a huge silver dome, like a giant metal egg sitting on top of the building. Inside, there is a museum about space, with meteorites you can touch, a scale that tells you how much you would weigh on Mars, and old Soviet spacesuits. And there is the Star Hall: a round room with soft chairs that lean back, and a ceiling that becomes the night sky.

Alex stood on the Mars scale. It said thirty kilograms.

“I wish I weighed this much on Earth,” said Nina, who had followed them with her bag of pancakes and refused to go home.

They found seats in the last row of the Star Hall. The lights went down. Thousands of stars appeared above them. A calm voice began to talk about galaxies, planets, and the question that humans have always asked: *Are we alone?*

For a few minutes, it was wonderful. Alex lay back in his chair and looked at the stars. In 3024, the sky above Moscow was always grey. He had never seen so many stars in his life.

“I wish I could show this to my mother,” he whispered.

Olga took his hand in the dark.

Then the voice said: “And now, ladies and gentlemen — a special guest!”

A spotlight came on. It was pointing at the stage, where a man in a silver costume with green antennas was standing and waving. It was an actor, part of the show. The audience laughed and clapped.

“Many people believe aliens look like this,” said the voice. “But real scientists think aliens — if they exist — might be completely different. They might be tiny. They might be made of gas. They might even look… just like us!”

The spotlight began to move across the audience, as part of the show. It moved from row to row, stopping on random people. Everyone laughed each time.

“Could *this* be an alien?” — a woman with a baby.

“Or *this*?” — a man eating popcorn.

The spotlight moved closer and closer to the last row.

“Oh no,” whispered Olga. “If only we'd sat somewhere else.”

“I wish I'd stayed in the library,” whispered Alex.

“Or *this*?” said the voice — and the spotlight stopped directly on Alex's face.

For a second, there was silence. Then somebody in the third row stood up.

“It's him! It's the alien from *The Hour of Truth*!”

Two hundred people turned around. Somebody screamed. Somebody started filming. A child shouted, “Mummy, it's a real one!” The actor in the silver costume took off his antennas and looked at Alex with great professional interest.

Alex did the only thing he could think of. He stood up slowly, raised one hand, and said, in a deep, serious voice:

“Greetings, Earth people.”

The room went silent.

“I come from the planet… Shishkin,” Alex continued. Olga closed her eyes. “On my planet, we eat only borscht. We pay with our wrists. And we have very, very clean ears.” He paused. “Also, I am part of this show.”

There was a long silence.

Then the actor in the silver costume, who was a professional and knew a gift when he saw one, began to clap loudly.

“Ladies and gentlemen!” he shouted. “Our *second* special guest! A big round of applause!”

The audience, relieved, laughed and clapped. The child who had screamed was now laughing so hard that she fell off her chair. The man with the popcorn threw some in the air.

And as everyone laughed together, Olga — who was watching the room, not the stage — saw something. At the back door of the Star Hall, a thin man in a grey coat with dusty shoes had just come in. He was holding something in his hand that looked like a small portable vacuum. He pointed it at the audience.

Nothing happened. The silvery mist didn't come out of anyone. Two hundred people were laughing together, and the machine in his hand just whined, coughed, and died.

The thin man looked at it in disbelief. Then he looked up — straight at Olga.

“Run,” said Olga.

They ran. Out of the emergency exit, past the meteorites, past the Mars scale, out into the cold evening air.

“I wish,” said Alex, out of breath, “that just once, I could go somewhere without running away.”

“Tomorrow,” said Kennet calmly, “I'll take you somewhere where nobody runs. It's too hot to run there.”

“Where?”

Kennet smiled. “A banya. A real Russian banya. Zoya told me about one.”

### 2 Грамматика

### I wish / If only

Wish + Past Simple выражает сожаление о настоящем: I wish I weighed this much (но я вешу больше). Wish + could — о том, чего не можешь сделать сейчас: I wish I could show this to my mother. Wish + Past Perfect — сожаление о прошлом: I wish I'd stayed in the library. If only — более эмоциональный вариант. Поставьте глаголы в правильную форму.

1. Nina: “I wish I _______ (weigh) thirty kilograms on Earth.”

2. Alex: “I wish I _______ (can) show this sky to my mother.”

3. Olga: “If only we _______ (sit) somewhere else!”

4. Alex: “I wish I _______ (stay) in the library.”

5. Alex: “I wish I _______ (not / have) such famous ears.”

6. The thin man: “I wish my machine _______ (work).”
