### Studio 5 · Live · 21:04

“Citizens of Moscow,” said Grumov, walking slowly around the chair. “For a week, this creature has been hiding among you. He has eaten your bread. He has drunk your coffee. He has repaired your computers — and studied them. But tonight, his journey ends.”

He turned to the camera.

“Tonight, you will see the truth with your own eyes. And then we will decide together what to do with him.”

The audience murmured. In the front rows, the people with signs were shouting. A man stood up and shouted, “Send him back to his planet!” Another shouted, “Make him fix my printer first!”

Grumov raised his hand for silence.

“Before we begin,” he said, “I will take a few questions from the audience. The people have the right to ask.”

This was what Olga had been waiting for. She raised her hand.

Grumov pointed at her. A young assistant ran over with a microphone.

Olga stood up. She adjusted her glasses and spoke in the calm, dry voice of a woman from a ministry.

“Good evening, Doctor Grumov. Could you tell us where your photograph of the spaceship was taken?”

“Of course.” Grumov smiled — or rather, his moustache moved. “Near Tverskaya Street. Last summer.”

“And do you know who took the photograph?”

“A witness. A brave citizen who wishes to stay anonymous.”

“I see.” Olga nodded. “I wonder if you could explain why there's no light from the street lamps in the picture. In a real photograph, there would be. Could you tell us why the shadows are painted with a brush?”

The studio went quiet. Grumov's eyes narrowed.

“I'm afraid I don't know what you mean.”

“Then perhaps you could tell us,” said Olga, “whether you have ever seen this book.”

She held up a thin, colourful children's book. On the cover was a red machine in an alley, with a little ginger cat sitting behind it. The title was *The Red Machine*. Below it: *Words and pictures by Olga Kovaleva.*

The camera operator, who had a daughter who loved that book, immediately zoomed in on it.

The audience gasped. On the big screen, the “spaceship” photo and the book cover were now side by side. The same alley. The same machine. The same shadows.

“That proves nothing,” said Grumov sharply. “Anyone could copy a famous spaceship into a book.”

“Of course,” said Olga. “So I'd like to know one more thing. Do you know what is behind the machine in your photograph? There's a very dark shadow there. I'd love to know what it's hiding.”

Grumov said nothing.

Olga lifted her phone and typed one word: *Now.*

In the control room, Lucy pressed a key.

On the giant screen behind Grumov, the “spaceship” picture began to change. Slowly, layer by layer, the dark shadow behind the machine disappeared. The silver glow faded. The mysterious light went away. And underneath, sitting behind the red machine with its tail curled neatly around its paws, was a small, ginger, extremely cheerful cartoon cat.

It was winking.

For two seconds, there was complete silence in Studio 5.

Then somebody in the back row laughed. It was the old man from the banya. A short, surprised laugh, like a cough.

Then his wife laughed. Then Zoya. Then the zookeeper. Then the students in their flowery foil hats. Then the busker, who began to play a cheerful tune on his accordion. Then Nina, who laughed so hard she dropped her pancakes. Then the people with the signs, who looked at the winking cat, looked at each other, and slowly lowered their signs.

“A cat,” said one of them. “The spaceship has a *cat*.”

“It's a drawing,” said another. “It's a drawing from a *children's book*.”

“I've read that book to my son forty times,” said a third, and started laughing helplessly.

The laughter spread through the studio like warm water. Three hundred people, laughing together at the same time, at the same silly, winking cat.

Grumov stood in the middle of the stage. His face had gone from grey to white.

“Stop it,” he said. “Stop laughing. Stop—”

Nobody stopped.

And deep below the building, on Level −3, something began to happen.

***

The Moodvac 3000 had been programmed to collect good moods from all over the city. At that moment, all its sensors were pointing at the biggest source of laughter in Moscow: Studio 5. It tried to suck the laughter in. But this was shared laughter — three hundred people laughing *with* each other — and the machine couldn't take it. It pulled harder. The dials went up. The pressure in the tank rose. The copper pipes began to shake.

What nobody knew — except Alex — was that when the guards had pulled him away from the control panel, he had managed to turn one small switch. It was marked *OVERLOAD PROTECTION*. On the other side of it was a word in faded red letters: *REVERSE*.

In Studio 5, Alex looked at Olga across the room and smiled.

“Could you tell me,” he said to Grumov, very politely, “whether you've checked your pressure dials recently?”

Grumov turned to him.

“What have you done?”

Under their feet, the whole building began to hum.

### Косвенные (вежливые) вопросы

Вежливые вопросы начинаются с Could you tell me…, Do you know…, I wonder…, I'd like to know… После них порядок слов прямой, как в утверждении, и нет do/does/did: Where was it taken? → Could you tell us where it was taken? Для вопросов «да/нет» используйте if / whether: Have you seen this book? → Could you tell us whether you have seen this book? Перепишите вопросы вежливо.

5. What is behind the machine? → I'd like to know …

6. Have you checked your pressure dials? → Could you tell me …
