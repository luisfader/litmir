### The City Library

The city library was the quietest place in Moscow, which made it the perfect hiding place for a man whose face was on every screen. It was also where Gleb worked.

Gleb had changed a lot since last summer. He wore a cardigan now, and a name badge that said *GLEB — ASK ME ANYTHING (QUIETLY)*. When he saw Alex walk in with a cap, sunglasses, and an *I ♥ MOSCOW* T-shirt, he didn't even look surprised. He simply put a finger to his lips and pointed upstairs.

“Reading room four,” he whispered. “Nobody goes there. It's the room with the dictionaries.”

Reading room four had long wooden tables, green lamps, and shelves of enormous books up to the ceiling. It smelled of old paper and floor polish. A single old professor was asleep in the corner, with a dictionary of ancient Greek on his chest.

“If I had known that libraries were this nice,” whispered Alex, sitting down, “I would have come here a year ago.”

“If you had come here a year ago,” whispered Olga, “you would never have found my café.”

“Our café,” corrected Nina, who had come with a bag of pancakes and was ignoring the sign that said *NO FOOD*.

Gleb brought them the old newspapers from the archive. Olga wanted to know everything about Gennady Grumov. If they were going to prove he was a liar, they needed to understand who he was.

It took them two hours. Lucy read the English newspapers. Kennet read the sports pages, because he said you could learn a lot about a city from its football. Olga and Gleb went through twenty years of Moscow papers, page by page, whispering whenever they found something.

What they found was very sad.

Gennady Grumov had wanted to be a comedian since he was a child. He had written jokes in notebooks — hundreds of notebooks. Twenty years ago, he had performed for the first time in a small club on Pushkin Street. A newspaper review from the next day said:

*“Mr Grumov's jokes were so long that some of the audience fell asleep and woke up again before the end. Nobody laughed. At the end, one man asked, ‘Is he finished?’ — and that was the only joke of the evening.”*

“That's terrible,” whispered Lucy.

“If somebody had laughed,” said Olga quietly, “just one person… maybe he would have continued. Maybe he would have become a normal, unfunny comedian, and none of this would have happened.”

There was more. After the club, Grumov had tried other jobs. He had been a weather forecaster — but he had been fired because he forecast rain every single day “to prepare people for disappointment”. He had been a dentist's assistant — but patients complained that he told them sad stories while they couldn't answer. Then, five years ago, he had started *The Hour of Truth*. And suddenly, millions of people were listening to him. Not laughing. But listening.

“He couldn't make people laugh,” said Gleb slowly. “So he decided to make them afraid. It's easier.”

Kennet put down his newspaper.

“There's something else,” he said. “Look.”

It was a small article from six months ago, on page twenty-three. *TV PRESENTER BUYS OLD FACTORY EQUIPMENT.* The photo showed Grumov standing next to a truck full of copper pipes, glass tubes, and dials. The caption said that the equipment would be used for “a new scientific project about air quality”.

“Air quality,” repeated Olga. “*Moscow Fresh Air.*”

“If we had read this six months ago,” said Kennet, “we would have known.”

“If we had read page twenty-three of anything six months ago,” said Nina, “we would be the first people in history to do it.”

The old professor in the corner woke up, looked at them, said “Shh!” very loudly, and went back to sleep.

Olga closed the newspaper. She was thinking hard.

“So we know three things,” she said. “One: the ‘spaceship’ is my drawing, and if we can get the original file, we can prove it — there's a cat under the shadow. Two: Grumov's machine is somewhere at Ostankino, in the same building as his studio. Three: the machine can't take good mood when people laugh *together*.”

“So what's the plan?” asked Alex.

Olga looked at him. A slow smile appeared on her face — the same smile Nina had seen when Olga was seven and had decided to climb the tallest tree in the park.

“We're going to get into the studio,” she said. “And we're going to need a lot of people who can laugh at the same time.”

### Третий тип условных предложений

Third Conditional описывает нереальное прошлое — то, что могло бы случиться, но не случилось: If + Past Perfect, would have + третья форма. If somebody had laughed, he would have continued. If I had known, I would have come. Поставьте глаголы в правильную форму.

1. If Alex _______ (know) about libraries, he _______ (come) earlier.

2. If Alex _______ (come) here a year ago, he _______ (not / find) the café.

3. If somebody _______ (laugh), Grumov _______ (become) a normal comedian.

4. If they _______ (read) the article, they _______ (know) about the machine.

5. If Grumov _______ (not / forecast) rain every day, he _______ (not / be) fired.

6. What _______ you _______ (do) if you _______ (be) in the audience?
