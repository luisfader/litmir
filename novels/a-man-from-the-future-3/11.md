### Moscow Zoo

They needed a computer to look at the picture properly. They also needed somewhere to hide, because in Gorky Park a boy on a skateboard had recognized Alex's ears under the cap and had started shouting about it.

So they went to the zoo.

It was Kennet's idea. “Nobody looks at people in a zoo,” he said. “Everyone looks at the animals. And the animals don't watch television.”

Moscow Zoo is one of the oldest zoos in Europe. It has polar bears, tigers, penguins, an elephant house, a big bird house, and — Fedya would have been very excited — a family of giraffes.

They had been walking around the zoo for about twenty minutes when the problem started. A zookeeper with a bucket of fish had been staring at Alex for some time.

“He's been following us since the penguins,” whispered Lucy.

“He's been looking at my ears,” said Alex.

“Everybody has been looking at your ears for three days,” said Olga. “Quick — in here.”

She pushed them through a door marked *STAFF ONLY*. They found themselves in a warm, high-ceilinged building that smelled of hay. Very high above them, two long necks turned slowly in their direction. Four enormous dark eyes looked down at them with polite curiosity.

“The giraffe house,” whispered Lucy. “Oh, they're beautiful.”

One of the giraffes lowered its head — slowly, slowly, like a crane on a building site — until its face was exactly in front of Alex. It sniffed his hair. Then it sniffed his ear. Then it licked his entire face with a tongue that was dark purple and about half a metre long.

“Ah,” said Alex. “Hello.”

“She likes you,” said a voice behind them.

They turned around. It was the zookeeper with the bucket of fish. He was an old man with a white beard and a friendly, wrinkled face. He closed the door behind him.

“Don't worry,” he said. “I'm not going to call anyone. I've been working at this zoo for forty-one years. I've seen the real animals and I've seen the television ones. The real ones are much nicer.” He nodded at the giraffe, who was now trying to eat Alex's cap. “And her name is Lyuba. She's been waiting for you.”

“Waiting for me?”

“Last summer, a man in a silver suit came here every day for two weeks. He was very polite. He brought her carrots and told her about his day. He said she was the only one in Moscow who listened without interrupting. Then one day he stopped coming.” The old man smiled. “She's been looking at the door ever since. I think she's been hoping he'll come back.”

“That was Gleb,” said Alex slowly. “From 3024. He works in a library now. He has a girlfriend called Vera.”

The giraffe made a soft, sad noise and stopped eating the cap.

“Well,” said the zookeeper. “Life is complicated. Even for giraffes.”

He showed them a small office at the back, with an old computer that had been running since before Olga was born. Alex fixed it in three minutes. Olga found Grumov's programme online and paused it on the picture of the “spaceship”. Then she opened her own illustration from the children's book, which was saved on her phone.

They put the two images next to each other.

It was the same picture. The same alley. The same red machine. The same angle, the same shadows, the same small window in the door. Grumov had made it darker and added silver light around it, so that it looked mysterious. But it was clearly Olga's work.

“Where's the cat?” asked Alex. “In your version, the cat is behind the machine.”

Olga pointed at the screen. In her original illustration, a tiny ginger cat was sitting behind the machine, with its tail curled around its paws. In Grumov's picture, the same place was covered in black shadow.

“He's painted over it,” said Lucy. She leaned closer to the screen, frowning like a professional. “But he hasn't been very careful. Look — the shadow is darker there than everywhere else. If we had the original image file from his studio, we could take away his layer of shadow. The cat would still be underneath.”

“And if the cat is underneath,” said Olga, “it proves that his ‘spaceship photo’ is a drawing from a children's book. And if that is a lie…”

“Then everything he said might be a lie,” said Kennet.

They looked at each other. For the first time in three days, everybody was smiling.

Then Olga looked at Lucy.

“You've been really helpful,” she said. It sounded a little stiff. “Thank you.”

Lucy smiled — a warm, slightly shy smile.

“I've been wanting to tell you something since we met,” she said. “Your illustrations are wonderful. I bought your children's book in a shop on our first day. I've been carrying it in my bag all week.” She took the book out. The corners were worn. “I'm such a fan. I didn't know how to say it.”

Olga looked at the book. Then at Lucy.

Behind them, Lyuba the giraffe lowered her long neck and gently licked Olga's hair, as if she understood something about jealousy that people take years to learn.

### Present Perfect Continuous

Present Perfect Continuous (have / has been + -ing) описывает действие, которое началось в прошлом и продолжается до сих пор или только что закончилось. Часто используется с for и since: He's been working at this zoo for forty-one years. He's been following us since the penguins. Поставьте глаголы в Present Perfect Continuous.

1. The zookeeper _______ (stare) at Alex for some time.

2. Everybody _______ (look) at Alex's ears for three days.

3. The zookeeper _______ (work) here for forty-one years.

4. Lyuba _______ (wait) for Gleb since last summer.

5. Lucy _______ (carry) the book in her bag all week.

6. How long _______ they _______ (hide)?
