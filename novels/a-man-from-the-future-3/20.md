### Moscow · Friday · 21:11

Down on Level −3, the Moodvac 3000 was shaking like a washing machine full of bricks.

Kennet, who had been locked inside the back of a grey truck, had spent the last ten minutes calmly bending the lock with his fingers. He might have been able to do it faster, but he didn't want to damage the truck. Kennet was a polite man, even when he was escaping.

When he finally opened the door, he saw the machine. The dials were all in the red. The copper pipes were glowing. Steam was coming out of the joints. And on the control panel, a small switch — the one Alex had turned — was flashing: *REVERSE. REVERSE. REVERSE.*

Kennet didn't know much about machines. But he knew a lot about pressure. You can't keep something trapped forever. Sooner or later, it finds a way out.

He walked to the big wheel on the side of the tank — the main valve — put both enormous hands on it, and turned.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then every bottle on every shelf opened at the same time.

***

People in Moscow still argue about what they saw that night. Some say it looked like snow falling upwards. Some say it looked like a thousand fireflies. A taxi driver on the Garden Ring said it was like the city was breathing out after holding its breath for a week.

From the top of the Ostankino Tower, a huge silver cloud rose into the night sky. It spread out over the city in every direction — over the river, over the parks, over the metro stations, over the markets and the rooftops and the windows of millions of flats. And everywhere it went, it came down softly, like warm rain, and found its way home.

The bride from Gorky Park, who was washing dishes in her kitchen, suddenly laughed at nothing. The old man in Sokolniki remembered his chess victory and smiled. The little girl on Tverskaya, already in bed, giggled in her sleep, and in the next room her grandmother giggled too, without knowing why.

On every street, people stopped and looked up. Neighbours who had reported each other to the police looked at each other and felt silly. Office workers took off their foil hats. Somebody's cat, which had been reported as an alien three times that week, stretched in the window and was given an extra fish.

In Danilovsky Market, the walnut seller started singing. In the zoo, Lyuba the giraffe made a happy noise that nobody had ever heard before. On the Circle Line, the trains were full of people laughing at things that weren't even funny.

***

In Studio 5, the silver cloud came up through the floor like mist from a lake. The audience was laughing so hard that nobody noticed it at first.

Then it reached Grumov.

He was standing in the middle of the stage, alone, with his arms around himself. He had been shouting at the cameras to stop filming. Nobody had listened. And then the silver mist touched him — not a single stolen bottle this time, not thirty seconds of somebody else's happiness, but the whole city's laughter, coming home, passing through him on the way.

Grumov's face changed.

It started at the corners of his mouth. Then his moustache moved — properly this time, up instead of sideways. Then his shoulders shook. Then a sound came out of him: rusty, strange, like an old door opening after twenty years.

Doctor Gennady Grumov was laughing.

He laughed so hard that he had to sit down on the floor of his own studio. Tears ran down his face. He tried to stop, and couldn't. And the audience, seeing the man who had never smiled sitting on the floor and laughing like a child, laughed with him — not *at* him, but *with* him.

Alex got up from the chair under the spotlight. He walked over to Grumov and sat down on the floor next to him.

“You should have tried this twenty years ago,” said Alex gently.

“I should have,” gasped Grumov, wiping his eyes. “I might have been a much nicer person. I must have been so… so *boring*.”

“You could have just asked people to laugh with you,” said Alex. “Instead of taking it.”

Grumov looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed again — smaller this time, but real.

“Nobody laughed at my joke,” he said. “Twenty years ago. It was about a weatherman who forecasts rain every day.”

“That's not a joke,” said Olga, who had come down from the third row and was sitting on the other side of him. “That's your biography.”

Grumov stared at her. Then he laughed so hard that he fell over.

At that moment, the studio doors opened, and Sergeant Popov came in with four police officers. None of them were wearing foil hats any more. Popov looked at the silver mist, the laughing audience, the TV presenter lying on the floor in tears, and the alien sitting next to him holding his hand.

“Right,” he said slowly. “Would somebody like to tell me what's going on here?”

Behind him, Kennet appeared in the doorway, covered in dust. Lucy came running from the control room, her dark wig half off and her pale hair falling out from underneath.

Lucy didn't stop running. She ran straight past Popov and threw her arms around Kennet's neck. Kennet, who could carry an easel up any staircase in Europe, stood completely still, as if he had forgotten how to move.

“You should have said something six years ago,” said Lucy into his shoulder.

“You might have said no,” said Kennet.

“I would never have said no, you enormous idiot.” And she kissed him.

The audience, which by now would have cheered for anything, gave them a standing ovation.

### Модальные глаголы в прошлом

Should have + третья форма — «следовало бы» (сожаление, упрёк): You should have tried this. Could have — «мог бы» (была возможность): You could have asked. Might have — «возможно, было бы»: I might have been nicer. Must have — «наверняка было» (уверенный вывод о прошлом): I must have been so boring. Заполните пропуски: should have, could have, might have или must have.
