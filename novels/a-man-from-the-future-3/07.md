### Ostankino · Basement Level −3

Far below the television centre at Ostankino, under three floors of concrete, there was a room that did not appear on any map.

It was large, cold, and very clean. In the middle of it stood a machine the size of a small bus. It was made of copper pipes, glass tubes, and dials, and it hummed like a giant fridge. Along one wall, on metal shelves, stood thousands of small glass bottles. Each bottle was filled with something soft, silvery, and gently glowing. Each one had a handwritten label.

*Child laughing at pigeons. Tretyakov Gallery. 14:20.*

*Two old friends meeting by accident. Arbat. 11:05.*

*Grandfather's joke at dinner (third time telling it). Sokolniki. 19:47.*

In front of the machine, on a tall chair, sat Doctor Gennady Grumov. He was not wearing his television suit. He was wearing a grey dressing gown and slippers with rabbits on them. He was holding one of the bottles up to the light.

“Beautiful,” he whispered. “Absolutely beautiful.”

His assistant, a nervous young man called Tolik, was standing beside him with a clipboard.

“Sir,” said Tolik. “The trucks have brought in four hundred and twelve bottles today. That's a new record. The city must be very distracted.”

“Of course it's distracted.” Grumov put the bottle down carefully. “People who are afraid of aliens don't notice that their good mood is disappearing. They think they're grumpy because of the alien. They can't imagine that the real reason is driving past them with a vacuum on its roof.”

He stood up and walked along the shelves, touching the bottles gently, like a collector touching rare stamps.

“Do you know what this machine is, Tolik?”

“The Moodvac 3000, sir.”

“It's justice.” Grumov's voice became quiet and strange. “Twenty years ago, I stood on a stage in a small club on Pushkin Street. I told my best joke. My very best. It took me a year to write. And do you know what happened?”

“Nobody laughed, sir?”

“*Nobody laughed.* One man coughed. One woman checked her watch. And somebody at the back said, ‘Is he finished?’” He turned around. “That night, I understood something. Laughter is not given equally. Some people have too much. They laugh at pigeons. They laugh at grandfathers. They laugh at *nothing*. And some of us… have none.”

He picked up a bottle and opened it. A small silvery cloud floated out. Grumov breathed it in deeply, closing his eyes.

For a moment, his face changed. His mouth moved. It almost — almost — became a smile.

Then the moment passed, and his face was grey again.

“It never lasts,” he said bitterly. “Somebody else's happiness only lasts thirty seconds. So I need more. Much more. All of it.”

Tolik looked at his clipboard nervously.

“Sir… about the alien. Alex. The police haven't found him. And he might not be an alien at all. He can't be from another planet — he eats borscht.”

“Of course he isn't an alien,” said Grumov. “He must be the most human person in this city. That's why he's perfect. People will believe anything about a man who can fix every computer. The more they look for him, the less they look at my trucks.” He smiled — or rather, his moustache moved. “He's my distraction, Tolik. And while all of Moscow is chasing him, I will collect every laugh in the city. By the end of the month, I'll have the biggest collection of happiness in history.”

“And then, sir?”

Grumov looked at the bottles, glowing softly on the shelves.

“And then, Tolik, I will finally be the funniest man in Moscow. Because I'll be the *only* man in Moscow who can laugh.”

***

At the same moment, on the other side of the city, Kennet was standing on a bridge with Lucy's binoculars, watching a grey truck turn into a side road near the Ostankino tower.

“That's the third one,” he said into his phone. “They all go to the same place. The television centre.”

On the other end, Olga was silent for a moment.

“The television centre,” she repeated slowly. “Where *The Hour of Truth* is filmed.”

“That might be a coincidence,” said Kennet.

“It can't be a coincidence,” said Olga. “The man who tells Moscow to look for aliens and the trucks that steal Moscow's good mood are in the same building. That must mean something.”

“What does it mean?”

Olga looked at Alex, who was sitting on the floor of the gallery café, wearing Lucy's scarf and eating a sad sandwich.

“It means,” she said, “that somebody wants everyone to look at my husband. So they don't look at anything else.”

### 2 Грамматика

### Модальные глаголы предположения

Must = «наверняка, должно быть» (мы уверены, что это правда). Might / may / could = «возможно». Can't = «не может быть» (мы уверены, что это неправда). The city must be very distracted. He might not be an alien. He can't be from another planet. Выберите правильный вариант.

1. Four hundred bottles in one day! The trucks (must / can't) be working very hard.

2. Alex eats borscht and sings badly. He (can't / must) be an alien.

3. All the trucks go to Ostankino. That (might / can't) be a coincidence, but probably not.

4. Grumov never smiles. He (must / might) be very unhappy.

5. I don't know where Tolik is. He (might / must) be in the basement, or he (might / can't) be at home.

6. That truck has a vacuum on its roof. It (can't / must) be an ordinary cleaning truck.
