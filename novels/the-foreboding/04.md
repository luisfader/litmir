# Chapter 4: The Railway Hospital

I spent the evening on the roof of a burned-out post office, watching the hospital through a pair of binoculars with one cracked lens.

It was not an inspiring view.

The railway hospital had been built a century earlier for injured workers from the depot, and it still had the stubborn, square dignity of buildings that were never meant to be beautiful. Three floors of dark brick. Tall windows, most of them boarded. A courtyard at the front, where ambulances had once parked and where two armored vehicles now stood with their engines cold and their gun turrets covered with canvas.

The flag with the white arrow hung from a pole on the roof. In the evening wind it kept folding over itself, as though even the flag wasn't entirely convinced.

I counted the guards.

Four at the gate. Two on the roof. One man walking slowly around the courtyard with a rifle on his shoulder and a cigarette in his mouth, stopping every few minutes to cough. Every hour, a pickup arrived from the direction of the city, unloaded something, and left again.

At first I thought the hospital had become a warehouse.

Then I saw the stretchers.

The pickup that arrived at nine o'clock brought a man who was screaming. Two crewmen carried him through the side door on a door that had been taken off its hinges and was being used as a stretcher. The one at the back was laughing at something. The man on the door kept screaming until the side door closed behind them.

So it was still a hospital.

That meant there was still a doctor.

***

I came down from the roof at midnight and walked around the block twice, keeping to the shadows of the ruined houses.

The railway lines ran behind the hospital. Beyond them stood the old depot: a long hall with a curved roof, half its glass gone, and a row of rusting carriages that nobody had moved in four years. A spur line passed directly beneath the hospital's rear wall, where a loading platform had once received supplies by train.

There was no guard on the platform.

There was a reason for that. The platform was overgrown with nettles taller than I was, and the steel door leading into the building had been welded shut.

I touched the weld with my finger.

It was a bad weld. Whoever had done it had been in a hurry, or had never learned properly, or both. The bead was uneven and porous, and along one edge it hadn't penetrated the metal at all.

I had a small hacksaw in my tool kit, a crowbar, and absolutely no desire to use either of them in the dark beside a building full of armed men.

I sat in the nettles and considered my options.

I could go back to the valley and tell Robert that Dr. Irina Morozova was being held by the Clear Path, that the hospital was guarded, and that I hadn't wanted to die. He would understand. He would probably even say I had done the right thing.

Then he would get on his motorcycle and come here himself.

I had seen him in the market. I knew exactly what he would do.

I took out the hacksaw.

***

It took me an hour and forty minutes.

I worked slowly, stopping whenever the guard in the courtyard coughed, because I had decided that a man who coughed that much was probably close enough to hear a saw. When the weld finally gave way, the door opened inward with a groan that sounded, in the silence, like a dying animal.

I froze.

Nothing happened.

I slipped inside and pulled the door closed behind me.

The corridor beyond smelled of bleach, rust, and something sweet and unpleasant that I didn't want to identify. A single bulb burned at the far end. Somewhere above me, a generator was running. I could feel its vibration in the floor and hear, beneath it, the irregular knock of a worn crankshaft bearing.

I made a note of that. It seemed like the sort of thing that might become useful.

I climbed a service staircase to the first floor and stopped at the door.

Through its small window I saw a long ward. Twenty beds, perhaps more. Most were occupied by men in the remains of Clear Path uniforms — bandaged arms, splinted legs, one man with his whole head wrapped in gauze. Two crewmen sat by the entrance playing cards on an upturned crate. Their rifles leaned against the wall.

Nobody was looking at the staircase door.

I went up another floor.

The second floor was different.

It was quieter, for one thing. And the beds were smaller.

I counted seven of them, arranged along the walls of a room that had once been painted yellow. Someone had hung drawings over the beds — houses, suns, a dog with six legs, a giraffe that was mostly neck. In each bed there was a child.

They were asleep, or trying to be. One boy was coughing, a deep, wet cough that made his whole body shake. A girl near the window was talking quietly to herself, her face flushed with fever.

Between the beds, bent over the coughing boy, stood a woman in a white coat that was no longer white.

She was in her middle fifties, thin, with grey hair cut very short and reading glasses pushed up on her forehead. She was holding a stethoscope to the boy's chest and frowning at the ceiling, as though the ceiling had personally disappointed her.

I opened the door.

She didn't turn around.

"If you've come for more morphine," she said, "there isn't any. You took the last of it yesterday."

"I haven't come for morphine."

"Then you've come for my bandages, my alcohol, my scissors or my patience. You can have the bandages. The rest is unavailable."

"I've come for you."

That made her turn.

She looked at me for a long time. She looked at my clothes, at my hands, at the tool bag on my shoulder. Then she looked at my face, and I had the uncomfortable impression that she was diagnosing me.

"You're not one of them."

"No."

"How did you get in?"

"Through the loading platform."

"It's welded shut."

"It was welded badly."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the place where a smile might once have lived.

"Dr. Irina Morozova?"

"That depends on who's asking."

"My name is Vasily. Vasya. A man called Robert sent me. He said you treated a friend of his once, before the Devastation."

She went back to the boy and listened to his chest again.

"I've treated a great many people's friends."

"He's from Argentina. He has an accent."

"Lots of people have accents."

"He rides an old motorcycle and he asks questions that make armed men uncomfortable."

She took the stethoscope out of her ears.

"Ah," she said. "That Robert."

***

She made me sit on an empty crate in the corner of the children's ward while she finished her round.

She checked every child. She touched foreheads, counted pulses, adjusted blankets, and gave the girl by the window half a tablet crushed into a spoonful of water. She did all of this without hurrying, as though there were no armed men downstairs and no stranger with a hacksaw sitting in the corner.

Then she came and sat on the bed opposite me.

She took a small tin from her pocket, removed something dry and green, rolled it into a thin cigarette, and lit it with a match.

The smell of mint filled the room.

"It isn't tobacco," she said, noticing my expression. "Tobacco is for people who expect to live long enough to die of it."

"I see."

"Tell me why Robert sent you."

I told her.

I told her about the valley, the river, the quarry, the generator and the lamp. I told her about the three rules written in Robert's notebook. I told her that he wanted to build a fortress, that he didn't want to be its king, and that he believed a settlement needed a clinic before it had a hundred residents.

She listened without interrupting.

When I finished, she smoked in silence for almost a minute.

"Does he have a hundred residents?"

"He has two."

"Two."

"Including me."

"So he has one, plus a burglar."

"I prefer to think of myself as an engineer."

She looked at the hacksaw sticking out of my bag.

"Of course you do."

I took Robert's envelope from my jacket and handed her the note with her name on it. She unfolded it, put on her glasses, and read it twice. I didn't know what Robert had written. Whatever it was, it made her take the glasses off again and rub her eyes.

"He remembers my name," she said. "Twelve years. I spoke to him for perhaps twenty minutes in a corridor in Valencia, while his friend was in surgery. And he remembers my name."

"He seems to remember everything except where he puts his tools."

"That sounds like him."

She folded the note and put it in the pocket of her coat.

"I can't go," she said.

I had expected that.

"Because of them?" I nodded toward the floor.

"Because of them." She nodded toward the beds.

***

Their names were Lev, Polina, the twins Oleg and Olya, little Misha, Tamara, and the girl by the window, whose name was Sonya.

They had come to the hospital over the past year. Some had been brought by their mothers, who had later died. Some had been found in the streets. Misha had been carried in by a Clear Path crewman who had discovered him in the basement of a building he was looting, and who had left him at the hospital door without a word.

"So they're not all monsters," I said.

"Nobody is all monster. That's the problem. If they were all monsters, it would be very easy to decide what to do about them."

When the Clear Path took the hospital last winter, they had offered Irina an arrangement.

She would treat their wounded. In exchange, they would allow her to keep the children's ward, give her a share of the medicine they stole, and refrain from shooting her.

"Generous," I said.

"They thought so."

"And if you leave?"

"Then the children stay here without a doctor. And without anybody to pretend that they are useful."

I didn't understand that at first.

Then I did.

"They'd sell them," I said.

"The Clear Path doesn't sell people. It allows people to pay their debts through work." Her voice was perfectly flat. "Lev is eleven. He can already carry a full crate of ammunition."

I looked at the boy with the cough. He had stopped coughing for the moment. He was asleep with one arm hanging over the edge of the bed, his fingers almost touching the floor.

"Then they all come," I said.

She stared at me.

"All of them?"

"Robert said a settlement needs a clinic. A clinic needs patients."

"Seven sick children, through a welded door, past twenty armed men, across a hundred and fifty kilometers of road. On what? Your bicycle?"

"Motorcycle."

"Your motorcycle."

"It's a very fine secondhand motorcycle."

She closed her eyes.

"You are as mad as he is."

"I'm beginning to suspect that's why he chose me."

***

We talked until four in the morning.

At least, I talked. Irina mostly asked questions, each one sharper than the last, and dismantled every plan I proposed with the efficiency of a surgeon removing an appendix.

We couldn't go through the front gate. Obviously.

We couldn't go through the loading platform with seven children, two of whom couldn't walk.

We couldn't wait for Robert, because Sonya had pneumonia and the antibiotics had run out, and Irina estimated that she had a week. Possibly less.

"Robert has antibiotics," I said. "Three packets."

"That's useful. Where is Robert?"

"Two days' ride away."

"That is less useful."

I rubbed my face.

Outside the window, the sky over the depot was beginning to turn grey. Beyond the broken glass of the long hall I could see the shapes of the abandoned carriages, and something else: a squat, boxy shape at the end of a siding, with a cab at one end and a long hood at the other.

I stood up and went to the window.

"What is that?"

Irina joined me.

"A shunting engine. It used to move carriages around the depot."

"Does it work?"

"It hasn't moved in four years."

"That isn't what I asked."

She looked at me.

"I'm a doctor, Vasily. I have no idea whether it works."

I studied the engine. A small diesel locomotive, probably built before I was born. Its paint was faded, its windows were dirty, and one of its headlights was broken. The rails beneath it disappeared under weeds, but they were rails, and they ran south out of the depot, past the hospital's rear wall, and into the darkness beyond the city.

South.

Toward the valley.

"The spur line," I said. "Under your loading platform. Where does it go?"

"It joins the main line about a kilometer from here."

"And the main line?"

"South. All the way to the coast, once. Nobody has used it since the bridges went down."

"Which bridges?"

"I don't know. Some of them."

I looked at the locomotive again.

Somewhere deep inside my chest, a small and very foolish part of me began to smile.

"Dr. Morozova," I said, "how long can you keep the guards from coming up to this floor?"

"They never come up to this floor. They're afraid of catching something."

"Excellent."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to need a few days. And a great deal of diesel."

She was quiet for a moment.

"There's a generator in the basement," she said. "It's dying. The crewmen keep asking me if I know anybody who can repair it. As though a doctor would know a mechanic."

I turned to her.

"You do now."

For the first time, she smiled properly.

It changed her whole face. For a moment, she looked ten years younger, and very dangerous.

"I'll tell them I've found someone," she said. "A drifter. Not very bright. Good with his hands."

"That's a little hurtful."

"It's a disguise, Vasily. Try to look stupid."

"I've had a lot of practice," I said.

Below us, in the courtyard, the coughing guard lit another cigarette. The flag with the white arrow hung motionless in the windless dawn.

And somewhere behind the depot, at the end of a forgotten siding, a small diesel engine waited for someone to remember it.
