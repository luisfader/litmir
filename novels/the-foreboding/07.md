# Chapter 7: Raven Dam

Kennet drove the way he did everything else: slowly, calmly, and with a complete indifference to the condition of the road.

The pickup was an old American model, enormous and square, with a cracked windshield and a bench seat held together with tape. Kennet had found it in the parking lot of an abandoned military base two years earlier, with half a tank of diesel and the keys still in the ignition.

"Like it was waiting for me," he said.

"Things don't wait for people."

"Sure they do. You just have to be the one who shows up."

We drove east for most of the morning, then north into the hills. The road climbed through pine forests, past villages where nobody lived anymore and a petrol station where somebody had painted *GOD HAS LEFT* across the roof in letters two meters tall.

"Cheerful," said Kennet.

"Somebody had a lot of paint."

"And a lot of time."

Toward noon, the forest opened, and we saw the reservoir.

It filled the whole valley ahead of us, long and narrow and dark green, with the hills rising steeply on either side. At the far end stood the dam: a curved wall of grey concrete, eighty meters high, with a road along its crest and a squat control building at one end. Below it, in the shadow of the wall, lay a village of perhaps forty houses.

Some of them had lights in their windows.

In the middle of the day.

"Electricity," I said.

Kennet slowed the truck.

"Guess your engineer's still home."

***

We were stopped at the edge of the village by a woman with a pitchfork.

She was perhaps seventy years old, small, with a headscarf tied under her chin and the expression of someone who had been stopping strangers with a pitchfork for some time and had become very good at it.

"What do you want?"

"We're looking for Maksim Orlov," I said.

"Why?"

"A friend of ours would like to talk to him."

"Everybody would like to talk to him. Crews come every month and talk to him. They want his electricity."

"We don't want his electricity," said Kennet.

She looked him up and down. It took a while.

"What do you want, then?"

"His advice," I said.

The woman lowered the pitchfork a few centimeters.

"Advice," she repeated, as though the word belonged to a language she had once spoken and almost forgotten. "Nobody ever comes for advice."

"We're a new kind of visitor."

She thought about this.

"He's at the dam," she said finally. "He's always at the dam. Tell him Granny Zoya sent you, or he won't open the door. And if you hurt him, I'll find you."

"I believe you," said Kennet sincerely.

***

Maksim Orlov opened the door of the control building with a large adjustable wrench in his hand.

He was about forty, short and wiry, with thinning red hair, a beard that hadn't been trimmed since the end of civilization, and eyes so red from lack of sleep that he looked like a man who had been crying for a week. He wore overalls that had once been orange.

"Granny Zoya sent us," I said quickly.

"Granny Zoya sends everybody. She thinks I'm lonely." He looked at Kennet. "Are you from a crew?"

"No."

"Then I don't have time for you. Go away."

He started to close the door.

From somewhere inside the building came a sound I knew very well: the high, thin whine of an electric motor straining against a load it couldn't move.

"That's your spillway gate," I said.

The door stopped.

"How do you know?"

"Because the motor's drawing too much current. The gearbox is probably seized. Or the gate's jammed in its guides."

Maksim looked at me properly for the first time.

"Who are you?"

"A mechanic."

"Can you fix seized gearboxes?"

I sighed.

"Yes."

He opened the door.

***

The problem was worse than a seized gearbox.

The dam had six spillway gates: enormous steel plates that could be raised or lowered to release water from the reservoir when it rose too high. Before the Devastation, they had been operated by electric motors controlled from the building where we now stood. A team of twelve people had maintained them.

Now there was Maksim.

Four of the gates were rusted shut. One had been damaged during the first winter, when ice had formed in its guides. The sixth was the only one that still moved, and its gearbox was failing.

"And the reservoir is rising," said Maksim.

He showed me a gauge on the wall. The needle was in the yellow zone, very close to the red.

"How fast?"

"Spring melt from the mountains. Ten centimeters a day. In two weeks, it will reach the crest."

"And then?"

"Then it goes over the top." He looked out of the window, toward the village in the shadow of the wall. "If it only goes over the top, the village floods. If it goes over the top for long enough, it erodes the foundation on the eastern side, which was never very good to begin with. And then the dam fails."

Kennet looked out at the village.

"How many people down there?"

"A hundred and twelve."

"Do they know?"

"Granny Zoya knows. She told the others I'm working on it." Maksim rubbed his eyes. "I've been working on it for three weeks."

"Alone?"

"Who else? The crews want electricity. They don't want to learn how a dam works."

I went to the window. The village lay peacefully below us. Smoke from chimneys. A dog barking. Children's voices. And above it all, eighty meters of concrete holding back a lake.

"Show me the gearbox," I said.

***

We worked for four days.

I don't remember much of them clearly. I remember the gearbox, which was the size of a small car and full of grease that had turned to something like tar. I remember lying on my back under it in the dark, with Kennet holding a flashlight and Maksim reading from a maintenance manual that was mostly in German.

I remember the sound of the water.

There was a gallery inside the dam, a long concrete tunnel that ran through its body from one end to the other. You could hear the reservoir through the walls — not a roar, but a deep, constant, patient pressure, like the breathing of something very large that was waiting.

On the second day, Kennet went down to the village and came back with eleven men and women and a horse. They carried tools, food, and an enormous pot of soup that Granny Zoya had made and that I can still taste.

On the third day, we freed the second gate. Kennet and four men from the village spent ten hours cutting the rust from its guides with hand chisels, because we didn't have enough fuel to run a compressor.

On the fourth day, at six in the morning, I reassembled the gearbox on the sixth gate and Maksim pressed the button.

The motor whined.

Then it stopped whining and started humming, a deep, steady, healthy sound.

The gate began to rise.

Water poured through the opening beneath it, white and furious, and thundered down the spillway into the river below.

Maksim watched the gauge.

He watched it for an hour, without moving and without speaking. Kennet brought him coffee. He didn't drink it.

At seven o'clock, the needle moved down a millimeter.

At eight, it moved down another.

Maksim sat down on the floor of the control room, put his face in his hands, and fell asleep.

***

He slept for fourteen hours.

When he woke, it was evening. Granny Zoya had covered him with a blanket. He found Kennet and me sitting on the crest of the dam, watching the sunset over the reservoir.

He sat down beside us without a word.

For a while, none of us spoke.

"So," he said eventually. "What does your friend want?"

I told him.

I told him about the valley and the river and the quarry. About the children and the doctor. About the three rules. About the people coming down the road every day with wheelbarrows, and Robert's notebook with its seven drawings of the outer wall.

Maksim listened with the expression of a man reading a technical specification.

"He wants to build a fortress," he said.

"Yes."

"With no engineer."

"That's why he sent us."

"And he wants me to come and live in it."

"He wants you to decide whether you want to."

Maksim looked down at the village. The lights were coming on in the windows, one after another.

"I can't," he said.

"I know."

"The gates will need maintenance every week. The eastern foundation needs monitoring. And if a crew comes and finds the dam without anybody who knows how to run it, they'll break something, or open the wrong gate, or—" He stopped. "A hundred and twelve people, Vasya."

"I know."

"You're not arguing with me."

"Robert told me not to."

Maksim turned and looked at me.

"He told you not to argue?"

"He said, if they can't come, ask what they need. If we can't bring them immediately, perhaps we can help them where they are."

Maksim was silent for a long time.

"What kind of man says that," he said finally, "when he's trying to recruit somebody?"

"The kind who wants people to say no when they mean no," said Kennet.

***

We left two days later.

Maksim didn't come with us. Not yet.

But we had made an agreement, written on a page torn from his German manual and signed by Maksim, Granny Zoya, and me, on behalf of Our Utopia. I signed it nervously, because I had never signed anything on behalf of anybody before.

The village would send two young men to the valley to learn how to maintain machines, under my instruction. I would come back to Raven Dam once a month to check the gates and train them.

In exchange, Maksim would come to the valley for one month at the end of the summer, once the reservoir was safe, to survey the ground, design the water system, and lay out the foundations of the walls.

"Only the foundations," he said. "I build things that hold water. I've never built anything to hold out people."

"Who has?"

"Nobody sane." He hesitated. Then he took a pencil from his overalls and wrote a name on the back of the agreement.

*Luis Herrera.*

"Who's that?"

"An architect. From Valencia. We worked together on a bridge, years ago, before the Devastation. He designed it. I made sure it wouldn't fall down."

"Is he good?"

"He's the best I've ever met." Maksim paused. "He's also the most arrogant, vain, impossible man I've ever met. He'll tell you your fortress is ugly. He'll tell you your engineer is an idiot. He'll redesign everything twice, and then he'll take credit for the parts he didn't design."

"That sounds terrible."

"It is terrible. But his walls will stand for three hundred years."

"Where is he?"

Maksim pointed east, toward the dark line of the hills on the horizon.

"The last time I heard about him, he was living with the Wild."

Kennet and I looked at each other.

"The Wild?"

"The clans in the eastern hills. They say he builds for them. Bridges, towers, fortifications." Maksim shrugged. "Or maybe they're holding him prisoner. With Luis, it's hard to tell the difference."

I folded the agreement and put it in my jacket, next to Robert's list.

Three names had become four.

***

On the way back, Kennet was quiet for a long time.

Then he said, "The Wild."

"You've heard of them?"

"Everybody's heard of them. In Ohio, we'd have called them raiders. Bikers, mostly. Big families, clans. They took the hills after the Devastation and they've been fighting everybody ever since. The Clear Path. Each other. Anybody who comes too close." He drove for a while. "They don't take prisoners."

"Then Luis isn't a prisoner."

"Guess not."

"So he's one of them."

Kennet shifted gears as we started down into the valley.

"Or," he said, "he's somebody who found out that if you build things people need, they'll let you live."

I thought about that.

"Like a mechanic," I said.

"Like a mechanic," Kennet agreed.

Ahead of us, the road wound down through the pines toward the river. When we came out of the forest, I saw the valley spread out below us in the evening light.

There were tents beside the river now. Fifteen, perhaps twenty. Smoke rose from cooking fires. Children were running along the bank. Somebody had started digging a long trench at the foot of the hill, where Robert's notebook said the eastern wall would one day stand.

And on top of the hill, beside the workshop, someone had raised a pole.

There was no flag on it yet.

"They're waiting for us," said Kennet.

"For the engineer," I said. "And we didn't bring one."

"We brought an agreement. And a name." He smiled. "Robert'll like that better."

He was right.

He usually was, about Robert.
