# Chapter 13: Strangers with Instruments

For two months after the Wild left, nothing happened.

I had never experienced anything like it. Not since the Devastation. Not, if I was honest, for some time before it.

Nobody attacked the valley. Nobody came down the road with guns. The fields grew. The walls grew. The children went to Anna Petrovna's school in the farmhouse in the mornings and ran wild along the river in the afternoons, and nobody worried about them, because there were people on the towers who watched the hills, and the hills were quiet.

Sasha said it was the most dangerous period in the history of Our Utopia.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because people are getting comfortable." She tapped her ledger. "Comfortable people waste things. They take two potatoes when they need one. They leave the generator running at night. They stop counting." She looked at me severely. "*You* left the generator running at night."

"Once."

"Twice. Tuesday and Thursday. Twelve liters of diesel."

I had no defense.

***

Zhenya learned to vote.

It turned out to be more difficult than she expected. Not the voting itself — that was simple, a hand in the air or a pebble in a jar. The difficult part was losing.

Her first motion at the general assembly was to build a firing range inside the walls, so that the thirty-one young people who had come with her could keep their skills sharp. She spoke well. She spoke with passion. The assembly voted against her by a large majority, because Anna Petrovna pointed out that the only flat ground inside the walls was where the school was going to be.

Zhenya stood in the middle of the courtyard afterward with her fists clenched.

"They're wrong," she said to Martin.

"Probably."

"Then why do I have to accept it?"

"Because next time, you might be right, and they might be wrong, and you'll want them to accept it."

"And if they're always wrong?"

Martin thought about it.

"Then you persuade them," he said. "That's the hard part."

Zhenya scowled.

"In the hills, we just hit people."

"I know," said Martin. "So did I. It's faster." He paused. "It doesn't last."

She looked at him for a long time. I couldn't tell whether she was angry or impressed. Possibly she couldn't, either.

***

Tolya, Grom's son, stayed too.

Grom never came back for him. Perhaps he was ashamed. Perhaps he thought Tolya would be safer inside the walls than outside them. Or perhaps — and this was what Luis believed — Grom had simply decided that a son who walked with a limp was no longer useful to a man who ruled by strength.

Tolya didn't seem to mind.

He followed Luis everywhere. He carried his drawings, sharpened his pencils, and held one end of the measuring tape while Luis shouted at stonemasons. He was a quiet boy, broad-shouldered like his father, with large careful hands. Within a month he could read Luis's plans better than I could.

"He has the gift," said Luis, watching him correct a mason's angle. "Grom made him a killer. He was born a builder."

"Like you."

Luis looked at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. It was almost shy.

"I was born a show-off, Vasya. I became a builder by accident." He smiled. "But thank you."

***

The musicians came in June.

Kennet saw them first, from the central tower: a single vehicle coming down the road from the west, very slowly. It was an old ice cream van — pink and white, with a faded picture of a smiling cone on its side and a loudspeaker on its roof — and it was pulling a small trailer covered with a tarpaulin.

As it reached the bottom of the hill, the loudspeaker crackled.

Then it began to play music.

I was in the workshop. I heard it through the open door and put down my wrench.

It wasn't a recording. I knew that immediately, because recordings don't hesitate. Somebody was playing a cello, live, through the loudspeaker on the roof of an ice cream van, and the sound was drifting up the hill through the warm afternoon air, low and slow and so sad that I felt it in my chest like a hand.

All over the valley, people stopped what they were doing.

Daria straightened up in her bean field. Sasha came out of the storehouse with her ledger under her arm. In the farmhouse, Anna Petrovna's children rushed to the windows. On the half-finished western wall, the stonemasons put down their tools and stood still, listening.

Then a violin joined the cello.

It came in quickly, playfully, dancing around the slow melody like a bird around a tree. And the sad song became something else — not happy exactly, but alive. Defiant. A song that knew it was sad and had decided to keep going anyway.

By the time the van stopped at the foot of the north ramp, half the valley was standing on the walls.

***

There were two of them.

The cellist climbed down from the driver's seat first. She was about thirty, with blonde hair pulled back in a long ponytail and a dusty traveling coat over a black dress embroidered with red and gold flowers. She was carrying her cello in her arms, the way you carry a sleeping child, and she looked up at the turquoise walls with an expression of complete astonishment.

The violinist came out of the back of the van. She was darker, smaller, quicker, with black hair cut in a bob and a wide red mouth that seemed to be on the edge of laughter at all times. She had her violin tucked under her arm and a bow in her teeth.

She took the bow out of her teeth.

"Well!" she called up to the crowd on the walls. "Is this the place where nobody owns anybody? Because we've been driving for eleven days, and if it isn't, I'm going to be very disappointed!"

Somebody on the walls laughed. Then everybody.

Robert came down the ramp to meet them.

"It is," he said. "Welcome to Our Utopia."

The cellist turned to him.

"Thank you," she said. "My name is Natalia. This is Oxana. We're musicians."

I was standing a few meters behind Robert. I saw his back go rigid.

It was only for a moment. A fraction of a second, the time it takes to miss a step on a staircase. Then he was smiling again, holding out his hand, saying something polite about the music.

But I had seen it.

So had Luis, who was standing beside me.

"Interesting," he murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing." He was watching Robert. Then he looked at the cellist, and his face changed into something else entirely. "Nothing at all."

***

They stayed for supper.

They told us their story around the big fire in the courtyard, while three hundred people sat on the ground and listened.

Natalia had been a cellist in a city orchestra before the Devastation. She had spent the first years afterward in a shelter with fifty other people, playing for them every evening, because, as she said, there was nothing else she knew how to give. When the shelter was abandoned, she had started traveling. Settlement to settlement. Playing for food, for fuel, for a bed.

"And then I had an idea," she said. "What if it wasn't just me? What if there were dozens of us, traveling from place to place? Musicians, actors, storytellers. People who carry nothing dangerous and bring nothing but news and music. A tour." She smiled. "Everybody told me I was mad."

"We know the feeling," said Robert.

"So I started looking for musicians. And in the spring, I found Oxana."

"Where?" asked Martin.

Oxana grinned at him. She had been grinning at him since the moment she sat down, and Martin had been studiously not looking at her, which was producing an interesting effect on his ears.

"Necrologia," she said.

The fire crackled.

Several people exchanged glances. I had heard the name, vaguely, the way you hear about places in fairy tales. A city underground. A shelter built for the very rich, before the Devastation, deep in the mountains to the south.

"It's real?" asked Sasha.

"Oh, it's real." Oxana rolled her eyes. "Twelve levels. Gardens under artificial sunlight. Swimming pools. A concert hall with marble floors and crystal chandeliers. They hired me to play at a banquet for the Directorate." She made a face. "Three hundred people in evening dress, eating oysters grown in tanks, while the people on the surface eat rats. They applauded very politely."

"And you left?"

"I met Natalia in the service corridor after the concert. She was trying to persuade the head of the kitchen to give her a crate of potatoes for the road." Oxana laughed. "I'd never met anybody so stubborn. I thought, either this woman is going to die very quickly, or she's going to do something extraordinary. And I wanted to see which."

"And which was it?" asked Martin.

It was the first thing he had said to her directly.

Oxana turned the full force of her grin on him.

"I'll tell you when I know," she said.

Martin's ears turned completely red.

***

After supper, they played.

They set up in the middle of the courtyard, on two chairs from the farmhouse, with a ring of torches around them. Three hundred people sat on the ground in the dark. The Wild sat together at one side, Zhenya among them with her arms folded. The children sat in front, with Lev and Sonya and the twins in the very first row.

Natalia closed her eyes and drew the bow across the strings.

I am not a musical person. I couldn't tell you what she played that night, or who wrote it, or whether she played it well by the standards of the world before. I only know that within a minute, I was crying, and so was Kennet beside me, and so was Daria two rows ahead, and so — I noticed with astonishment — was Irina, who sat at the back with her mint cigarette unlit between her fingers and tears running steadily down her face.

When the cello finished, Oxana played alone.

She played something fast and wild and joyful, something that made the children jump to their feet and start dancing, and then the Wild started stamping their boots in rhythm, and then Zhenya was on her feet, and the Brookfield people, and Anna Petrovna, and Sasha with her ledger tucked under her arm, and suddenly the whole courtyard was dancing in the torchlight under the turquoise walls.

I looked for Robert.

He was standing at the edge of the light, by the central tower. He wasn't dancing. He was watching Natalia, who was sitting very still with her cello, smiling at the dancers.

He was watching her with an expression I had never seen on his face before.

Grief.

And beside him, Luis was watching her too. But there was no grief in Luis's face at all.

***

Later, when the torches had burned down and most people had gone to bed, I found Robert on the eastern rampart.

He was looking at the hills.

"Are you all right?" I asked.

"Yes."

"Robert."

He was silent for a while.

"My wife's name was Natalia," he said.

I didn't say anything.

"She was an army doctor. The first week, before the Devastation, when everything was falling apart, they called up every medical officer in the reserve. She went. She told me she'd be back in a month." He looked down at his hands on the stone. "I waited in the bunker for two years. And then I came out, and I looked for her. Everywhere. Every hospital. Every camp. Every settlement."

"And?"

"And nothing. Not a name on a list. Not a grave. Nothing."

"I'm sorry."

"It's been four years, Vasya. I've accepted it." He smiled slightly. "I thought I had."

"The cellist—"

"Is a different woman. With the same name. And blonde hair, which my wife never had. And a cello, which my wife couldn't have played if her life depended on it." He shook his head. "It's nothing. A name. It just caught me by surprise."

I looked down into the courtyard. Natalia was packing her cello into its case by the light of the last torch. Luis was helping her. He was saying something that made her laugh.

"Luis likes her," I said.

"I noticed."

"Does that bother you?"

Robert was quiet for a long time.

"It shouldn't," he said finally.

That wasn't the same as no. We both knew it.

***

Natalia and Oxana were supposed to stay for three days.

They stayed for the summer.

Natalia said it was because the van's gearbox needed repairing. That was true; I repaired it myself, and it took me a week, mostly because I kept stopping to listen to her rehearse in the courtyard.

Oxana said it was because she wanted to see whether a place without kings could survive. That was also true.

It was not, I suspected, the whole truth. Because every evening, when Martin came down from the towers after the night watch, Oxana was somehow always sitting on the steps of the guardhouse, tuning her violin.

"She's dangerous," Martin told me.

"Oxana?"

"She asks questions."

"What sort of questions?"

He frowned.

"Whether I've ever been in love," he said. "Whether I'm afraid of anything. Whether I've ever cried at a concert." He shook his head. "Nobody's asked me questions like that in twenty years."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her to mind her own business."

"And?"

Martin looked at me with an expression of genuine bewilderment.

"She laughed," he said. "And then she asked me again."
