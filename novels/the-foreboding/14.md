# Chapter 14: Passions

The trouble began with a fuel run.

Every three weeks, Kennet drove the truck to the trading post at Kurgan crossroads, eighty kilometers north, where the Clear Path depot that Grom had once taken now belonged to nobody in particular, and anybody with something to trade could buy diesel by the canister. He took potatoes, beans, leather from the Wild, and medical salves that Irina made from herbs Daria grew in the greenhouse. He came back with diesel, salt, nails, and news.

In the middle of August, he didn't come back.

On the first evening, nobody worried. Kennet was often late. The truck broke down regularly, and Kennet's response to a breakdown was to sit beside the road, eat a sandwich, and wait for the universe to send him a mechanic.

On the second evening, Sasha came to the workshop.

"He should be back," she said.

"The truck probably broke down again."

"Then he would have walked. He's been gone fifty-one hours. If he'd walked, he'd be here."

"He might have stopped somewhere."

"Kennet doesn't stop anywhere. He does the run. He comes back. He brings me the receipts." She was holding her ledger against her chest, both arms wrapped around it. "He always brings me the receipts."

I looked at her face.

I had known Sasha for a year. I had seen her argue with Daria, count potatoes, refuse Robert three times in a single afternoon, and once, memorably, make Luis apologize for using a page of her ledger to sketch an arch. I had never seen her frightened.

"I'll get Dima," I said.

***

Dima found the message on the radio at four in the morning.

It came on the frequency the Kurgan traders used, a man's voice, young and harsh, repeating the same words every ten minutes.

*Utopia. Utopia. We have your American. If you want him back, send Martin. Alone. To the old customs post on the Kurgan road. You have three days. Utopia. Utopia.*

Dima played the recording for us in the council room. Robert. Irina. Daria. Sasha. Anna Petrovna. Me, because I was there.

And Martin.

He listened to it twice. His face didn't change.

"Kostya," he said.

***

"No," said Robert.

"It's me they want."

"And if you go, they'll kill you."

"Probably."

"And then they'll kill Kennet anyway."

"Probably." Martin leaned against the wall with his arms folded. "Kostya isn't interested in trades. He's interested in me. I left him on the rails with a broken knee and took his doctor and his children. In the Clear Path, that's a debt. And Kostya was always very serious about debts."

"Then we'll negotiate," said Robert. "We'll offer him something else. Diesel. Medicine. Repairs."

"He doesn't want diesel."

"Everybody wants something."

"Kostya wants to watch me die. Slowly." Martin looked at him. "Robert. You've done remarkable things here. I didn't believe any of it would work, and most of it has. But you don't understand men like Kostya. You never have. You think everybody is a reasonable person who's been unlucky. Some people aren't unlucky. Some people are just cruel."

"Then what do you suggest?"

"I go. Not alone. With Zhenya and six of her people. We take them at the customs post at night. We bring Kennet home."

"And Kostya?"

Martin didn't answer.

"No," said Robert again.

"It's not your decision."

"No. It's the council's."

They voted.

Four to two against Martin's plan. Robert, Irina, Daria and Anna Petrovna voted against. Martin voted for.

So did Sasha.

Everybody turned to look at her.

"I'm sorry," she said. Her voice was very steady. "I'm sorry, Robert. You're right about the rules. You're right about almost everything. But they have Kennet. And I've done the arithmetic, and I can't find any way to make the numbers work that doesn't involve somebody going in there with a gun."

Robert looked at her for a long moment.

"The council has decided," he said quietly. "We'll send a message. We'll offer a trade. We'll try."

Martin pushed himself away from the wall.

"Try," he said. "And when it fails, remember that I offered something else."

He walked out.

***

The message went out that morning.

Robert recorded it himself. He offered diesel, two hundred liters. Medicine. A year of free repairs to every vehicle the crew owned. He offered to meet Kostya in person, unarmed, anywhere he chose.

The reply came at noon.

It was the same voice. It didn't mention diesel, or medicine, or repairs.

It said: *The American has two days left. Send Martin.*

Then, a moment later, it played a different sound. A man's voice, very deep and calm, with an accent I had only heard in old American films.

*Hey, Sasha. Tell Eleanor I'm sorry about the corn.*

Then a sound like a blow. Then nothing.

Sasha stood up and walked out of the council room without a word.

***

I found her at midnight in the storehouse.

She was standing in front of the locked cabinet where the community's weapons were kept: six rifles, four pistols, Kostya's shotgun, and a box of ammunition that Martin guarded more carefully than his own life. The key hung around her neck on a string, as it always had, because the stores were her responsibility.

The cabinet was open.

She was loading a pistol.

"Sasha."

She didn't turn around.

"Go to bed, Vasya."

"What are you doing?"

"What does it look like?"

"It looks like you're about to break the second most important decision this council has ever made."

"The council decided to *try*." She snapped the magazine into place. "We tried. It failed. Now I'm doing something else."

"Robert—"

"Robert is a good man." She turned around, and I saw that her eyes were red and absolutely dry. "He's the best man I've ever known. And he's going to let Kennet die because he can't believe that some people don't want what he wants."

"So you're going with Martin."

"Martin's going whether I go or not. He's been waiting at the north gate for an hour with Zhenya and six of the Wild." She tucked the pistol into her coat. "Someone has to count."

"Count what?"

"Everything." Her voice cracked for the first time. "How many of them. How many bullets. How many minutes. How many of ours come back." She swallowed. "I'm good at counting, Vasya. It's the only thing I'm good at."

I looked at her.

At the pistol in her coat. At the open cabinet. At the ledger lying on the shelf behind her, open to a page on which, in her neat handwriting, she had recorded the last fuel run. *Kennet. Kurgan. 14 August. Departed 06:10.*

The line for the return was empty.

"I'm coming with you," I said.

"You're not armed."

"I've never been armed." I picked up Kostya's shotgun from the cabinet, looked at it, and put it back. "I'm still not. But somebody needs to drive the truck back."

"We don't have the truck. Kostya has the truck."

"Exactly," I said.

***

There were ten of us.

Martin. Zhenya and six of the Wild — four young men and two women, all with rifles, all moving in the darkness with a silence that frightened me more than any weapon. Sasha with her pistol. And me, with my tool bag.

We rode two hours north on motorcycles without lights, following the road by starlight. We left the bikes in a ravine a kilometer from the customs post and walked the rest of the way.

The customs post was a concrete building beside the road, with a barrier, a watchtower, and a parking area surrounded by a chain-link fence. Kennet's truck was parked inside the fence. Beside it stood two motorcycles and a battered car.

A fire was burning in an oil drum. Three men sat around it.

There was a light in the building.

Martin lay on his stomach in the long grass at the top of the ridge and studied the post through Kennet's binoculars for twenty minutes without moving.

"Seven," he said finally. "Three at the fire. Two in the tower. Two inside. Kostya's inside." He passed the binoculars to Zhenya. "So is Kennet."

"How do you know?"

"Because Kostya would want to keep him where he can see him."

Zhenya looked through the binoculars. Then she handed them to Sasha.

Sasha looked for a long time.

"There," she said quietly. "In the window. On a chair."

I took the binoculars.

Through the dirty glass of the customs post window, in the yellow light of a single lamp, I could see a large shape slumped in a chair. Its head was down. Its hands were behind its back.

It wasn't moving.

***

I won't describe what happened next in detail.

I didn't see most of it, because Martin had given me one job, and I did it. While the others crept down the ridge toward the fence, I circled around through the ditch beside the road to the back of the building, where a diesel generator was chugging away under a corrugated iron shelter, powering the lamp in the window and the floodlights on the tower.

I opened its fuel valve, waited for the right moment, and closed it.

The generator coughed and died.

Every light at the customs post went out.

Then the shooting began.

It lasted perhaps forty seconds. I crouched behind the generator with my hands over my ears and counted, because I had learned from Sasha that counting was better than thinking. I counted twenty-three shots. I heard men shouting, then screaming, then nothing.

Then Martin's voice, very calm, very close.

"Vasya. Light."

I opened the valve. Pressed the starter. The generator caught.

The lamps came back on.

***

Kennet was alive.

His face was swollen almost beyond recognition. His hands were tied behind the chair with wire that had cut into his wrists. He had two broken ribs and a cracked cheekbone, and he was missing a tooth.

When Sasha cut the wire, he looked up at her with one eye — the other was swollen shut — and smiled with what was left of his mouth.

"Hey," he said. "Did you bring the receipts?"

Sasha made a sound I had never heard a human being make. Something between a laugh and a sob. Then she put her arms around his neck and held on, and didn't let go, and Kennet sat very still in his chair with his bleeding hands hanging at his sides, as though he didn't dare move in case she stopped.

I turned away.

Behind me, in the doorway, Martin was standing over Kostya.

The young man was sitting on the floor with his back against the wall. He was holding his stomach with both hands. His bad knee was stretched out in front of him at an odd angle. He looked up at Martin, and his face was no longer cruel, or angry, or anything except afraid.

"Sergeant," he said.

"Kostya."

"You came."

"You asked me to."

Kostya laughed weakly. Blood ran from the corner of his mouth.

"Are you going to kill me?"

Martin looked down at him for a long time.

I waited for him to say no. I waited for him to bend down, the way Irina would have, and press his hands against the wound, and shout for somebody to bring a bag.

Martin didn't move.

"You shot me," he said quietly. "In front of the children. You took a man who'd done nothing to you and beat him for three days. You'd have killed him, and then you'd have killed me, and then you'd have come back for the doctor and the children, because you think everything in the world is a debt." He shook his head. "Robert would save you. He'd carry you back to the valley and let the doctor sew you up and give you a bed and a vote."

"And you?"

Martin was silent.

Then he turned around and walked out of the building.

Kostya died twenty minutes later, alone, on the floor of the customs post, while the rest of us loaded Kennet into the back of his own truck. Nobody shot him. Nobody helped him either.

I have thought about those twenty minutes many times since.

I still don't know what I should have done.

***

We got back to the valley at dawn.

I drove the truck. Kennet lay in the back on a pile of blankets with Sasha beside him, holding his hand. The Wild rode their motorcycles behind us in a line, quiet, exhausted, their rifles slung across their backs.

Martin rode at the front.

Robert was waiting at the north gate. So was Irina, with her bag. So were half the people in the valley, who had heard the motorcycles leave in the night and had been standing on the walls ever since.

Irina took one look at Kennet and started shouting for a stretcher.

Robert didn't say anything at all.

He looked at Kennet. At Sasha, with blood on her coat. At Zhenya and the Wild. At the open weapons cabinet, which he must have seen hours earlier.

Then he looked at Martin.

"How many?" he asked.

"Seven," said Martin.

"All of them?"

"All of them."

There was a long silence.

"You broke a decision of the council," said Robert.

"Yes."

"You took weapons from the stores without permission. You took people with you who should never have been asked to kill."

"They volunteered."

"You killed seven men."

"Seven men who would have killed Kennet." Martin's voice was hard. "And then, eventually, all of us. I brought him home, Robert. You tried. I brought him home."

Robert didn't answer.

Around us, three hundred people were watching. Brookfield farmers. Children. The Wild. Natalia and Oxana, standing by the ice cream van.

I could see it in their faces. Some of them were horrified. Some of them were relieved. Some of them were looking at Martin the way you look at a man who has just done something terrible and necessary, and wondering whether you would have had the courage to do it yourself.

And some of them were looking at Robert.

As though, for the first time, they weren't sure he was right.

***

That evening, Oxana found Martin on the guardhouse steps.

I was in the workshop, and the door was open, so I heard most of it, although I didn't mean to.

"You didn't save him," she said.

"Kennet?"

"The boy. The one who shot you. Vasya told me."

Martin was quiet for a while.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because I didn't want to."

"That's honest."

"You asked."

Oxana sat down on the step beside him. She had her violin with her, but she didn't play it.

"I don't think you're a bad man," she said.

"You don't know me."

"I know you came here as a prisoner, and now you guard the walls every night. I know you stood in front of a shotgun for children who weren't yours. And I know that tonight you left a boy to die alone on a floor because you were angry, and now you can't sleep." She looked at him. "Bad men sleep very well, Martin. I've played for them in Necrologia. They sleep like babies."

Martin didn't answer for a long time.

Then he said, very quietly, "Play something."

She lifted the violin to her chin.

What she played was very soft and very slow, and it went on for a long time, long after the lights in the valley had gone out one by one, until there was nothing left in the darkness except the music, and the man on the steps beside her, with his head in his hands.

And across the courtyard, in the clinic window, Sasha sat by Kennet's bed and counted his breaths, every one of them, all night long.
