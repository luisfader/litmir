# Chapter 23: Her

I have tried many times to write this chapter properly.

I've started it on the backs of Sasha's ledger pages, on the margins of Luis's old drawings, in a notebook Natalia gave me for my birthday. Every time, I've crossed it out. Because the truth is that I don't know what they said to each other at the gate that morning. Not all of it. I was twenty meters away, on the broken stairs of the central tower, with Kennet and Sasha behind me, and the smoke was still drifting across the courtyard, and some of it they said very quietly.

So I'll tell you what I saw.

And what she told me later, by the river.

And you'll have to forgive me for the gaps.

***

Robert ran across the courtyard.

He shouldn't have been able to. Two months earlier he had been on a table in the clinic with Irina's hands inside him. A week earlier he couldn't walk to the river without stopping three times. But he ran, through the smoke, past the burning storehouse and the ruins of the school, past a crater where the flagpole had been, without his stick, holding his side with one hand.

He stopped at the gate.

She was standing on the other side of it. Between them lay the remains of the two tree trunks that had served as the north gate since the first winter. One of them had been split in half by a shell.

For a long moment, neither of them moved.

She was not as I had imagined her. I don't know what I had imagined. Someone softer, perhaps. Someone from a photograph in a man's wallet. The woman at the gate was thin and straight-backed, with a narrow, serious face and dark eyes, and the grey uniform hung on her as if it belonged to someone else. There were two silver stars on her collar. Her face was perfectly composed.

Then she looked at his jacket.

"You mended it with wire," she said.

It was the first thing she said to him. After four years and four months. I heard it clearly, because she said it in a perfectly ordinary voice, the voice you use to comment on the weather.

Robert looked down at his shoulder. At the tear she had once mended with dental floss in a hotel in Lisbon.

"I couldn't find dental floss," he said.

And then her face broke.

It didn't break all at once. It broke the way a dam breaks — Maksim explained it to me once — with a small crack first, at the weakest point, and then, slowly, everything. She put her hand over her mouth. Her shoulders began to shake.

Robert stepped over the broken gate.

He didn't say anything. He just put his arms around her, and she put her face against his shoulder, against the wire, and they stood like that under the carving with the three rules while the smoke drifted around them, and neither of them said a word for a very long time.

Behind me, Sasha was crying.

So was Kennet.

So, I discovered, was I.

***

She told me afterward that she had rehearsed it every night for five days. What she would say. How she would explain.

She told me she didn't say any of it.

What she said instead, when she finally lifted her face from his shoulder, was, "I have one hour."

***

General Varga had given her one hour.

She told Robert this at the gate, quickly and quietly, with her hands still holding the front of his jacket. The bombardment had been meant to frighten the settlement into opening the gate. When the gate didn't open, and nobody appeared on the walls, and the engineers reported that the shelter entrance in the quarry had collapsed, the general had begun to suspect that the settlement was empty. Or dead.

She had asked him to stop firing.

She had told him that she was the chief medical officer of the column, and that if there were survivors in the ruins, they would be dying now, and that the Directorate had promised no one would be harmed. She had told him that a dead settlement full of rubble was worth nothing to Necrologia. That seeds buried under collapsed rock were worth nothing. That if she walked in alone under a white flag, someone might come out to talk to her, because people on the surface didn't trust soldiers, but they might trust a doctor.

The general had looked at her for a long time. Then he had given her one hour.

"He's a decent man," she said. "For a general. He doesn't want to do this. But he has orders."

"What does Necrologia want?" asked Robert.

"Food."

"Our food?"

"Anyone's." She looked past him, at the ruined courtyard. "The tanks are failing. The oysters, the algae. The hydroponic farms have a blight nobody can identify. In six months, perhaps eight, there won't be enough to eat on the lower levels. The upper levels will be fine, of course. They always are." Her mouth tightened. "Four thousand people, Robert. Workers. Their children."

Robert was silent.

"The Directorate read about your seeds," she said. "Your fields. Three times what you need. They decided to take them."

"And you came with them."

"I came," she said, "because I saw a photograph of a man on a rock beside a river. With a chicken."

Robert closed his eyes.

***

He called me down.

"Vasya. Get Daria."

"She's in the shelter."

"I know. Open the tunnel."

I stared at him.

"Robert. The whole point—"

"I know what the point is." He was still holding her hand. "Get Daria. Tell her I need her. Tell her to bring the potato jars. All of them. And the wheat. The winter varieties, the ones from the north. And tell her—" He hesitated. "Tell her it's a trade. Not a surrender. Not tribute. A trade. And that I'd like her to be the one who decides."

***

I ran to the clinic.

The clinic was a ruin. The fifth shell had taken off its roof and most of its front wall. But the back wall was still standing, untouched, exactly as Luis had built it, with its innocent plaster and its single shelf where Irina had once kept her jars of willow bark.

I pressed the third tile from the left, below the shelf, the way Luis had shown me.

The wall slid open.

Darkness. Cold air from deep in the hill. And, very far away, at the bottom of the long tunnel, a light.

Somebody was coming up.

It was Luis.

He came out of the dark with his lantern, his long black coat grey with limestone dust. He stopped at the top of the tunnel and looked at me, and then at the ruined clinic, and then past me, through the broken wall, at the courtyard.

"How bad?" he asked.

"The school. The storehouse. The clinic. The corner tower. Ten meters of the western wall. The bell."

Luis closed his eyes.

"My western wall," he said quietly. "I finished it in one day."

"I know."

"It was supposed to last three hundred years." He opened his eyes. "Why did they stop?"

I told him.

He listened without moving. When I finished, he didn't say anything for a long time.

"His wife," he said finally.

"Yes."

"In the grey uniform."

"Yes."

Luis looked down at the lantern in his hand. Then he laughed — very softly, without bitterness, almost tenderly.

"Of course," he said. "Of course she came back. He built her a fortress." He shook his head. "And I was standing in front of three hundred people asking a woman to marry me, as though walls were something a man could build for himself."

He turned back toward the tunnel.

"I'll get Daria," he said.

***

Daria came up twenty minutes later, with two wheelbarrows of jars and a face like thunder.

"A trade," she said to Robert at the gate, without even glancing at the woman in grey. "With people who just shelled my fields."

"Yes."

"With the people who want to take everything."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Robert looked at her.

"Because there are four thousand people under a mountain who'll starve in six months," he said. "And because their blight is killing tomatoes and lettuce in tanks under artificial light, and you know more about growing things in difficult conditions than anybody alive. And because no one goes hungry while food can be shared." He paused. "Even them. Especially them. If we make an exception for them, it isn't a rule."

"It's a mood," said Daria. "I know. I was there when you said it to Grom."

She turned and looked at the woman in grey for the first time.

"You're a doctor?"

"Yes."

"Then you understand that a blight isn't cured by stealing someone else's potatoes. It's cured by understanding what's killing yours." Daria folded her arms. "I'll give you seed. Potatoes, the blight-resistant varieties. Winter wheat. Beans. Enough for your first planting. And I'll write you instructions — proper ones, with drawings, for idiots." She paused. "And in the spring, I'll send two of my people to your mountain to look at your tanks. If your Directorate wants to feed four thousand people, it'll need to learn how. Not take."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," said Daria, "your general turns his house-sized machines around and goes home. And your Directorate writes down, on a piece of paper with a seal on it, that Our Utopia is not theirs and never will be. And if any of them ever comes back with guns, there will be no more seeds. Ever." She lifted her chin. "Tell your general that's the price. Tell him it's not negotiable. And tell him that if he wants to argue about it, he can come and argue with me."

The woman in grey looked at her for a long moment.

Then, for the first time, she smiled.

"I think," she said, "he'd rather face the guns."

***

She went back to the column with the seeds.

Kennet drove her. He loaded both wheelbarrows of jars into the back of his truck, very carefully, packed in straw, and drove down the north ramp and along the narrow road between the river and the hill with a white bedsheet flying from the cab. The same bedsheet Sasha had signed out of the stores for the visit to Grom. She had never let anyone throw it away.

Robert stood at the gate and watched them go.

I stood beside him.

"Will it work?" I asked.

"I don't know."

"What if the general says no?"

"Then they'll start shelling again." He didn't take his eyes off the truck. "And we'll go down the tunnel. And we'll wait."

The truck disappeared into the smoke.

We waited.

An hour.

An hour and a half.

Then, very far away, beyond the river, beyond the burned fields, we heard engines. Deep, grinding, enormous engines.

They were getting quieter.

***

She came back once more, that evening.

Alone. On foot. Without the white flag.

The column was already moving. From the broken central tower, I could see the land cruisers on the southern road, crawling back into the forest the way they had come, the trucks behind them, the men in grey. Kennet had come back an hour earlier with a piece of paper in his hand — thick, cream-colored paper, with a seal at the bottom and the signature of General Varga, on behalf of the Directorate of Necrologia.

Sasha had read it three times. Then she had put it in the ledger, on the page after the one with the cigarette packet.

The woman in grey walked up the north ramp and stopped at the gate.

Robert was waiting for her.

By then, the tunnel was open, and people were coming up. Three hundred and fifty of them, blinking in the evening light, coming out through the ruined clinic into the ruined courtyard and standing there, looking at what was left. Children. Farmers. The Wild. Martin and Zhenya, with their rifles slung. Oxana with her violin. Irina with her bag.

And Natalia, with her cello.

She came out of the tunnel and saw them at the gate. Robert and the woman in grey, standing very close, holding each other's hands.

She stopped.

I saw her face. I'll never forget it.

There was no anger in it. No jealousy. Only a kind of quiet, terrible understanding, the expression of someone who has been reading a book and has just turned to the last page and found that the ending is not the one she was hoping for, but is the right one.

She didn't move toward them.

She simply stood there, holding her cello case, at the edge of the courtyard, in the evening light.

***

"Stay," said Robert.

He said it very quietly. I was only a few meters away, helping Lev carry the medical bags out of the tunnel. I heard it.

His wife looked at him for a long time.

"I can't."

"Why?"

"Because there are four thousand people under that mountain," she said, "and I'm the only doctor down there who knows what a blight is, and what hunger does to a child's body, and how to tell the Directorate the truth so that they'll listen." She swallowed. "If I stay, they'll take the seeds and plant them wrong and blame the surface when they fail. And then they'll come back. With more than one column."

"Then I'll come with you."

"No." She touched his face. "No, Robert. You built this. They need you. And I've seen your fortress. I've seen your rules carved over the gate." She smiled. "You'd last a week in Necrologia. You'd stand up in the Winter Garden and ask the Directorate a reasonable question, and they'd have you thrown into the algae vats."

"That sounds like something Irina would say."

"Then Irina is very wise." She looked past him. Into the courtyard. At the edge of it, where a woman with a blonde ponytail was standing very still, holding a cello case. "And so, I think, is she."

Robert didn't turn around.

"She stayed by you," said his wife. "Didn't she? When you were wounded. Your big American told me, in the truck. A cellist. She stayed by your bed for four days."

"Yes."

"And you said my name."

Robert closed his eyes.

"Yes."

"And she answered."

"Yes."

His wife was quiet for a moment. Then she took his hand, the left one, and turned it over, and looked at his fingers.

He wasn't wearing a ring. I had never seen him wear one. I learned later that he had lost it in the first winter, in the bunker, when his hands had grown so thin from hunger that it slipped off in his sleep, and that he had searched for it for three days and never found it.

She took off the thin, scratched ring from her own finger. She held it up so that he could see the inside of the band.

Two words, worn almost smooth.

"*Siempre*," she said softly. "It still means always, Robert. It always will." She closed his fingers over the ring. "It just doesn't mean *here*."

She kissed him once, on the forehead, where the scar from the coup was still pink.

Then she let go of his hand and walked down the ramp.

She didn't look back.

***

Sasha found him on the eastern rampart, at midnight, looking south.

I was there. I was always there, by then. Kennet too. Sasha came up the stairs with her ledger under her arm and stood beside Robert without saying anything for a long time.

"You did the right thing," she said finally. "Letting her go."

"Did I?"

"Some people are needed in more than one place." She looked south, at the dark road where the column had disappeared. "I've counted it. Four thousand people under a mountain. Three hundred and fifty here. A doctor who knows about blight. A man who knows how to make people vote." She shrugged. "The numbers only work if you're both where you are."

Robert didn't answer for a long time.

"You're right about everything, Sasha," he said finally.

"I know," said Sasha. "But I like to be sure."

***

Two weeks later, Our Utopia had a celebration.

It wasn't planned. The courtyard was still a ruin. The school was a heap of stones, the storehouse was a shell, the clinic had no roof, and the bell lay in the crater where the central tower's lower floor had been, cracked straight down the middle. Luis said it couldn't be repaired. Tolya said it could, if they were patient. They were arguing about it every day.

But the fields were untouched. The shells had been aimed at the walls and the buildings, not at the land. The seed vault in the hillside was intact. The shelter under the quarry had held, exactly as Luis had promised. And on the second day after the column left, the assembly had met — pebbles in jars, counted three times — and voted on three things.

To rebuild. Every wall, every tower, every tile. Using Sasha's ledger, with its list of every shell and everything it destroyed, as the plan.

To let Martin, Zhenya and the thirty-four come home. On probation. With no rifles for a year. Martin voted against himself. He was outvoted two hundred and ninety to one.

And to accept the resignation of Luis Herrera from the council, which he had offered on the first morning, standing on the stone platform with his hands in his pockets, without any speech at all.

"I'm an architect," he said. "Not a leader. I'd like to go back to building things."

Nobody argued.

That afternoon, he took Tolya's chisel and climbed a ladder to the lintel above the north gate. He spent two hours up there, in the cold wind, working very carefully. When he climbed down, the fourth line of the carving was gone. Only the three rules remained, deep and clean, as they had always been meant to be.

He didn't tell anybody he was going to do it.

He didn't need to.

So, on the evening of the fourteenth day, somebody lit torches in the ruined courtyard, and somebody else brought out what was left of Granny Zoya's smoked fish, and Daria opened a barrel of something she had been fermenting in the greenhouse since the summer and refused to identify. And then Natalia and Oxana sat down on two chairs in the torchlight, and played.

They played everything.

The sad song from the ice cream van. The dances. Oxana's wild, brilliant music, with Martin sitting on the ground at her feet, very close, not even pretending anymore. The piece Natalia had written, about a man getting up off his knees. And then something new, something neither of them had ever played before, which they had written together in the shelter, forty meters underground, by the light of a single lantern, during the five days when nobody knew whether they would ever see the sky again.

It was called *Our Utopia*.

I won't try to describe it.

When it was over, nobody applauded. Three hundred and fifty people sat in the ruined courtyard in complete silence, under the broken walls and the cold stars, for a very long time.

Then Natalia stood up.

She put her cello down on her chair. She stood in the torchlight, in her black dress with the red and gold flowers, and looked out over the crowd.

"I want to say something," she said.

Everybody turned toward her.

"Two months ago, a man asked me to marry him. Here. In this courtyard. In front of all of you." She looked at Luis, who was sitting with Tolya by the cracked bell. "And I said no. And I said it badly. And I'm sorry for that."

Luis inclined his head. He didn't look away.

"I said no because I didn't know what I felt," said Natalia. "I've had a long time to think about it, since then. Forty meters underground is a very good place for thinking." She smiled slightly. "And I've decided that if I can't tell somebody what I feel in front of all of you — the way he did — then I don't deserve to feel it."

The courtyard was utterly silent.

Natalia turned.

She looked at the two men. One sitting by the bell, in his long black coat, with limestone dust still in his hair. The other sitting on the steps of the ruined clinic, with a stick across his knees and a thin, scratched ring on a string around his neck.

She lifted her hand.

She pointed.

And three hundred and fifty people began to applaud.

***

Who did she choose?

I know, of course. I was there. I was sitting four meters away, between Kennet and Sasha, with Lev on the step below me and Eleanor the chicken asleep in a basket at Kennet's feet.

But I've decided not to tell you.

Not because it doesn't matter. It mattered enormously, to her, and to both of them, and to everybody in the courtyard who had watched the three of them for almost a year. One of the two men was very happy that night. The other was not. But he stood up, and crossed the courtyard, and shook the other's hand in front of everybody, and then he went back to his seat and sat down. And life went on.

I won't tell you because it's her story, not mine. And because Robert taught me, a long time ago, beside a fire, that words become rules, and rules become habits, and we'd better choose them carefully.

And because I'd like you to think about it.

Which one would you have chosen?

***

I still have the foreboding sometimes.

The one I had on the first day, sitting in the dust of a ruined market with a cable on the counter and a rifle pointed at my chest. The feeling that whatever we build, somebody will come over the hill to take it. That the strong take and the weak give, and that's how it's always been.

Martin had it too. He told me so, once, by the river.

I think everybody does.

But I've learned something about it, since then. A foreboding isn't a prophecy. It's a warning. It's the part of you that remembers the Clear Path, and Grom, and the land cruisers in the mist, and won't let you forget them.

And the answer to it isn't walls. Walls are good. Luis's walls are the best walls in the world, even with ten meters missing from the western side.

The answer is the three sentences carved above the gate. And three hundred and fifty people who argue about them every week. And a ledger, and a bucket of pebbles, and somebody who counts them twice.

Robert still goes up to the eastern rampart some nights.

He doesn't look at the hills anymore. He looks south, toward a mountain two hundred kilometers away, where a woman in a grey uniform is teaching four thousand people how to grow potatoes that won't turn black.

And then he comes down again, and goes to bed, and in the morning he gets up and argues with Daria about the beans.

I asked him once whether he regretted it. Letting her go.

He thought about it for a long time.

"No," he said. "She came back. That's what I was waiting for. I just thought it would mean she'd stay." He smiled. "It turns out it meant I could stop waiting."

The bell is still cracked. Tolya and Luis are still arguing about it.

The western wall is half rebuilt. Kennet found more tiles at the Lazurit factory. A different shade, slightly greener, from a batch that was never finished. Luis says it looks absurd.

Magnificently absurd.

And on the flagpole above the central tower, which we raised again on the first day of winter, the turquoise flag with three white stripes is flying in the wind.

One for each rule.

*The End.*
