# Chapter 22: The Storm

Kennet saw it first, as he always did.

It was the eighth day after the journalists left. A cold, clear October morning, with frost on the turquoise tiles and mist lying in the valley like milk in a bowl. Kennet was on the central tower with a cup of coffee and the binoculars, and at seven o'clock he put down the coffee, picked up the binoculars, and looked south for a long time.

Then he rang the bell.

Not the long, slow peal that called the assembly. Short, hard strokes, one after another, the way Martin had taught him during the first winter. The way you ring a bell when there's no time to explain.

I ran up the stairs in my socks again. I was getting used to it.

Kennet handed me the binoculars without a word.

At first I saw nothing. The southern road, winding out of the forest across the fields toward the river. The mist. The trees on the far ridge, red and gold.

Then the mist moved.

Something was coming through it. Something large and grey and low, moving slowly along the road on enormous tracks. Then another behind it. Then another.

I counted six before I stopped.

They were like nothing I had ever seen. Not tanks. Too big for tanks. They were the size of houses, with sloping armored hulls and a single long gun on each, and they moved through the mist with a deep, grinding rumble that I could feel in the stones of the tower beneath my feet.

Behind them came trucks. Dozens of trucks. And behind the trucks, marching in ranks, men in grey.

"Land cruisers," said Kennet quietly. "I saw pictures of something like those, once. Prototypes. Before."

"Whose?"

He pointed at the leading machine. On its flank, painted in white, there was an emblem: a circle, with a mountain inside it, and a star above the mountain.

"Necrologia," said Dima, who had come up behind us with his radio. His face was white. "The Central Power."

***

The message came an hour later.

Dima picked it up on the old military frequency we had never used. A man's voice, calm and formal, speaking slowly and clearly, as if reading from a document.

*To the settlement known as Our Utopia. This is General Varga, commanding the Southern Column of the Central Power, on behalf of the Directorate of Necrologia.*

*The Directorate has resolved to extend its protection to your settlement. You are requested to accept the administration of the Central Power. Your seed collection, surplus harvests and stores will be transferred to Necrologia for the common good. Your residents will be treated with respect and provided with medical care. No one will be harmed.*

*You have until dawn tomorrow to open your gate.*

*If you do not open your gate, I am instructed to open it.*

*General Varga out.*

The radio hissed.

Nobody in the council room said anything for a long time.

Then everybody turned and looked at Luis.

***

He was standing by the window.

He had been standing there since the bell rang. He had not said a word. He was wearing his long black coat, and his hair was untied, and his face was the color of the stone.

"The seed collection," said Daria slowly. "The surplus harvests. The stores." She looked at him. "How do they know about the stores, Luis?"

Luis didn't answer.

"How do they know about the shelter?" said Sasha.

Robert looked up.

"What shelter?"

"The general's message didn't mention it," said Sasha. "But the woman with the tape recorder asked me about it. On the second day. She asked me how many people the chambers under the quarry could hold." She looked at Luis. "I didn't tell her. I didn't know what she was talking about. The council didn't know about the shelter until the spring."

"Luis," said Robert.

Luis turned around.

"I told them," he said.

His voice was very quiet.

"I told them everything. The walls. The seeds. The fields. The shelter. I told them that Robert was a saint who couldn't defend what he'd built, and that a place like this was too valuable to be left defended by votes." He laughed, a horrible little laugh. "I thought they'd print it. I thought people in Necrologia would read about the turquoise fortress and the architect who built it. I thought that if somebody powerful knew about us, they'd want to be our friend. And I wanted..." He stopped.

"What did you want?" asked Irina.

Luis looked at the floor.

"I wanted to be seen," he said. "I wanted somebody to look at what I'd built and know that it was mine." He closed his eyes. "Even if she didn't."

Nobody spoke.

Outside, through the window, we could hear the rumble of the land cruisers, still far off, still coming.

"Well," said Robert finally. "Now they've seen it."

He stood up, with his stick.

"There'll be time to talk about whose fault this is," he said. "If we survive. Right now, there are three hundred people in this valley who need to know what's happening. Ring the bell, Kennet. The long bell. We need an assembly."

***

The assembly lasted forty minutes.

It was the shortest assembly in the history of Our Utopia. Robert stood on the platform at the foot of the central tower with his stick, and told them the truth. All of it. The column. The message. The interview. Luis.

He didn't make a speech. He told them in order, as clearly as he could. Then he said, "We have until dawn. I want to hear what you think."

They told him.

Some wanted to open the gate. The Central Power promised no one would be harmed. The Central Power had doctors, and electricity, and food. Perhaps administration wouldn't be so bad.

Irina told them about the Directorate. She had treated a woman from Necrologia once, a deserter, in the railway hospital. "They promise respect," she said. "They mean ownership."

Some wanted to fight. Climb the walls with rifles and hold them, the way they had held them against Grom.

Kennet told them about the land cruisers. "Those guns can throw a shell ten kilometers," he said. "Our walls are made of limestone and bathroom tiles. Grom's catapults threw a hundred kilos of stone. Those things will throw a hundred kilos of explosive."

Some wanted to run. Leave the valley in the night, scatter into the hills, take the seeds with them.

Daria told them about the seeds. "Four hundred varieties. Some of them in jars of fifty grams. If we scatter, we'll lose half of them in a week. Without the vault, without the fields, without the people who know how to plant them, they're just beans."

And then Luis stood up.

He didn't climb onto the platform. He stood at the bottom of it, in the crowd, in his long black coat, and he didn't look at anybody.

"There's another way," he said.

"What way?" called somebody.

"The shelter." Luis raised his head. "I told them about it. The seven chambers under the quarry. I told them how big it is, how many people it holds, where the water comes from. I drew them a picture." He swallowed. "They'll know where the entrance is. The big one, in the quarry face, behind the timber doors. Their engineers will go straight to it."

"Then it's useless," said Fyodor's wife from the crowd.

"No." Luis's voice was stronger now. "Because I didn't tell them about the other entrance."

There was a silence.

"What other entrance?" asked Robert.

Luis looked at him.

"The one behind the clinic," he said. "The tunnel I showed you two years ago. The one we started that first winter. You remember? You said the council should know when it was ready." He took a breath. "It's been ready since August. I never told the council. I never told anybody. Because I thought..." He stopped again. "I thought it would be useful, one day, to have a door that only I knew about."

Nobody said anything.

"It's four hundred meters long," said Luis. "It goes down through the hill and comes out in the seventh chamber, behind a false wall. From the seventh chamber, you can reach all the others. If we close the quarry doors from the inside and collapse the entrance — I know how, I designed it to collapse — their engineers will find a pile of rock and nothing behind it. They'll think the shelter was never finished. Or that we lied." He looked out over the crowd. "And everybody will be underneath them. Safe. With the seeds. With water and food for two months."

"And then?" said Daria.

"And then they'll take the walls," said Luis. "And the fields. And the stores. And they'll find nothing worth taking, because everything worth taking will be forty meters underground, with you."

"They'll wait," said Kennet. "They'll sit up here and wait for us to come out."

"Perhaps." Luis spread his hands. "But a column like that costs a great deal of fuel to keep in the field. The Directorate wants seeds and harvests. Not an empty fortress in the north. If there's nothing to take, they'll go home."

Robert looked at him for a long time.

"You built a door only you knew about," he said.

"Yes."

"For yourself."

"Yes." Luis held his gaze. "And now I'm giving it to everybody. It's the only thing I have left to give."

***

They voted.

Pebbles in jars, the way Sasha insisted, even with the land cruisers rumbling closer every hour. Two jars. One for opening the gate. One for the shelter.

The shelter jar was so full that Tolya had to bring a second one.

***

The evacuation began at noon.

Luis led it. He stood at the mouth of the tunnel behind the clinic with a lantern and a list, and he sent people down in groups of twenty, with a guide for each group. Old people first. Then mothers with children. Then everybody else.

They carried what they could. Blankets. Food. Water. Tools. Daria went down with the first group of farmers and the four hundred jars of seeds, packed in straw in wheelbarrows, which she refused to let anyone else push.

Irina went down with the clinic. All of it — the jars of salves, the bandages, the willow bark, the surgical instruments in their boiled cloths, the stretcher made from the farmhouse door. Sonya carried the last bag. Lev carried Kostya's shotgun, which he had taken back from the weapons cabinet without asking anybody, and which nobody tried to take away from him.

Anna Petrovna went down with the school. Thirty children, holding hands in a line, singing.

Natalia and Oxana went down with their instruments.

At the mouth of the tunnel, Natalia stopped and looked back at Robert.

He was standing by the clinic door with his stick. He wasn't going down.

"Robert," she said.

"Somebody has to stay," he said. "Somebody has to talk to the general. If nobody's here, he'll start digging. And if he starts digging, he might find more than a pile of rock."

"Then let somebody else talk to him."

"Who?"

She didn't answer.

"It should be me," said Robert gently. "It's my fortress. I started it. I should be the one standing at the gate when they come."

Natalia looked at him for a long moment.

Then she put down her cello case, walked across to him, and kissed him.

It was very quick. Hardly more than a touch. Then she picked up the cello case and went down into the tunnel without looking back.

Robert stood very still by the clinic door.

Behind him, at the mouth of the tunnel, Luis was holding his lantern. He had seen it. His face showed nothing at all.

He turned away and sent the next group down.

***

Martin came at sunset.

We saw him from the walls: a single motorcycle on the north road, coming fast. Then a second behind it. Then thirty more.

Kennet raised his rifle. Robert put a hand on his arm.

"Wait."

The motorcycles stopped at the foot of the north ramp. Martin climbed off the first one. Zhenya climbed off the second. Behind them, the thirty-four who had walked out of the gate in the dawn, two months earlier, sat on their machines and looked up at the walls.

Martin looked up at Robert.

"We heard," he said. "On the radio. The Central Power."

"Yes."

"We came to help."

Robert looked down at him for a long time.

"You were expelled," he said. "By the assembly."

"I know."

"The assembly hasn't changed its mind."

"I know that too." Martin's voice was steady. "I'm not asking to come back. I'm not asking for a vote. I'm asking to stand at the shelter door. Inside. With Zhenya and the others. In case the engineers find a way through." He paused. "I held a line once in the wrong place, Robert. For the wrong reasons. I'd like to hold one in the right place."

Robert looked at Zhenya.

"And you?"

Zhenya lifted her chin.

"My mother is down there," she said. "And Tolya. And twenty people I taught to vote." She paused. "And I still don't like losing. But I'd rather lose a vote than lose them."

Robert was silent for a long time.

Then he turned to Luis, who had come up onto the wall behind us with his lantern.

"Can they get through the tunnel in time?"

Luis looked at the sky. The sun was touching the western ridge.

"If they hurry."

Robert looked back down at Martin.

"Then hurry," he said.

***

By midnight, the valley was empty.

Three hundred and sixteen people had gone down the tunnel behind the clinic. Thirty-four more had gone down after them. The quarry doors had been closed from the inside and the entrance collapsed. I had heard it from the walls: a long, deep rumble, like a mountain clearing its throat, and then silence.

Luis had gone down last. He had stood at the mouth of the tunnel with his lantern for a long moment, looking back at the empty courtyard, at the walls he had built, at the carving above the north gate with his name.

"You should come," he said to Robert.

"No."

"They'll kill you."

"Maybe."

Luis looked at him. Then he did something I didn't expect. He held out his hand.

Robert took it.

"I'm sorry," said Luis.

"I know."

"Not for the interview. Well — for the interview. But not only for that." Luis's voice was very quiet. "For all of it. The Steward. The carving. The ring. For wanting to be the one who built this. When I knew it was never mine."

"You did build it," said Robert. "The walls are yours. Nobody can take that away."

"The walls." Luis laughed softly. "Yes. The walls are mine."

He let go of Robert's hand and went down into the dark.

The tunnel door closed behind him. It looked exactly like the back wall of the clinic.

Four of us were left.

Robert. Kennet. Sasha, who had refused to go down, because, she said, somebody had to count the shells. And me, because somebody had to describe it afterward.

***

The bombardment began at dawn.

I won't try to describe it properly. I don't think I can.

The first shell struck the southern wall at six o'clock, at the corner where Luis had added a tower to cover the dead ground at the bottom of the slope. I was on the central tower with Kennet. I felt it before I heard it: a blow that came up through the stones and into my feet, my knees, my teeth. Then the sound, so loud it wasn't a sound at all but a kind of pressure, a hand slapping the whole world.

When the smoke cleared, the corner tower was gone.

The second shell hit the western wall. The one Luis had finished in a single day. A section ten meters long simply disappeared, and turquoise tiles fell from the sky like rain.

The third hit the storehouse.

The fourth hit the school.

Sasha sat on the floor of the central tower with her back against the wall and her ledger on her knees, and counted. She wrote down every shell. The time. Where it landed. What it destroyed. Her hand was perfectly steady.

"Why are you doing that?" I shouted.

"Because when this is over," she shouted back, "somebody will have to rebuild it. And they'll need to know what was here."

The fifth shell hit the clinic.

I watched it from the tower window, and for a terrible moment I thought of the tunnel door behind the back wall. But the clinic was empty, and the tunnel was four hundred meters long, and Luis had built it to survive anything.

I hoped he had.

The sixth hit the central tower.

Not where we were. Lower. The bell chamber. I heard the bell, the bell that Kennet had found in a church and four men had carried back in the truck, ring once, very loudly, a long, deep, broken note, and then fall.

The whole tower shook.

Dust poured from the ceiling. Kennet grabbed Sasha and dragged her to the doorway, where the walls were thickest. I followed. Robert was already there, on his knees, with his arms over his head.

We crouched together in the doorway while the tower groaned around us.

And Sasha said, "Kennet."

"Yeah?"

She was holding the ledger against her chest. Her face was grey with dust. Her voice was steady.

"I've been in love with you since the day you brought me the receipts from the first fuel run," she said. "The very first one. Two years ago. You'd written them on the back of a cigarette packet because you couldn't find paper, and you'd added them up wrong, and you'd drawn a little chicken in the corner." She swallowed. "I still have it. In the ledger. Page eleven."

Kennet looked at her.

The tower shook again. Something fell from the ceiling and shattered on the floor beside us.

"I know," said Kennet.

Sasha stared at him.

"You *know?*"

"Since the customs post." He smiled with his broken mouth. "You held on for about ten minutes. I figured that wasn't just about the receipts."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

Kennet thought about it.

"I was waiting," he said, "for you to count it."

Sasha made the sound again — the laugh that was also a sob. Then she put her arms around his neck, ledger and all, and held on.

Another shell hit the courtyard below.

And then the shelling stopped.

***

The silence was worse than the noise.

It went on and on. Dust was still falling from the ceiling, very slowly, like snow. Somewhere below us, something was burning. I could smell it. Through the broken window, I could see smoke rising from the courtyard, from the storehouse, from the ruins of the school, so thick that the whole valley had disappeared into it.

Robert got to his feet.

He did it slowly, with his stick, and he went to the window and looked out into the smoke.

"Why did they stop?" said Kennet.

Nobody answered.

Then the smoke moved.

Somebody was walking through it.

A single figure, coming up the north ramp toward the shattered gate. Walking slowly. Not running, not crouching. Walking upright, the way people walk when they have decided not to be afraid.

A woman.

She was wearing a grey uniform. Her dark hair was pinned up at the back of her neck. She was holding something in her hand, something white, that moved in the wind.

A flag.

She stopped at the foot of the gate, beneath the carving with the three rules, and looked up at the central tower.

Robert stood at the window. He didn't move. He didn't speak.

I looked at his face.

I had seen Robert frightened, and angry, and grieving, and in pain. I had seen him laugh until he couldn't stand. I had seen him kneel in the dirt with a sabre wound in his side and get up again.

I had never seen him look like this.

His lips moved. He didn't make a sound. But I knew what he said. I had heard it in the fever, through the clinic window, over and over, all night long.

One word.

A name.

The stick fell out of his hand and clattered on the floor.

And Robert ran.
