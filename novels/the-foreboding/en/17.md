# Chapter 17: Steel

The whole valley came.

They came slowly at first, in ones and twos, called by the bell, stepping out of doorways and tents and the farmhouse with their faces pale and their eyes on the rifles. Then faster. Then all at once. By the time Kennet had wheeled himself out of the clinic in his garden-cart chair, with Sasha walking beside him and not looking at anybody, there were three hundred people standing in a wide ring in the middle of the courtyard.

Inside the ring, there was nothing but bare earth.

And two men.

Luis brought the sabres down from the council room. He carried them across the courtyard on his outstretched arms, like a waiter carrying a tray, and his face was perfectly expressionless.

He stopped between Robert and Martin.

"They're museum pieces," he said. "Nineteenth century. Cavalry pattern. They haven't been sharpened since before your grandparents were born." He looked at each of them in turn. "They'll still cut."

Martin took one without looking at it.

Robert took the other and looked at it for a long time.

"I've never held one of these," he said.

"I know," said Martin.

"Have you?"

"In the army. Ceremonial training." Martin weighed the sabre in his hand. "And afterward. In the south."

"Then this isn't fair."

"No." Martin met his eyes. "Nothing is. That's what I've been trying to tell you."

***

Irina stood at the edge of the ring with her medical bag at her feet.

"I want to say something," she said loudly.

Everybody turned toward her.

"I have eleven people in my clinic who were hurt this morning because some of you can't tell the difference between holding a rifle and being responsible for one." She looked at Zhenya's boys, who looked at the ground. "In a minute, I'll probably have twelve. Maybe thirteen." She picked up the bag. "When this is over, whoever is still standing will carry whoever isn't to my clinic. And then you'll all go home, and you'll be ashamed of yourselves. All of you. Every single person in this courtyard who watched and didn't stop it."

Nobody answered.

Irina folded her arms and stayed where she was.

***

They began.

I had imagined a duel would be like the films. Swift, elegant, ringing steel, men leaping across tables. It wasn't like that at all.

It was ugly.

Martin moved forward slowly, his sabre held low, his feet sliding carefully across the dirt. Robert held his sabre in both hands, like an axe, and backed away. When Martin struck, it was fast and short, a flick of the wrist, and Robert got his blade up just in time. The clash of steel was flat and harsh, not a ring but a clang, like somebody dropping a pipe in the workshop.

Robert staggered backward.

Martin didn't follow. He waited.

"Yield," he said.

"No."

Martin struck again. Higher. Robert blocked again, clumsily, and the force of the blow drove him sideways into the crowd. People scrambled out of the way. Robert caught his balance and turned back into the ring.

"Yield, Robert."

"No."

Martin attacked.

He did it properly this time. Three strokes, high, low, high, so fast I could barely follow them. Robert blocked the first. The second hit his blade at an angle and slid along it. The third caught him on the left forearm.

Robert didn't cry out.

He looked down at his arm. His jacket sleeve had been cut open from wrist to elbow. Beneath it, a long red line was beginning to bleed.

The crowd made a sound — one sound, three hundred people breathing in at once.

At the edge of the ring, Natalia had both hands pressed over her mouth.

"That's first blood," said Martin. His voice was hoarse. "In the army, that's enough. Yield."

Robert switched the sabre to his right hand. His left arm hung at his side, dripping.

"We're not in the army," he said.

***

It went on.

I don't know how long. Minutes. It felt like hours. Martin attacked and Robert blocked, or failed to block, and was driven back, and stumbled, and got up. Martin was faster, stronger, better. He had spent eleven years learning to do this. Robert had spent his life building things.

But Robert didn't stop.

Every time he fell, he got up. Every time Martin told him to yield, he said no. His face was grey and wet with sweat. There was blood on his arm, on his hand, on the hilt of his sabre. He was breathing like a man who had run up a mountain.

And slowly, something strange happened.

Martin began to slow down.

Not because he was tired. Martin was barely breathing hard. He slowed down because every time he struck, he struck a little less hard. Every time Robert fell, he waited a little longer before he attacked again.

He was looking for a way to win without killing him.

And there wasn't one.

"Robert," he said. "Please."

"No."

"I don't want to hurt you."

"Then stop."

"You know I can't."

"Why?" Robert straightened up. He held the sabre out in front of him, the point wavering. "Because you'd lose? Because you'd have to accept the vote?"

"Because if I stop, you'll keep doing it your way, and one day it will get everybody killed."

"Maybe." Robert took a step forward. "And if you win, you'll do it your way, and one day it will get everybody owned. Which is worse, Martin? Tell me. Which is worse?"

Martin didn't answer.

He attacked.

***

It was the last attack, and it was the worst.

I think Martin meant it to end things. A hard blow to knock the sabre out of Robert's hand, and then the flat of the blade against his head, and then it would be over and nobody would die.

Robert didn't let go of the sabre.

He held on, and the force of the blow twisted him around, and Martin's second stroke, meant for his blade, caught him in the side instead, just below the ribs.

Robert dropped to his knees.

For a moment, nobody understood what had happened. Martin stood over him with the sabre in his hand, staring. Robert knelt in the dirt with one hand pressed against his side and blood running between his fingers.

Then Natalia screamed.

Irina was moving before the scream had finished, pushing through the crowd with her bag.

"*Stop!*" she shouted. "It's over! It's over!"

"No," said Robert.

Everybody froze.

He was still on his knees. He hadn't dropped the sabre. Slowly, leaning on it like a walking stick, he got one foot under himself. Then the other.

He stood up.

He stood in the middle of the ring, swaying, with blood soaking through his shirt and running down into the dirt at his feet. He raised the sabre.

"I don't yield," he said.

Martin stared at him.

"You'll die."

"Maybe."

"For what? For a vote? For a rule?"

"For them." Robert didn't take his eyes off Martin. But he lifted his chin slightly toward the ring of faces around them. "Because if I yield, they'll learn that the man with the sharpest blade decides. And they'll never unlearn it. Not in this valley. Not ever." He coughed. "So no. I don't yield. If you want to lead them, you'll have to kill me in front of them. And then they'll know exactly what you are."

The courtyard was utterly silent.

Martin looked at Robert. At the blood. At the sabre wavering in Robert's hand.

Then he looked around the ring.

At the Brookfield farmers who had walked from Pinefield with their wheelbarrows. At the children in the farmhouse windows. At Anna Petrovna with her arm in a sling. At Daria in her grey coat, Kennet in his chair, Sasha standing behind him with her face like stone. At Zhenya, holding Kostya's shotgun, her eyes wide.

At Oxana.

She was standing at the front of the crowd with her violin case in her hand. She wasn't crying. She was simply looking at him, the way she had looked at him on the guardhouse steps.

Martin lowered his sabre.

He looked down at it for a long time, at the blood on the old steel.

Then he opened his hand and let it fall into the dirt.

"I yield," he said.

***

Robert collapsed a second later.

He didn't fall dramatically. He simply folded, the way a tent folds when somebody pulls out the center pole, and Irina caught him before he hit the ground, and then Kennet was out of his wheelchair somehow, on his knees beside them with his broken ribs and his swollen face, pressing both enormous hands over the wound in Robert's side while Irina tore open her bag.

"Stretcher!" Irina shouted. "Now! Somebody get me a door!"

People ran.

I didn't move. I couldn't.

Martin was still standing in the middle of the ring with his hands empty. Zhenya came up to him. She was shaking with fury.

"You yielded."

"Yes."

"You had him. He was finished. One more stroke—"

"One more stroke," said Martin, "and I'd have killed him."

"So?"

He looked at her.

"So I'd have been Grom," he said quietly.

Zhenya opened her mouth. Then she closed it.

She looked at the shotgun in her hands as though she had never seen it before. Then she looked at the ring of faces around her. Three hundred people, and not one of them was looking at her with fear. They were looking at her with something worse.

Pity.

She dropped the shotgun.

It fell into the dirt beside Martin's sabre.

***

They carried Robert to the clinic on the farmhouse door, the same door that had carried Martin's stretcher out to the first vote by the river, almost two years earlier.

Natalia walked beside it, holding his hand.

He was unconscious by the time they reached the clinic steps.

Irina closed the door behind them. Through the window, I saw her bending over the table, and Natalia standing against the wall with both hands over her face, and Kennet in the corner, still on his knees, his hands red to the wrists.

I sat down on the clinic steps.

After a while, somebody sat down beside me.

It was Sasha.

She didn't say anything for a long time. She was holding her ledger against her chest, both arms wrapped around it, the way she had held it the night Kennet didn't come back.

"I gave Martin the key," she said finally.

I didn't answer.

"Last night. After Kennet fell asleep. Martin came to the clinic and said the Clear Path would be back for Kennet. For revenge. That the council would argue and argue while they planned it. That the only way to keep him safe was to make sure we had enough rifles, and people who'd use them." She swallowed. "I believed him. I still believe him. That's the worst part. I gave him the key because I'd do anything to keep Kennet safe, and I didn't stop to count what it would cost everybody else."

"Did you know what he was going to do this morning?"

"No." Her voice cracked. "I thought he'd go to the assembly and argue. I thought he'd ask them for a Defense Council and they'd vote, and he'd lose, and that would be the end of it. I didn't think..." She looked at the clinic door. "I didn't think."

I put my arm around her shoulders.

She leaned against me and cried, silently, without letting go of the ledger.

Through the window, I could see Kennet in the corner. He had turned his head and was looking out at us. At Sasha.

His face was very still.

***

Irina came out after three hours.

She stood on the clinic steps and lit a mint cigarette with hands that were shaking so badly it took her four matches.

"Well?" I said.

"He's alive."

"Will he—"

"I don't know." She blew out the smoke. "The cut went through the muscle and nicked the bowel. I've closed it. If it doesn't get infected, he'll live. If it does..." She shrugged. "We have no antibiotics, Vasily. We used the last of them on Sonya, two years ago. You know that."

"Then what can we do?"

"Wait. Keep him clean. Keep him warm. Hope." She looked at the courtyard, where people were standing in small, silent groups, watching the clinic. "And keep these idiots from killing each other while I'm busy."

She looked at me.

"He'll have a fever tonight. A bad one. He may not know where he is. He may say things."

"What kind of things?"

Irina took a long drag on her cigarette.

"The things men say when they think they're dying," she said. "Mostly names."

***

That evening, the council met without Robert.

It met in the council room, under the empty hooks where the sabres had hung. Irina. Daria. Anna Petrovna, with her arm in a sling. Kennet, in his chair. Sasha, who had offered to resign and been told by Daria, in a voice of iron, to sit down and be quiet.

Martin's chair was empty.

He was in the guardhouse with Zhenya, under guard by four Brookfield men who didn't know how to hold their rifles. He had surrendered the key to the weapons cabinet, the eight new rifles, and himself, without a word.

And Luis.

He wasn't a member of the council. He had never been elected. But he was standing by the window when the others arrived, and nobody asked him to leave.

"We need to decide what happens to them," said Daria. "Martin. Zhenya. The others."

"The assembly decides," said Anna Petrovna. "That's what Robert said. Whoever won, the assembly meets and votes."

"Robert isn't here," said Daria.

"That doesn't change the rules."

"It changes who will explain them." Daria looked around the room. "Who will stand up in front of three hundred frightened people tomorrow and tell them why the man who nearly killed Robert deserves a fair vote? Irina is in the clinic. Kennet can barely speak. Sasha gave him the key." She turned to Anna Petrovna. "You?"

Anna Petrovna hesitated.

"I teach children," she said. "I don't..."

She didn't finish.

The room was silent.

Then Luis turned away from the window.

"I will," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"I'm not a member of this council. I know. I've never been elected. I've never asked to be." He spread his hands. "But I built these walls. I know every stone in them. And I know these people — I've worked beside them every day for two years. They trust me." He paused. "Let me speak to them. Tomorrow. At the concert."

"What concert?" asked Kennet hoarsely.

"Natalia and Oxana want to play," said Luis. "Tomorrow night. In the courtyard. For Robert." He smiled faintly. "They say that after a day like today, people need something to remind them why they're here. I think they're right."

"And you'll speak afterward," said Daria slowly.

"Before. After. Whenever it's useful." Luis looked at each of them. "I'll tell them the truth. That Robert fought for the rules. That Martin broke them. That the assembly must decide what happens now." He paused. "Isn't that what Robert would want?"

Daria looked at him for a long time.

"Yes," she said finally. "That's what Robert would want."

They voted.

It was unanimous.

I was standing by the door. I didn't have a vote; I had never been elected either. But I remembered a night in the secret room, and a man standing very still with his back to us while a woman knelt to bandage another man's head.

And I remembered his face, afterward, in the starlight.

The face of a man drawing plans.

I didn't say anything.

I've told myself many times since that I had no reason to. That he had done nothing wrong. That he had built the walls and saved our lives in the secret room and offered, when nobody else would, to stand up in front of a frightened crowd and explain the rules.

All of that was true.

I still should have said something.
