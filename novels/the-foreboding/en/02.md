# Chapter 2: The Price of Freedom

The man behind the counter looked at me without lowering his rifle.

"Food," he repeated. "Everybody wants food."

"I can pay you back."

"With what?"

It was a reasonable question, and I hated him for asking it. I had no money, although money had become a rather abstract concept. I had no ammunition, no medicine, and nothing worth exchanging except a broken wristwatch that had stopped at 3:17 on a morning I preferred not to remember.

"I can work," I said.

He glanced toward the entrance.

"That's what I wanted to hear."

I should have recognized the expression on his face. It was the satisfaction of a man who had finally heard the right answer to a question he had asked a hundred times.

He lowered the rifle slightly and reached beneath the counter. For a moment I thought he was going to produce a tin of beans. Instead, he took out a length of electrical cable.

"Hands."

"What?"

"Put your hands on the counter."

I backed away.

The shopkeeper sighed. "Don't make this difficult. You'll work for your food. That's how things operate here."

"I meant ordinary work. Cleaning, repairing something, carrying boxes."

"Of course."

Then somebody laughed behind me.

"Look what we've got here."

I recognized the voice before I turned around.

The young man with the shotgun stood in the doorway, accompanied by his enormous friend with the chainsaw. There was a third man I hadn't seen before, older than the others, wearing a military jacket without insignia.

The shotgun was still in the young man's hands.

"You're the drifter," he said. "The one who doesn't understand respect."

I attempted a smile.

"Hello again."

"See? He's learning."

The others laughed.

The older man stepped inside and studied me. He had a narrow face, close-cropped hair, and a large scar across his chin.

"How old are you?"

"Thirty-two."

"Healthy?"

"More or less."

"Can you read?"

"Yes."

"Good. We need somebody who can read the labels in the chemical warehouse. Last idiot mixed two barrels and nearly killed us."

He said this as casually as someone offering employment in an ordinary factory.

"And what would you pay me?"

All three men stared.

The shopkeeper laughed first.

"Pay him? Listen to this!"

The older man approached me.

"You'll get meals. A place to sleep. Protection. Everything you need."

"And when can I leave?"

His expression changed.

"When your debt is settled."

"What debt?"

He gestured toward the counter.

"You came into our territory. You asked for our food. Somebody has to cover the cost."

I looked at the empty shelves.

"What food?"

The young man struck me across the mouth with the stock of his shotgun.

I fell against a shelf. A sharp pain spread through my jaw, and I tasted blood.

"You don't joke about the Crew," he said.

I raised my hands.

"All right. I understand."

I understood perfectly.

Their philosophy of individual freedom had apparently developed one small exception: my freedom.

The older man took the cable from the shopkeeper and motioned for me to stand.

"Let's go."

For a moment I considered resisting. Then I looked at the shotgun, the hunting rifle, and the chainsaw. There are circumstances in which bravery becomes indistinguishable from stupidity, and I had no desire to discover where that boundary lay.

I held out my wrists.

That was when a motorcycle stopped outside.

***

The engine was old. I could tell by the uneven rhythm and the metallic rattle that accompanied its final revolutions. I had heard enough engines before the Devastation to recognize a machine that had been repaired many times.

A man entered the shop.

He appeared to be in his early forties, with dark hair and a little gray at the temples. He wore a black leather jacket, a black shirt, dark jeans, and boots covered with road dust. There was nothing particularly imposing about him. He wasn't as tall as the man with the chainsaw, and he carried no visible rifle.

He looked at me, at the cable, and finally at the three men.

"What's going on?"

"Private business," the older man answered.

"Doesn't look very private. The door's open."

The stranger spoke English with a foreign accent. I couldn't place it. He sounded tired, but there was something irritatingly calm about his voice.

"He's coming with us," the older man said.

"Does he want to?"

Nobody answered.

The stranger turned to me.

"Do you?"

"No."

"Then I suppose that settles it."

The young man with the shotgun burst out laughing.

"Who the hell are you?"

"Robert."

"Robert what?"

"Just Robert is fine."

I couldn't believe what I was hearing. He was speaking to armed men as though they were arguing over a parking space.

The older man stepped forward.

"Listen, Robert. This fellow owes us a debt. We have rules."

"Did he borrow anything?"

"No."

"Steal anything?"

"No."

"Damage anything?"

"He entered our sector and demanded food."

Robert looked around the shop.

"Did you give him any?"

The shopkeeper tightened his grip on the rifle.

"That's not the point."

"Then explain the point."

The older man appeared genuinely offended.

"Every man owns himself. That's the first principle of the Clear Path. Nobody has the right to take what belongs to another man."

Robert considered this.

"Good principle."

The older man looked pleased.

"Exactly."

"So why are you trying to take him?"

The silence that followed was beautiful.

Unfortunately, it didn't last.

"Enough!" shouted the young man, raising the shotgun. "Get out of here before I put a hole in you!"

Robert looked at the weapon.

"You're going to shoot me for asking a question?"

"Try me."

Robert slowly raised his hands.

"All right. No need for that."

He took a step backward and glanced toward the counter.

For one terrible moment I thought he was leaving.

Then he said, "How much for the man?"

The shopkeeper frowned.

"What?"

"You say he owes a debt. Name your price."

The older man smiled.

"Now you're talking sense."

Robert opened the canvas bag hanging from his shoulder. Inside were three sealed packets of antibiotics, several batteries, and a small metal box.

Every man in the room stared at the medicine.

Even I knew how valuable it was. People had killed for far less.

"Two packets," Robert said. "And he walks out with me."

The older man hesitated.

"All three."

"Two."

"Three, or we're keeping him."

Robert looked at the medicine for a long moment.

Then he closed the bag.

"No."

The older man's smile disappeared.

"No?"

"I'm not buying a person. I wanted to see whether you actually believed your own rules."

The shotgun rose again.

"That's enough," the young man said.

Robert sighed.

"Yes. It is."

He moved faster than I expected.

He struck the switch on the electrical distribution box beside the counter. The single lamp illuminating the shop went out, and the room plunged into darkness.

Somebody shouted. A rifle fired, and the sound nearly destroyed my hearing.

Then a hand seized the back of my jacket.

"Move!"

It was Robert.

He dragged me between two shelves toward a narrow doorway at the rear. He had evidently noticed it before entering. I stumbled over a crate, recovered my balance, and followed him into a passage that smelled of damp concrete.

Another shot struck the wall behind us.

We emerged through a metal service door into an alley.

Robert ran toward the motorcycle.

"Get on!"

I didn't argue.

The machine was an old touring motorcycle, scratched and patched, with two leather bags attached to the rear. Robert swung himself into the saddle, kicked the engine into life, and accelerated before I had properly found the footrests.

We shot out of the alley.

Behind us, somebody was yelling.

Then came an engine.

"They're following us!" I shouted.

"I know!"

A battered pickup truck emerged from the side street. Its headlights bounced over the broken asphalt, and I recognized the man with the shotgun leaning out of the passenger window.

He fired.

The shot struck a parked car to our left.

I pressed myself against Robert's back.

"Are they going to kill us?"

"That's usually what shooting means!"

"Do you have a plan?"

"Yes!"

"What is it?"

"Hold on!"

This didn't inspire much confidence.

We accelerated along a ruined boulevard, passed an overturned bus, and turned between two apartment blocks. The pickup followed, its engine roaring.

Ahead stood an old pedestrian bridge crossing a drainage canal. Half its railings were gone, and several concrete slabs had collapsed into the water below.

Robert headed straight for it.

"That's a pedestrian bridge!" I shouted.

"Correct!"

"It's broken!"

"Also correct!"

We reached the bridge at considerable speed.

I closed my eyes.

The motorcycle jumped over a gap in the concrete, landed hard, and continued across the narrow walkway. I felt the rear wheel slip sideways for an instant. Robert corrected it, and we emerged onto the opposite bank.

The pickup stopped at the entrance.

Its driver shouted something I couldn't hear.

Robert didn't look back.

He kept riding until the city lights had disappeared behind us.

***

We stopped beside an abandoned petrol station.

Robert switched off the engine and removed his helmet. I climbed down and immediately sat on the ground because my legs no longer seemed interested in performing their usual duties.

For several minutes neither of us spoke.

Then Robert opened one of his saddlebags.

"Here."

He handed me a tin of beans, a piece of bread wrapped in cloth, and a bottle of water.

I stared at the food.

"What's the price?"

He looked surprised.

"Price?"

"What do you want in exchange?"

"Nothing."

I didn't move.

Robert sat on a broken concrete barrier.

"You're hungry. Eat."

"But you don't know me."

"That's true."

"I might be a thief."

"You might."

"I might even be dangerous."

Robert looked at my trembling hands and swollen lip.

"I'll take my chances."

I opened the tin.

The beans were cold. They had no seasoning, and the bread was hard enough to damage my teeth. It was the best meal I had eaten in months.

Robert waited until I finished.

"What's your name?"

"Vasily. People call me Vasya."

"All right, Vasya. What were you doing in that part of the city?"

"Looking for food."

"Before that."

I hesitated.

"Surviving."

He nodded, as though that were a perfectly respectable occupation.

"What did you do before everything happened?"

"I worked with transport equipment. Engines, electrical systems, a little of everything. Nothing important."

"Nothing important?"

"I repaired things that other people broke."

"Sounds important to me."

I looked at his motorcycle.

"Your engine is misfiring."

He raised an eyebrow.

"Is it?"

"The ignition timing is probably off. And I think one of the fuel lines is partially blocked."

Robert glanced at the machine.

"Can you fix it?"

"Probably."

He smiled.

"Then you've already found something useful to do."

I expected him to tell me that I owed him for the rescue. Instead, he took a folded map from his jacket and spread it across the barrier.

"There's a valley southwest of here," he said. "An old quarry, a river, and some abandoned buildings. I've been there twice. The ground is stable, there's fresh water, and the main road passes several kilometers away."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I'm going to build something there."

"A farm?"

"Partly."

"A military camp?"

"No."

"Then what?"

He looked at me.

"A fortress."

I laughed.

I couldn't help it.

"A fortress? With towers and walls?"

"Eventually."

"Against whom?"

"Against people like the ones we met tonight."

"Those people have guns."

"I noticed."

"And you think walls will stop them?"

"Walls will make them think before coming in. But walls aren't the important part."

"What is?"

"The people inside."

He folded the map.

"I don't want another armed crew. I don't want somebody declaring himself king because he has more ammunition than his neighbors. I want a place where people can work, raise children, grow food, and sleep without wondering who will take everything from them tomorrow."

I studied him.

"Sounds impossible."

"Probably difficult."

"There's a difference?"

"A considerable one."

He told me he had been born in Argentina and had lived in several countries before the Devastation. When the world collapsed, he had lost people, work, and the future he had imagined for himself. He had spent a long time merely moving from one settlement to another, trying to decide whether anything was still worth building.

"And then?" I asked.

"Then I got tired of watching decent people live like fugitives."

I looked toward the dark road.

"Why a fortress? Why not just find an existing settlement?"

"I've tried. Most have someone at the top who believes everybody else owes him obedience. I want a place that belongs to the people who live there."

"Who decides the rules?"

"They do."

"Even if they disagree with you?"

"Especially then."

That answer interested me more than anything else he had said.

For four years I had heard people talk about freedom while holding weapons against somebody else's face.

This was the first man I had met who seemed prepared to give up some of his own power.

"What would you call this fortress?" I asked.

Robert considered the question.

"Our Utopia."

I nearly laughed again.

"That's an ambitious name."

"It'll have to be an ambitious place."

"People will think you're mad."

"Quite a few already do."

He stood and walked toward the motorcycle.

"I'm leaving at dawn. You're welcome to come with me. There's work, but I can't promise comfort or safety."

I looked at the empty petrol station, at the road, and at the distant silhouette of the city.

"What if I say no?"

"Then I'll give you enough food for two days and show you the safest road out of here."

I remained silent for a long time.

"What if I say yes?"

"Then we'll start building."

I looked at the man who had risked his life for a stranger and given away food without demanding anything in return.

"I'm coming," I said.

Robert held out his hand.

"Welcome, Vasya."

I shook it.

I still didn't believe in his fortress.

But I believed that he was going to try.

And, for the moment, that was enough.
