# Chapter 15: A Night Without Comfort

The general assembly met three days after the rescue.

It met in the courtyard, because there was no other space large enough, with three hundred and twelve adults sitting on the ground, on crates, on the low walls of the half-finished school. The council sat on a row of chairs in front of the central tower. Kennet sat with them, in a wheelchair I had built from a garden cart and two bicycle wheels, his face still purple and swollen, Sasha standing behind him with one hand on his shoulder.

Robert spoke first.

He didn't make a speech. He never made speeches. He simply told them what had happened, in order, as clearly as he could. The fuel run. The message. The vote. The trade that had been offered and refused. The weapons cabinet. The customs post. Seven dead men.

"The council made a decision," he said. "Martin broke it. He saved Kennet's life. Both of those things are true. I don't know how to make them fit together. I want to hear what you think."

For a long moment, nobody spoke.

Then a Brookfield farmer stood up. His name was Fyodor. He had walked to the valley from Pinefield with his wife and four children, pushing a wheelbarrow.

"I'll tell you what I think," he said. "I think if it were my son in that chair, I'd want Martin to go and get him. Rules or no rules."

There was a murmur of agreement.

Anna Petrovna stood up.

"And if Martin decides next time that he doesn't like a decision about the school? Or the fields? Or who gets the next bed in the clinic?" She looked around. "What's the point of voting if the person with the guns can ignore the result?"

Another murmur. Different people.

Then Zhenya stood up.

"In the hills," she said, "Grom decided everything. Nobody voted. If you disagreed, he hit you. Here, you vote. That's good. I like it." She looked at Robert. "But when the vote is wrong, people die. And then voting doesn't help them."

"How do you know the vote was wrong?" asked Irina.

"Because Kennet is alive," said Zhenya. "And if we'd done what the vote said, he wouldn't be."

It went on for four hours.

At the end, the assembly voted. Not on punishment, because nobody could agree what the punishment should be. On a single question, proposed by Daria in a voice of iron: *Should the council's decisions be binding on everyone, without exception?*

Two hundred and four voted yes.

A hundred and eight voted no.

Robert looked at the numbers for a long time.

"Thank you," he said.

He didn't look like a man who had won anything.

***

That night I couldn't sleep.

I lay in my bunk in the workshop listening to the generator, thinking about the hundred and eight. A third of the valley. A third of the people Robert had fed, sheltered and protected had decided that rules were good, except when they weren't.

I wasn't sure I disagreed with them.

That was the worst part.

At two in the morning, I gave up and went outside.

The courtyard was empty and silent. The torches had burned down. The turquoise walls were grey in the starlight. On the central tower, the night watch was walking slowly back and forth, a dark shape against the stars.

Somebody was walking across the courtyard toward the north gate.

He was moving quietly, keeping to the shadows of the walls. But I knew that walk. Slow and measured. A man who had decided long ago that hurrying made people stupid.

Martin.

He spoke briefly to the guard at the gate. The guard was one of Zhenya's people. He nodded and opened the small door beside the main gate.

Martin went out.

I stood in the courtyard for a full minute.

Then I followed him.

***

He went down to the river.

I followed at a distance, through the dark fields, crouching every time he paused, feeling ridiculous and terrified in equal measure. He walked along the bank toward the old ford, where the river was shallow enough to cross on foot and where the road from the north met the valley.

There was a light on the far bank.

A lantern, hanging from the branch of a tree. Beneath it, three figures were waiting. Two men with rifles. And a third, older, in a long coat of good grey wool, with a fur hat and polished boots.

Polished boots. In a world where nobody had polished their boots in four years.

On his coat, a small enamel badge. A white arrow in a black circle.

I crouched behind a willow and held my breath.

Martin waded across the ford. The water came up to his knees. He stopped in front of the man in the grey coat and stood with his hands at his sides.

"Arbiter," he said.

"Sergeant Martin." The man's voice was soft and pleasant, the voice of a bank manager. "It's been a long time."

"Not long enough."

"You killed seven of our people."

"Seven of your people kidnapped one of mine."

"Kostya was undisciplined. A disappointment. I won't pretend otherwise." The Arbiter sighed. "But he was ours. You understand how this works, Sergeant. You were one of us for three years. A debt has been created. A debt must be settled."

"How much?"

The Arbiter smiled.

"Diesel. A hundred liters a month. Medicine — the doctor's salves, the ones she makes from herbs. Twenty jars a month. Seed potatoes, in the spring." He paused. "And the young people from the hills. Twelve of them. For a year. We have work at the chemical warehouse."

"No."

"No?"

"The diesel and the medicine. Maybe the seed potatoes. Not people."

The Arbiter tilted his head.

"You've changed, Sergeant."

"Not as much as you'd think."

"Then let me offer something in return. Something you'll appreciate." The Arbiter gestured to one of the riflemen, who stepped forward and opened a long wooden crate on the ground at his feet.

Rifles. New ones, still wrapped in oiled paper. Eight of them. And boxes of ammunition.

"Your Utopia has six rifles and four pistols," said the Arbiter. "Your Robert refuses to buy more. He thinks walls and votes and kindness are enough." He smiled. "You and I know better. The Wild will come back. Or someone else will. And when they do, you'll need more than a hundred people who know how to vote."

Martin looked at the rifles for a long time.

"And in exchange?"

"The diesel and the medicine, every month. Delivered here. To you. Not to the council. To you." The Arbiter closed the crate. "And when the time comes, Sergeant, and your foreigner's dream falls apart — as dreams do — you'll remember who your friends are."

Martin didn't answer.

I waited for him to refuse. I waited for him to turn around, wade back across the river, and walk up the hill to tell Robert.

He bent down and picked up the crate.

***

I ran.

I ran back across the fields, stumbling in the dark, and I didn't stop until I reached the willows near the bottom of the hill. There I crouched, gasping, while Martin came back across the ford with the crate on his shoulder and walked slowly up the path toward the north gate.

He stopped three meters from me.

"You can come out, Vasya."

I stayed where I was.

"I heard you following me at the ford," he said. "You breathe like a locomotive."

I came out.

We stood facing each other in the darkness.

"You took his rifles," I said.

"Yes."

"You promised him diesel. Medicine. Irina's medicine."

"Yes."

"Behind the council's back. Three days after the assembly voted that the council's decisions are binding on everyone."

"Yes." Martin shifted the crate on his shoulder. "Are you going to tell Robert?"

"Of course I'm going to tell Robert."

"Good. Tell him." His voice was perfectly calm. "And then he'll take it to the council, and the council will take it to the assembly, and the assembly will argue for a week. And while they're arguing, the Clear Path will be counting how much diesel we have, and the Wild will be counting how many rifles, and somebody else, somebody we haven't even heard of yet, will be counting how long it takes three hundred people to agree on anything."

"That's the point. That's the whole point."

"No, Vasya." He shook his head slowly. "The point is staying alive long enough to vote again."

He walked past me toward the gate.

Then he stopped.

"You know what I've learned here?" he said, without turning around. "Robert's right about almost everything. He's right that people can share. He's right that they can govern themselves. He's right that a doctor shouldn't refuse a patient and a man shouldn't own another man." He paused. "And he's going to get every single one of us killed, because he can't accept that some decisions have to be made by one person, quickly, in the dark."

"Like you."

"Like me," Martin agreed.

He went through the gate.

***

I didn't go straight to Robert.

I should have. I've told myself that many times since. But it was three in the morning, and Robert's window was dark, and I was standing in the courtyard shaking, and I didn't know what I was going to say.

So I walked.

I walked around the inside of the walls, slowly, the whole two kilometers, the way Maksim had walked the valley before he laid the foundations. I passed the clinic, where a single lamp was burning in the window and Sasha was asleep in a chair beside Kennet's bed. I passed the storehouse, the school, the greenhouses, Daria's fields.

I passed the ice cream van.

There were two people sitting on its steps in the dark.

I stopped before they saw me, in the shadow of the wall.

It was Luis. And Natalia.

She had her cello case beside her. He was sitting one step below her, so that his head was level with her knees, and he was speaking in a low voice that I had never heard him use before. Not the hurricane voice. Not the voice that explained why your arches were wrong.

"...since the first night," he was saying. "When I heard you play from the road. Before I even saw you. I thought: whoever is playing that, I have to meet her, or I'll regret it for the rest of my life."

Natalia didn't answer.

"I've built bridges in six countries," said Luis. "Towers. A concert hall in Lisbon. A hotel in the mountains that was never finished. I've been admired. I've been flattered. I've been loved, I think, by people I didn't deserve." He laughed softly. "And I've never, once, felt what I feel when you play."

"Luis—"

"I'm not asking for anything. I just wanted you to know."

Natalia was quiet for a long time.

"You're a remarkable man," she said finally. "You're brilliant, and generous, and you make me laugh. And these walls you've built..." She looked up at them. "I've traveled for two years. I've never seen anything like them."

"But."

"But I don't know." Her voice was very gentle. "I'm sorry. I don't know what I feel. I don't know whether I'm staying here or leaving in the spring. And..."

She stopped.

"And there's someone else," said Luis.

She didn't deny it.

Luis sat very still on his step.

"Robert," he said.

Natalia looked away. Toward the eastern rampart, where, even at three in the morning, a single figure was standing alone, looking at the dark hills.

"Every night," she said quietly. "Every night he stands there. Looking at nothing. And I don't know why. And I don't know why I care."

Luis didn't answer.

After a while, he stood up. He brushed the dust from his long black coat. He bowed to her, a strange, old-fashioned bow, like something from a play.

"Good night, Natalia," he said.

He walked away across the courtyard, toward the central tower. He walked like a man who had been hit very hard and was determined not to fall down.

He passed within two meters of me without noticing I was there.

His face, in the starlight, was not the face of an architect.

It was the face of a man drawing plans.

***

I found Robert on the eastern rampart at four in the morning.

He didn't seem surprised to see me. Nobody in Our Utopia slept that night, it seemed. Not Martin, not Luis, not Natalia, not Sasha. Not Robert, who had been standing there, according to Natalia, since midnight.

I told him everything.

The ford. The Arbiter. The rifles. The diesel and the medicine, every month. To Martin, not to the council.

Robert listened without interrupting. He didn't look at me. He looked at the hills.

When I finished, he was quiet for a long time.

"Thank you, Vasya," he said finally.

"What are you going to do?"

"Call an assembly. Tomorrow. Today, rather." He looked at the sky, which was turning grey above the eastern ridge. "I'll tell them what you've told me. Martin will tell them his side. And they'll decide."

"Decide what?"

"Whether he stays."

I stared at him.

"You'd expel him?"

"I wouldn't do anything. The assembly would."

"Robert, he's half the reason we're still alive. He held the walls against Grom. He brought Kennet home."

"He also made a secret agreement with the Clear Path, behind the backs of the people he was elected to serve, three days after they voted that nobody is above the rules." Robert's voice was very quiet. "If I let that pass, Vasya, then the rules mean nothing. And if the rules mean nothing, then this isn't Our Utopia. It's Martin's fortress, with a turquoise flag."

"And if they vote for him?"

Robert was silent.

"Then I'll have been wrong," he said. "About a great many things."

***

The bell on the central tower rang at seven o'clock, calling the assembly.

I heard it from the workshop, where I had finally fallen asleep on a pile of sacks at five. I sat up, groggy, and went to the door.

The courtyard was filling with people. Brookfield farmers. The Wild. Children being sent back to the farmhouse by their parents. Natalia and Oxana by the ice cream van. Irina at the clinic door.

Martin was standing at the foot of the central tower.

He wasn't alone.

Zhenya was beside him. And behind them, in a loose semicircle, stood twenty of the Wild. And some of the Brookfield men. Fyodor, the farmer who had said he'd want Martin to go and get his son. And others, perhaps thirty, perhaps forty.

They were all carrying rifles.

New rifles, still smelling of oiled paper.

I counted them.

Eight from the crate. And the six from the stores. And the four pistols.

And Kostya's shotgun, which I had last seen in the weapons cabinet two nights earlier, and which Lev had taken from the hospital platform the night we escaped, and which was now in the hands of Zhenya.

Somebody had opened the cabinet again.

Somebody who had a key.

I looked toward the clinic.

Sasha was standing in the doorway, with the string around her neck.

There was no key on it.
