# Chapter 3: Three Names

I had imagined the future fortress would at least have the beginnings of a wall.

It didn't.

After two days on the road, Robert brought me to a valley surrounded by wooded hills. A shallow river crossed its western side, and an abandoned stone quarry occupied the opposite slope. There were several ruined agricultural buildings, an old workshop with a collapsed roof, and a narrow road that disappeared into the forest.

Robert stopped his motorcycle beside a heap of stones.

"Here we are."

I looked around.

"Where?"

"At the fortress."

I waited for him to laugh.

He didn't.

"Robert, that's a field."

"Good observation."

"There are no walls."

"Not yet."

"No towers."

"Not yet."

"No houses."

He looked toward the ruined farm.

"Technically, there's one."

"With no roof."

"You're very difficult to impress, Vasya."

I climbed off the motorcycle and inspected the terrain. The river was clear, at least to the eye, and the surrounding hills would provide some protection from the wind. The quarry offered stone in enormous quantities. There were also several places where an attacking force could enter the valley without much trouble.

I pointed them out.

Robert listened carefully.

"That's why we need walls," he said.

"And people. You can't build all this alone."

"Exactly."

He opened one of his saddlebags and removed a notebook.

Inside were sketches of buildings, drainage channels, workshops, gardens, and defensive walls. Some drawings were surprisingly detailed. Others consisted of rectangles with question marks beside them.

One page showed a central square surrounded by houses. Another showed a school and a medical building. There were plans for wells, food storage, a kitchen, and an underground shelter.

The outer wall had been drawn several times.

"Why are there so many versions?" I asked.

"Because I haven't decided how to build it."

"You're planning a fortress, and you don't know how to build the walls?"

"That's why I need an engineer."

At least he recognized his limitations.

I turned another page.

There was a drawing of the entrance. Above it, in large letters, Robert had written:

*OUR UTOPIA.*

Below that were three sentences.

*No one owns another person.*

*No one goes hungry while food can be shared.*

*Every adult has a voice in the decisions of the community.*

I read them twice.

"And who's going to enforce these rules?"

"We will."

"With weapons?"

"With agreements first. We may need weapons for defense, but they won't decide who is right."

I thought about the men in the market.

"They'll come here eventually."

"I know."

"They'll want your food, your fuel, your land."

"Probably."

"And what happens when they discover you're building a settlement without a gang leader?"

Robert closed the notebook.

"Then we'll have to be ready."

He wasn't smiling anymore.

***

We spent the first week working.

Robert had already collected some tools and materials in the workshop. Most were useless until we repaired the roof, but there was a generator, several drums of diesel, a welding machine, and enough scrap metal to make a mechanic happy for approximately fifteen minutes.

I started with the generator.

It hadn't run in years. The fuel system was clogged, the wiring had been damaged by rodents, and the starter motor had probably died before the Devastation.

Robert watched me take it apart.

"Can you fix it?"

"Stop asking me that."

"Why?"

"Because every time I say yes, you find another broken machine."

He laughed.

I spent three days cleaning the fuel system, replacing damaged cables, and improvising a repair to the starter. On the fourth day, the generator coughed, shuddered, and started.

Robert came running from the quarry.

"Vasya!"

"Don't touch anything!"

"Is it working?"

"Yes, but don't touch anything!"

The generator continued running.

I connected an old lamp.

Light filled the workshop.

For a few seconds we stood without speaking.

It was only a lamp, suspended from a beam by a piece of wire. Before the Devastation, I wouldn't even have noticed it.

Now it seemed extraordinary.

Robert placed a hand on my shoulder.

"Look at that."

"It's a very old lamp."

"It's our first electric light."

I looked at him.

He was genuinely happy.

Not because he had gained a generator, or because he could order somebody to repair another machine. He was happy because something worked again.

I had forgotten that people could react like that.

That evening, we ate beside the river.

Robert had found potatoes in an abandoned cellar and traded a few batteries for dried fish at a distant settlement. We roasted the potatoes in the fire and discussed the next stage of construction.

He wanted a temporary fence around the workshop and a sheltered place to store food. I wanted to repair the old water pump before we made any further plans.

"You can't build a settlement without a reliable water supply," I told him.

"I agree."

"And you can't build a wall without proper foundations."

"I agree."

"And you definitely can't build a fortress with two people."

Robert nodded.

"I agree with that too."

I frowned.

"Then why do I feel as though we're arguing?"

"Because you're getting used to me."

He took a sip of water.

"Actually, Vasya, I've been thinking about giving you a different job."

I looked toward the workshop.

"If it's another generator, I'm leaving."

"No. Something more important."

He reached into his jacket and took out a folded piece of paper.

There were three names written on it.

***

"These are people I need to find," Robert said.

He handed me the paper.

I unfolded it and read.

**ROBERT'S LIST**

1. **Dr. Irina Morozova** — Physician and emergency surgeon. Last seen at the old railway hospital near North Station.
2. **Maksim Orlov** — Civil engineer and water systems specialist. Last known location: the maintenance compound at Raven Dam.
3. **Daria Sokolova** — Agronomist and former biology teacher. Believed to be protecting a seed collection at the Pinefield Agricultural Research Station.

I looked up.

"Who are they?"

"People who know things we don't."

"Friends of yours?"

"Not exactly."

"What does that mean?"

"I've met Irina. Years ago, before the Devastation. She treated a friend of mine after an accident. I've heard about Maksim from a man who worked at the dam. And Daria sent a message over a radio channel last winter. She was looking for fuel to keep her greenhouses warm."

"So you don't actually know where two of them are."

"I know where to start."

I examined the paper again.

"Why these three?"

Robert pointed to the first name.

"Irina is a doctor. We need somebody who understands medicine, not merely somebody who knows how to put a bandage on a wound. People are going to arrive here sick, injured, exhausted. Some will have children. We need a clinic before we have a hundred residents."

He moved to the second name.

"Maksim worked on bridges, retaining walls, and water systems. If we're going to build a fortress, I want somebody who can tell me whether the walls will still be standing after the first winter."

"And Daria?"

"Food. Seeds. Soil. Crop rotation. She knows how to grow things in difficult conditions."

"We could just plant potatoes."

Robert looked at me with an expression of profound disappointment.

"Vasya, how long do you think people can survive on potatoes alone?"

"Depends how many potatoes."

He shook his head.

"Daria would know how to answer that properly."

I sat down on a crate.

"Suppose I find them. What am I supposed to say?"

"Tell them what we're building."

"They'll laugh at me."

"Possibly."

"They might shoot me."

"That's also possible."

"You're not making this assignment very attractive."

Robert sat opposite me.

"Listen. I can build a shelter by myself. Two of us can repair a few machines and raise a fence. But if we're building a place where people can live, we need people who know how to do things I don't. I don't want to make every decision, either. I want others who'll disagree with me when I'm wrong."

I examined the names.

"How do you know they share your ideas?"

"I don't."

"Then what if they refuse?"

"Thank them for listening and leave."

"Just like that?"

"Just like that."

"What if they ask for something in exchange?"

"We can offer food, shelter, work, and a say in what we're building. But don't promise them money we don't have or safety we can't guarantee."

I remembered the market.

"You don't want me recruiting them the way the Clear Path recruited me."

"Definitely not."

He leaned forward.

"There's something else. Don't think of this as collecting three useful people. Irina may have patients she refuses to abandon. Maksim may have a family. Daria may be protecting people as well as seeds. Ask what they need, and tell me. If we can't bring them immediately, perhaps we can help them where they are."

I looked at the names again.

They no longer seemed like entries on a shopping list.

They were people with histories, fears, and obligations. Every one of them might have good reasons not to trust another stranger promising a better future.

"Why are you giving this job to me?" I asked.

"Because you know how to travel without attracting too much attention."

"Not in the market, apparently."

"You survived four years without a weapon."

"Mostly by hiding."

"That's a skill. You also understand machines, know how to read maps, and ask difficult questions. I need someone who can think for himself."

He paused.

"And you've already seen what happens when people aren't allowed to say no. I don't think you'll forget it."

I didn't answer immediately.

Nobody had spoken about me with that much confidence in years.

"What about transport?" I asked at last. "I'm not walking three hundred kilometers."

Robert smiled.

"I was hoping you'd ask."

***

He led me behind the workshop.

Under a large tarpaulin stood a second motorcycle.

It was smaller than his touring machine, with a scratched dark-green fuel tank, a black seat, and a steel luggage rack. One mirror was missing. The headlight was intact, but the front mudguard had been repaired with two pieces of sheet metal.

I recognized it immediately.

It was the old single-cylinder motorcycle we had found in the workshop's storage shed three days earlier.

"That's the one we repaired," I said.

"Correct."

"You said you wanted to keep it for local transport."

"I changed my mind."

I walked around the machine.

"We haven't tested it properly."

"Then you'd better do that before leaving."

"Robert, are you giving me this motorcycle?"

"Yes."

I looked at him.

"Not lending it?"

"Giving it."

"Why?"

"Because you'll need it."

That seemed to be his answer to everything.

I touched the fuel tank.

Before the Devastation, I had owned a motorcycle. A cheap machine that required constant maintenance, but it had taken me everywhere. It had disappeared during the first winter, along with nearly everything else I owned.

I hadn't expected to have another one.

Robert handed me a small ring of keys.

"She's yours."

"She?"

"Motorcycles are always women."

"That's ridiculous."

"Then she's perfect for you."

I laughed.

He opened the luggage rack and showed me the equipment he had prepared: a tool kit, two spare spark plugs, a length of fuel hose, a small medical pouch, a flashlight, water purification tablets, and several folded maps.

There was also a spare fuel container.

"I managed to get enough petrol for the first part of your journey," he said. "You'll need to trade for more along the way. There are two settlements marked on the map where people still sell fuel."

I checked the tires.

They were worn but serviceable.

"Have you tried starting it?"

"No."

"Then how do you know it works?"

"Because you repaired it."

"That's a dangerous level of confidence."

I turned the key, opened the fuel tap, and pushed the starter.

The engine coughed twice.

Then it started.

I listened carefully. The idle was a little uneven, but the engine sounded healthy enough.

I opened the throttle.

The motorcycle responded immediately.

I couldn't stop smiling.

Robert watched me.

"You like it."

"No."

"You're smiling."

"I'm checking the carburetor."

"Of course."

I switched off the engine.

"There's one problem."

"What?"

"The rear brake needs adjusting, and I want to replace the chain before taking it on a long trip."

"Can you do that?"

I stared at him.

"Robert."

He raised his hands.

"Sorry. I'll stop asking."

***

We spent the next day preparing the motorcycle.

I adjusted the brakes, replaced the chain with one salvaged from another machine, and checked every electrical connection. Robert helped when I needed an extra pair of hands, although his mechanical abilities were limited and he had an unfortunate habit of putting tools down in places where nobody could find them.

At lunchtime, I discovered another problem.

The front wheel bearing was beginning to fail.

I showed it to Robert.

"See?"

He bent down.

"I see a wheel."

"Very helpful."

I explained the problem. He listened without pretending to understand more than he did, then fetched the spare bearing I had left in the workshop.

By evening, the motorcycle was ready.

We pushed it outside and stood looking at it.

"Well," Robert said. "Now we have a recruiter."

"An extremely underpaid recruiter."

"You're getting a motorcycle."

"A secondhand motorcycle."

"A very fine secondhand motorcycle."

"With a missing mirror."

"I'll give you mine."

"You only have two."

"I'll manage."

I shook my head.

"You're impossible."

He smiled.

"So I've been told."

***

The night before I left, we sat by the river.

The generator was running in the workshop, and the electric lamp shone through the open doorway. Beyond that small circle of light, the valley was dark.

Robert had spread his notebook across his knees.

"What are you drawing now?" I asked.

"The entrance."

"Again?"

"I haven't decided where the main gate should be."

I looked at the sketch.

"Put it on the eastern side."

"Why?"

"The road approaches from there. The slope is gentler, and we can make vehicles slow down before they reach it. If you put it on the southern side, they'll have a straight run at the wall."

Robert considered this.

"You're right."

He crossed out the old entrance and drew a new one.

I watched him for a moment.

"You know what worries me?"

"Tell me."

"Suppose we build your fortress. Suppose it works. We have walls, gardens, electricity, people. What happens when someone decides he should be in charge?"

Robert closed the notebook.

"Then the others will have to stop him."

"What if it's you?"

He looked at me.

For once, he didn't answer immediately.

"Then you'll have to stop me."

I studied his face.

"Would you let us?"

"I hope so. But don't rely on my good intentions. We need rules that apply to everybody, including me."

I liked that answer.

Not because it was reassuring. It wasn't.

It was the first time Robert had admitted that his future settlement might need protection from its own founder.

"All right," I said. "I'll look for your people."

"Our people, if they decide to come."

"You're very particular about words."

"Words become rules. Rules become habits. We'd better choose them carefully."

I took the list from my pocket and studied it by the firelight.

Three names.

Three journeys.

Three strangers who might decide whether a fortress could really become a home.

"One more thing," I said.

"Yes?"

"If I find somebody who isn't on your list but needs help, what do I do?"

Robert looked toward the river.

"Use your judgment."

"That's not a very precise instruction."

"No. But I trust you."

***

I left the following morning.

Robert stood beside the workshop, holding a metal cup of coffee. The sun was just beginning to rise over the eastern hills.

I fastened my luggage, put on my helmet, and started the motorcycle.

The engine settled into a steady rhythm.

Robert approached and handed me a folded envelope.

"What's this?"

"A letter."

"For whom?"

"All three of them. It explains what we're building and what I can offer. There are also individual notes for each person."

I tucked it inside my jacket.

"Anything else?"

"Yes. Don't take unnecessary risks."

I looked at him.

"That's a remarkable thing to hear from a man who attacked three armed lunatics in a shop."

"I didn't attack them."

"You turned off the lights and dragged me out while somebody was shooting."

"All right. Perhaps I took a small risk."

"A small risk?"

He laughed.

Then his expression became serious.

"Vasya, if things go wrong, come back. Even if you haven't found anybody. I don't want you getting killed because you think you have to prove something to me."

I nodded.

"And Robert?"

"Yes?"

"Don't start building the walls without an engineer."

He raised his cup.

"I promise."

I accelerated along the dirt road.

In the mirror, I saw him standing beside the workshop, watching me leave.

The valley disappeared behind the trees.

For several kilometers I rode without speaking. The engine worked well, the morning air was cool, and the road was almost empty.

At a crossroads, I stopped and unfolded the map.

North Station was the nearest destination.

*Dr. Irina Morozova.*

A physician who might still be treating patients in a ruined hospital.

I wondered what sort of person would remain in a hospital four years after the end of civilization.

Perhaps a courageous one.

Perhaps a stubborn one.

Perhaps somebody who had nowhere else to go.

I folded the map and continued north.

By late afternoon, the outskirts of the city appeared on the horizon.

I slowed down as I approached the old railway district.

The hospital was still standing.

That was the good news.

The bad news was the barricade across its entrance.

There were armed men outside, two armored vehicles parked beside the gate, and a flag hanging from the hospital roof.

I recognized the symbol immediately.

A white arrow painted inside a black circle.

The emblem of the Clear Path.

I stopped behind a ruined bus and switched off the engine.

For several minutes I watched the men at the entrance.

Then I took Robert's list from my pocket.

*Dr. Irina Morozova.*

I looked at the hospital again.

"Oh, wonderful," I muttered. "Of all the hospitals in the world, it had to be this one."

I put the list away and began studying the surrounding streets.

Robert had told me not to take unnecessary risks.

Unfortunately, he had also asked me to find a doctor.

And I had a feeling that this particular doctor was going to be very difficult to reach.
