# Chapter 21: Interlude — Glass Rooms

*I wasn't there for what follows. Nobody from the valley was. I know it only because, much later, she told it to me herself, one evening by the river, in a voice so steady that I understood she had told it to herself many times before. I have written it down as closely as I can remember.*

***

The hall was on the ninth level of Necrologia, three hundred meters beneath the mountain.

It was called the Winter Garden. The ceiling was a single vault of glass, lit from above by panels that imitated the sky: pale blue in the mornings, gold at noon, a soft violet in the evenings, so that the people who lived there would not forget what time it was. Beneath the glass grew orange trees in marble tubs, and jasmine, and a lawn of real grass that a team of gardeners trimmed every night with scissors.

There were fountains. There was a string quartet that played every afternoon from four until six. There were waiters in white jackets who brought coffee that tasted like coffee.

She hated it.

She sat at a small table beside one of the fountains, in the grey uniform of the Medical Service of the Central Power, with the two silver stars of a colonel on her collar. Her dark hair was pinned up tightly at the back of her neck. On the table in front of her lay a tablet, a cup of coffee she hadn't touched, and her hands.

Her hands were the only part of her that she still recognized.

They were a surgeon's hands, long-fingered and steady, with short, clean nails. On the left hand, she wore two rings. One was the plain gold band of an officer's service ring, given to every colonel of the Central Power on promotion. The other was thinner, older, scratched, and slightly too loose, because she had lost weight in the first year and never regained it.

She turned it with her thumb while she read. It was a habit she had never been able to break.

***

The newspaper was called *The Surface Herald*.

Nobody in Necrologia took it seriously. It was printed once a week on the seventh level by a man in a cream-colored suit who sent reporters up to the surface to bring back curiosities: a village that worshipped a broken satellite, a man who had trained wolves to pull a sledge, a commune that lived on a river barge and spoke only in rhymes. The residents of the upper levels read it at breakfast and laughed.

This week, the front page was different.

*THE TURQUOISE FORTRESS*, said the headline. *An Architect's Utopia on the Surface.*

Beneath it was a photograph of walls.

She had never seen walls like them. Grey stone, faced with tiles the color of shallow sea, arranged in long flowing patterns like waves. Round towers with conical roofs. A flag, turquoise, with three white stripes. A tall man in a long black coat standing beneath a carved lintel, smiling.

She read the article slowly.

It described a valley in the north, two hundred kilometers beyond the last outpost of the Central Power. A river, a quarry, fields that produced three times what their people needed. A collection of four hundred varieties of seed, the only one of its kind left on the surface. A clinic. A school. Three hundred people governing themselves by vote.

It described the architect, Luis Herrera, who had designed the walls and saved the settlement during a coup. It quoted him at length.

*The world has never left anything alone that was worth taking.*

*A place like this is too valuable to be left defended by votes.*

On the third page, almost as an afterthought, there was a paragraph about the founder.

*The settlement was established three years ago by a foreigner known only as Robert, originally from Argentina. Mr. Herrera describes him as "a saint and a dreamer". He was seriously wounded in the recent unrest and is said to be in poor health.*

Beside the paragraph there was a small photograph.

A man sitting on a rock beside a river. A stick across his knees. A chicken asleep beside him. He was looking out at something beyond the edge of the picture, and he didn't know the camera was there.

He was thinner than she remembered. Greyer. There were lines on his face that hadn't been there before.

But she knew how he held his head when he was thinking. She knew the shape of his hands on the stick. She knew the black leather jacket, which had a tear on the left shoulder that she had once mended with dental floss in a hotel in Lisbon because she couldn't find thread.

The tear was still there. Mended with something else now. Wire, possibly.

She put the tablet down on the table.

She sat very still for a long time.

The string quartet was playing something by Schubert. A fountain splashed. A waiter in a white jacket asked whether she would like fresh coffee.

She didn't answer him.

After a while, he went away.

***

She had not seen him for four years and four months.

She knew the number exactly, because she had counted, the way he had counted in his bunker, although she didn't know that yet. She had counted on the wall of a field hospital tent in the south, and then on the wall of a refugee camp, and then on the inside cover of a notebook she still carried in the breast pocket of her uniform.

The first week, before the Devastation, they had called up every medical officer in the reserve. She had kissed him in the doorway of their flat. She had told him it would be a month. She had said, *Wait for me*, and he had said, *Always*, and then she had gone.

The month had become a year. Then the end of the world.

Her field hospital had been evacuated south when the long grey came. Then further south. Then the trucks had stopped, and the roads had disappeared, and she had spent two winters in a camp in the mountains, treating frostbite and radiation sickness and despair with a dwindling supply of everything.

She had sent messages north. Every month. By radio, by traveler, by anyone who would carry them.

None of them had been answered.

And then, in the third winter, the men from Necrologia had come to the camp. They were looking for doctors. They offered food, safety, warmth, medicine, a laboratory with equipment she hadn't seen since before the Devastation. They offered her a rank and a uniform.

And they told her that the north was gone.

All of it. Every city above the fiftieth parallel. They had aerial survey photographs to prove it. Burned. Frozen. Empty.

She had believed them.

She had cried for three days, and then she had gone with them, because there were children in the camp who needed medicine she couldn't give them, and the men from Necrologia promised that the children would come too.

They had kept that promise. She was grateful to them for that. She had been grateful for almost two years.

She looked at the photograph of the man on the rock.

*The north was gone.*

She turned the ring on her finger.

***

At five o'clock, she was summoned to the Directorate.

She had expected it. The article had appeared in the morning. By noon, she had heard three different officers discussing it in the corridors of the Medical Service. By three, the whole of the ninth level was talking about the turquoise fortress, the seed collection, and the fields that produced three times what they needed.

Because Necrologia was hungry.

Nobody on the upper levels said so. Nobody in the Winter Garden would have believed it. But she was a colonel of the Medical Service, and she read the nutrition reports every week, and she knew.

The oyster tanks on the fourth level had been failing for eight months. A bacterial infection that nobody could identify. The hydroponic farms on the fifth and sixth levels had a blight that turned the lettuce black and the tomatoes to mush. The algae vats that provided protein for the lower levels — the workers' levels, the levels the residents of the Winter Garden never visited — were producing a third of what they had a year earlier.

In six months, perhaps eight, there would not be enough food for everybody.

The Directorate had known for some time. They had been looking for a solution.

And this morning, a man in a long black coat had handed them one on the front page of *The Surface Herald*.

***

The Directorate met in a room with no windows on the eleventh level.

There were seven of them, around a table of black glass. She knew them all. She had treated four of them personally — two for heart conditions, one for gout, one for a nervous complaint he believed was secret. Beside the table, at attention, stood General Varga, commander of the Southern Column, a broad, grey, careful man who had once been an artillery officer in a country that no longer existed.

The chairman of the Directorate held up a copy of *The Surface Herald*.

"You've read this, Colonel?"

"Yes."

"Your assessment."

She considered her words carefully.

"The seed collection is genuine, I think. The reporters brought back soil samples and a photograph of the vault. The fields, I can't judge. If the figures are accurate, they could make a significant contribution to our food supply."

"Significant," the chairman repeated.

"Very significant."

The chairman nodded slowly.

"The Directorate has decided," he said, "to extend the protection of the Central Power to the settlement known as Our Utopia. General Varga's column will depart in five days. Land cruisers, infantry, engineers. The settlement will be asked to accept Central Power administration. Their seed collection and surplus harvests will be transferred to Necrologia for the benefit of all."

"Asked," she said.

"Asked," said the chairman pleasantly. "Of course. We are not the Clear Path."

"And if they refuse?"

The chairman glanced at General Varga.

"Then the General has his orders," he said.

She looked at General Varga. He didn't look back at her. He was looking at the black glass of the table, with the expression of a man who has given and received orders for thirty years and has long ago stopped hoping that any of them would be pleasant.

"The article says they have a shelter," she said. "Under the quarry. Seven chambers. Enough for the whole population."

"Mr. Herrera was most helpful in that respect," said the chairman. "General Varga's engineers will deal with it."

"Deal with it how?"

Nobody answered.

She sat very still.

Then she said, "I want to go with the column."

The chairman looked at her in surprise.

"You, Colonel? Why?"

She had prepared the answer while she waited outside the door. It was a good answer. It was even partly true.

"Because if the settlement resists, there will be casualties. Theirs and ours. General Varga's column has two field medics and no surgeon. And because if the settlement accepts, someone will have to assess the health of three hundred people who have been living on the surface without proper medical care for three years." She folded her hands on the table, so that nobody would see them. "And because I'm the only senior officer of the Medical Service who has lived on the surface since the Devastation. I know how those people think. They won't trust soldiers. They might trust a doctor."

The chairman looked at her for a long moment.

Then he smiled.

"Very commendable, Colonel. Very well. You'll accompany General Varga as chief medical officer of the column." He turned to the General. "Any objection?"

General Varga looked at her for the first time.

She didn't know what he saw. She hoped it was a doctor.

"No objection," he said.

***

That night she sat on the edge of her bed in her quarters on the ninth level, with the lights off and the artificial sky outside her window turning slowly from violet to black.

She held the tablet in her lap. She had enlarged the small photograph until it filled the screen.

A man on a rock beside a river. A stick across his knees. A chicken.

She looked at his face for a long time.

*Robert, originally from Argentina, said to be in poor health.*

She had not cried in two years. She had decided, on the day she put on the grey uniform, that she would not cry again, because she had cried enough for one lifetime and it had never once helped a patient.

She didn't cry now.

She took the thin, scratched ring off her finger and held it up in the light from the tablet. Inside the band, very small, worn almost smooth, there was an inscription. Two words in Spanish, engraved in a jeweler's shop in Valencia fifteen years earlier, on a morning when she had been late for work and he had made her later.

*Siempre. R.*

Always.

She put the ring back on her finger.

Then she got up, turned on the light, and began to pack.

***

*She told me that the hardest part of the next five days was not the packing, or the preparations, or the long briefings with General Varga about routes and fuel and ammunition. The hardest part was deciding what she would say when she saw him.*

*She told me that she rehearsed it every night, in the dark, the way he had once talked out loud to her in his bunker so he wouldn't forget the sound of her name.*

*And she told me that when the moment finally came, she didn't say any of it.*

*But that comes later.*
