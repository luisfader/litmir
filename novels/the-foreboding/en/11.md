# Chapter 11: White Flag

The council argued for three hours about who should go.

Robert wanted to go himself. That was the first thing everybody agreed on: he couldn't.

"You're the one Grom wants," said Martin. "If he holds you, he holds the valley."

"Nobody holds the valley. The valley holds itself."

"Tell that to three hundred men with motorcycles."

"Martin is right," said Irina. "You'd walk into his camp, ask him a reasonable question, and he'd cut your throat while you were waiting for the answer."

"That's unfair."

"It's a diagnosis."

Robert looked around the table. Daria. Sasha. Anna Petrovna. Kennet. Irina. Martin.

"Then let's vote," he said.

They voted six to one.

Robert was the one.

He sat for a moment, looking at the hands raised around the table. Then he nodded slowly.

"All right. I accept the decision."

"Do you?" asked Martin, watching him closely.

"Rules that apply to everybody," said Robert. "Including me. I said that before you were here." He smiled slightly. "I just didn't expect to find it so unpleasant."

***

So it was Kennet and Martin who went.

Kennet, because he was American and calm and very large, and because Luis said that Grom respected size more than almost anything. Martin, because he had been a soldier, because he understood men who believed in strength, and because, as he said, if anybody was going to be shot under a white flag, it might as well be someone who had already been shot once.

And me, because somebody had to drive the motorcycle while Kennet held the flag.

"Why can't Kennet drive?" I asked.

"Because the last time Kennet drove a motorcycle," said Martin, "he drove it into the river."

"It was icy," said Kennet.

"It was August."

"It was icy in spirit."

Luis drew us a map. He marked the road into the hills, the passes, the river crossings, the place where Grom's scouts would stop us. He marked the camp itself: an old mountain village, half-ruined, with Grom's hall in the center, built from the stones of the church.

"He'll be in the hall," he said. "He'll be sitting by the fireplace. He'll make you wait. Don't sit down unless he tells you to. Don't look at the dogs. Don't mention me."

"Why not?" asked Martin.

"Because he'll ask you whether I'm alive, and if you say yes, he'll want to know where. And if you say no, he'll want to know who killed me, so that he can kill them too." Luis paused. "Grom's feelings are complicated."

"Any advice?"

Luis thought about it.

"Don't make him laugh," he said. "He's at his most dangerous when he's amused."

***

We left at dawn on a cold April morning.

I drove Robert's touring motorcycle, because it was larger and more stable than mine. Martin rode behind me. Kennet followed in the truck with a pole fixed to the cab, from which hung a white bedsheet that Sasha had signed out of the stores with visible pain.

The road climbed into the hills through forests that were still patched with snow. Twice, we saw smoke on the ridges ahead. Once, a motorcycle appeared on the road behind us, followed us for a kilometer, and disappeared.

The scouts stopped us at a stone bridge over a mountain river, exactly where Luis had said they would.

There were six of them. Big men, bearded, with long hair tangled into ropes and decorated with beads, bones, bottle caps, and in one case what looked like the keys of a piano. They wore leather and furs and pieces of armor taken from riot police. Two had rifles. The others had axes, spears, and a crossbow.

They looked at the white flag, at Kennet, and at Martin.

"Utopia," said their leader. He pronounced it as if it were the name of a disease.

"Our Utopia," said Martin. "We've come to talk to Grom."

The leader grinned. His teeth were filed into points.

"Grom doesn't talk," he said. "Grom eats."

"Then he can eat after he's talked."

***

The camp was worse than Luis had described, and also better.

Worse, because there were more of them than I had imagined. Hundreds. They lived in the ruins of the old village, in houses roofed with tarpaulins and sheets of metal, around fires where whole sheep were roasting on spits. There were dogs everywhere, enormous dogs with spiked collars, which watched us pass with yellow, interested eyes. Children ran between the houses, filthy and loud and laughing, and stopped to stare at Kennet.

Better, because it was not a camp of monsters.

It was a village.

Women were cooking. An old man was repairing a motorcycle in the doorway of a barn, with a care I recognized. Two girls were carrying water. On the steps of a half-ruined house, a young woman sat sharpening a long knife and watching us with dark, steady eyes.

She stood up as we passed.

She was perhaps twenty-two, tall and lean, with black hair shaved at the sides and braided on top, and a thin white scar that cut through her left eyebrow. She wore a leather jacket covered with metal studs and carried the knife as though it were part of her hand.

"You're the ones from the blue walls," she said.

"Yes," said Martin.

"Is it true what they say?"

"That depends on what they say."

She walked beside us. The scouts made no attempt to stop her. One of them even stepped aside to make room.

"That nobody owns anybody there," she said. "Not even women. Not even children. That everybody votes. Even the ones who don't fight."

"It's true."

"Even the ones who were slaves before?"

Martin looked at her.

"Even them," he said.

The young woman was silent for a few steps.

"Grom will kill you," she said. "Probably not today. But he'll kill you."

"We'll see."

"I'm Zhenya," she said. "If he doesn't kill you today, remember my name."

Then she turned and walked back to her steps, and sat down, and went on sharpening her knife.

***

The trebuchets stood at the edge of the village.

There were two of them, enormous, built from whole pine trunks lashed and bolted together, each with a counterweight box the size of a truck. I couldn't stop looking at them as we passed. It was impossible not to admire them, the way you admire a wolf.

And then I noticed the pins.

Each trebuchet's throwing arm rotated on a steel axle that passed through two massive wooden uprights. The axle was held in place at each end by a pin. On Luis's machines, I was sure, the pins would have been steel, heavy and carefully fitted.

On these machines, the pins were wood.

Somebody had built them from Luis's drawings without Luis. And somebody had decided that wood was good enough.

I didn't say anything.

But I remembered it.

***

Grom made us wait for two hours.

We stood in his hall, in front of the fireplace that was big enough to roast a pig, and in which a pig was in fact roasting. We stood while dogs walked around us, sniffing our boots. We stood while men came and went, laughing and shouting and paying us no attention whatsoever. Kennet held the white flag. Martin held his face perfectly still. I held my bladder.

Then Grom came in.

He was not as tall as Kennet. That was the first thing I noticed. But he was broader, much broader, and he moved like a man who had never once in his life stepped aside for anybody. Half his face was scarred by an old burn that had melted his left ear and pulled his mouth into a permanent half-smile. He wore a coat made from the skins of several different animals, some of which I couldn't identify.

He sat down in a chair by the fire. It was the only chair in the hall.

"So," he said. "Utopia."

"Our Utopia," said Martin.

"Ours." Grom tasted the word. "Whose ours?"

"The people who live there."

"How many?"

"Enough."

Grom laughed. It was a deep, rumbling, genuinely amused laugh, and I remembered Luis's warning and felt my stomach turn over.

"Enough," he said. "I like that. Sit down, soldier. You were a soldier, weren't you? I can see it. Sit."

Martin didn't move.

"We'll stand."

"Suit yourself." Grom took a knife from his belt and cut a piece of meat from the roasting pig without getting up. He ate it slowly. "So. You've come to beg."

"We've come to offer."

"Offer what?"

Martin glanced at me. I took the paper from my jacket — the list Robert had written the night before, in his careful handwriting.

"Seed grain," I read. "Two hundred kilos of potatoes and wheat, from the Pinefield collection, every spring. Medical treatment for your sick and wounded, by Dr. Irina Morozova, at our clinic. Repairs to your vehicles and machines. Trade at fair prices for your meat, leather and timber. And peace."

Grom stopped chewing.

"Peace," he repeated.

"Yes."

"From you to me."

"Yes."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he looked at Martin, and at Kennet, and at the white bedsheet on its pole.

"Do you know what I do," he said, "with people who offer me things?"

"Tell me," said Martin.

"I take what they offer. And then I take the rest."

"There's nothing to take," said Martin. "The seeds only grow if somebody knows how to plant them. The doctor only heals if she wants to. The machines only work if somebody repairs them. You can burn our walls. You can kill our people. But you can't take what makes the valley worth having. It isn't in the valley. It's in the people."

Grom stopped smiling.

"Pretty speech," he said. "Did your Robert write it?"

"No," said Martin. "I did."

There was a silence in the hall. Even the dogs seemed to be listening.

"You know what I think?" Grom said finally. "I think you were a soldier. A good soldier. I think you held a line somewhere, once, and watched it break. And then you came out into this new world and you found out that the strong take and the weak give, and that's how it's always been. And you knew it. You knew it in your belly." He leaned forward. "And now some foreigner with a notebook has told you it doesn't have to be that way, and you've believed him. Like a child believes in a fairy tale."

Martin's jaw tightened. He didn't answer.

"Here's my offer," said Grom. "You go back. You tell your Robert that he has until the new moon. On the night of the new moon, he opens his gate. My people come in. They take what they want, and the people who stay will work for me, and I'll let them live. Nobody gets hurt. Nobody burns. Your doctor keeps her clinic. Your farmers keep their fields. They just give the harvest to me."

"And if he doesn't open the gate?"

Grom smiled his half-smile.

"Then I'll throw your blue walls over your blue walls," he said, "until there's nothing left but blue dust."

He stood up.

"Go. Before I change my mind."

***

We rode out of the hills in silence.

At the stone bridge, the scouts stood aside to let us pass. Their leader grinned at us with his filed teeth and drew a finger across his throat.

Kennet waved at him cheerfully.

It was dark when we reached the river at the foot of the hills. I stopped the motorcycle to let the truck catch up. Martin climbed off and walked to the edge of the water. He stood there for a long time, looking at the current.

I went and stood beside him.

"He was wrong," I said. "About you."

Martin didn't look at me.

"No," he said. "He wasn't."

"Martin—"

"I held a line. In the south, before the Devastation. Eleven years. And then one day the orders stopped coming and the line broke and everybody ran, and I ran with them. And then I found the Clear Path, and they told me that the strong take and the weak give." He picked up a stone and threw it into the river. "And he's right. I knew it in my belly. I still know it."

"Then why did you say what you said? In there. About the people."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Because I wanted it to be true," he said. "More than I've wanted anything in years."

Kennet came down from the truck and stood on Martin's other side. The three of us watched the dark water.

"Nine days," said Kennet. "Until the new moon."

"He won't wait nine days," said Martin. "He'll attack before. He'll attack when he thinks we're arguing about whether to surrender." He turned toward the valley, where the turquoise walls were just visible on their hill in the starlight. "That's what I'd do."

***

He was right again.

Six nights later, Kennet was on watch in the central tower when the bell began to ring.

I ran up the stairs to the rampart in my socks, holding my boots. Martin was already there, and Luis, and Robert, standing in a row at the eastern wall, looking out.

The whole eastern slope was full of lights.

Hundreds of them. Torches. Headlights. Fires. They moved slowly down through the trees toward the valley floor, like a river of burning oil.

And behind them, rising over the ridge against the stars, two enormous black shapes.

The trebuchets.

"So," said Luis quietly. "He's brought my children with him."

Robert didn't say anything.

He was looking at the lights. Then he looked down at the valley inside the walls: at the tents and the fields and the clinic and the farmhouse, where three hundred people were waking up and starting to understand.

"Positions," said Martin. His voice was perfectly calm. "Everybody who can hold a rifle on the walls. Everybody who can't, to the clinic. Kennet, the north gate. Vasya, the generator, and get the floodlights working. Luis—"

"Yes?"

"How long before those things are in range?"

Luis looked at the trebuchets.

"Dawn," he said.

Martin nodded.

"Then we have until dawn," he said, "to think of something Grom has never heard before."
