# Chapter 10: The Architect

He arrived during a snowstorm, which I later learned was entirely typical of him.

Martin's night watch saw the headlight first: a single yellow eye moving slowly down the eastern slope through the falling snow, disappearing among the trees and reappearing again. By the time Martin had woken Kennet and Kennet had woken me, the motorcycle had reached the foot of the hill and stopped beside the turquoise mountain of tiles.

A man climbed off.

He was tall and lean, in a long black coat that reached almost to his ankles and was covered with snow. His hair was dark and long, tied back at the neck, and he had the kind of face that belonged on the cover of a magazine about expensive watches: high cheekbones, a straight nose, and a short beard trimmed with a precision that seemed impossible in a world without barbers.

He was holding his hands up.

"I'm unarmed," he called. "Well. Mostly unarmed. There's a knife in my boot, but it's for bread. I'm looking for a man named Robert."

Martin kept the rifle on him.

"Who are you?"

The stranger lowered one hand to brush the snow from his shoulder, very slowly, the way a man moves when he knows he is being watched through a sight.

"Luis Herrera," he said. "I'm the architect."

***

Robert met him in the farmhouse kitchen.

I was there, and Kennet, and Martin, who refused to leave and who sat by the door with his rifle across his knees, watching the newcomer like a dog watching a fox. Maksim had gone back to Raven Dam a week earlier, which turned out to be fortunate for everybody.

Luis took off his coat, shook it, hung it carefully on a nail by the stove, and looked around the kitchen.

He looked at the cracked plaster, the table made from a door, the mismatched chairs, the bucket in the corner where the roof leaked.

"Charming," he said.

"It's temporary," said Robert.

"Everything is temporary. Some things are just temporary for longer." He sat down without being invited. "I saw your foundations on the way in. Who designed them?"

"Maksim Orlov."

Luis laughed. It was a warm, delighted laugh, the laugh of a man meeting an old friend at a party.

"Maksim! Of course. Only Maksim would put a four-meter foundation under a wall that isn't there. That man would reinforce a soap bubble if you gave him enough concrete." He shook his head fondly. "Is he here?"

"He went back to the dam."

"Pity. I'd have enjoyed telling him the eastern section is in the wrong place."

"Is it?" I asked.

Luis looked at me for the first time.

"You're the mechanic. The one with the train."

"How does everybody know about the train?"

"My dear friend, everybody within two hundred kilometers knows about the train. In the hills, they say you drove it with one hand and fought off forty men with the other."

"That isn't quite what happened."

"It never is. That's what makes a good story." He turned back to Robert. "Yes, the eastern section is in the wrong place. It follows the contour of the hill, which is sensible, but it leaves a dead zone at the bottom of the slope where attackers can gather without being seen from the walls. You'll need to move it out by about twelve meters and add a tower at the corner." He smiled. "Maksim builds things that keep water out. Water doesn't hide."

Robert opened his notebook to the page with the seventh drawing of the wall and pushed it across the table.

Luis studied it.

He studied it for a long time, without speaking. He turned the notebook sideways, then upside down. He traced a line with his finger.

"Who drew this?"

"I did."

"It's very bad."

"I know."

"The gate is on the wrong side."

"It's on the east side," I said. "The road comes from there, and the slope makes vehicles slow down."

"Exactly. Which is why it's on the wrong side. You *want* them to slow down? You want a column of motorcycles sitting at your gate, at a comfortable speed, while they decide which part of your wall to break?" He shook his head. "No. You put the gate where the approach is long and narrow and visible from three towers at once. Then they slow down because they're terrified."

I opened my mouth to argue. Then I closed it again.

He was right.

"And this," said Luis, tapping the page, "is the most important part of the whole drawing."

He was pointing at the words above the gate. *OUR UTOPIA.* And the three sentences beneath.

"You're going to carve these into the stone?"

"Yes," said Robert.

"Good. Carve them deep. People forget words that aren't carved." He closed the notebook and slid it back across the table. "I'll build your walls."

There was a short silence.

"We haven't asked you yet," said Robert.

"You were about to."

"I wanted to ask you something else first."

"Ah." Luis leaned back in his chair. "You want to know why I left the Wild."

"Yes."

"And whether they sent me."

"That too."

Luis looked at Martin, at the rifle, at Kennet standing silently by the stove, and at me.

When he spoke again, his voice had changed. The amusement had gone out of it.

"They're coming in the spring," he said. "As soon as the passes are clear. Eight clans, perhaps three hundred fighters. Motorcycles, trucks, two armored cars they took from a military depot. And catapults."

"Catapults?" said Kennet.

"Siege engines. Trebuchets, technically. Counterweight design, about twelve meters high, capable of throwing a hundred kilos of stone two hundred meters." Luis looked down at his hands. "Or a barrel of burning oil."

"How do you know?" asked Martin.

Luis didn't look up.

"Because I designed them."

***

He told us the whole story that night, sitting by the stove with a cup of Kennet's terrible coffee.

He had been in the hills when the Devastation came, on a commission to design a mountain hotel that would never be built. The Wild had found him in the first winter, half-starved in the shell of the hotel, and had been about to kill him for his boots when he told their chief, a man called Grom, that he could build things.

"I built them a bridge," he said. "Then a granary. Then a tower. Then a hall for the chief, with a fireplace big enough to roast a pig, because Grom wanted a fireplace big enough to roast a pig." He smiled faintly. "I was very good at it. They loved me. I was the only man in the hills who didn't have to carry a weapon, because everybody knew that if anybody touched me, Grom would feed them to his dogs."

"And the catapults?"

"Grom wanted to take the Clear Path's fuel depot at Kurgan. Its walls were too thick for rifles. So I designed him something that could break them." He was silent for a moment. "We took the depot. Nobody died. The Clear Path ran away as soon as the first stone came over the wall. I told myself it was just architecture."

"And now?"

"Now Grom has heard about a valley with a river, a doctor, seeds, fields and walls made of blue tiles. And he wants it." Luis looked at Robert. "He asked me to build two more catapults. Bigger. Strong enough to throw burning oil over a wall."

"And you said no."

"I said no." He shrugged elegantly. "It was the first time in four years anyone had heard me say no to Grom. It was a very short conversation. Afterward, I left in the night with my motorcycle and my drawings, and I rode for three days through the snow, and here I am."

"Why here?" asked Robert. "You could have gone anywhere."

Luis was quiet for a long time.

"Because I heard about your rules," he said finally. "In the hills, everybody was laughing at them. *No one owns another person.* A man named Robert, building a fortress without a king. They thought you were the funniest thing since the Devastation." He looked at the fire. "I didn't laugh. I thought, I've built bridges and towers and halls for a man who owns three hundred people. Perhaps, before I die, I'd like to build something for people who own themselves."

He smiled again, and the warmth came back into his face.

"Also," he said, "I've never designed a fortress covered in turquoise bathroom tiles. I'm curious to see whether it can be done without looking absurd."

"Can it?" I asked.

"Certainly not," said Luis. "But it can be done *magnificently* absurd. That's quite different."

***

Martin didn't trust him.

"He built Grom's catapults," he said to Robert the next morning. "He lived with them for four years. For all we know, Grom sent him here to find our weak points."

"For all we know," said Robert, "Grom wants to kill him."

"Same thing, if he's lying."

"Then we'll watch him."

"I'll watch him," said Martin.

"You'll watch him," Robert agreed. "And Vasya will work with him every day, and tell me everything he builds. And the council will decide whether we accept his plans."

"And if he betrays us?"

Robert looked out of the window at the snow falling on the foundations.

"Then I'll have made a mistake," he said. "I'd rather make that mistake than turn away the only man who can build our walls before spring."

***

Working with Luis Herrera was like working with a hurricane that had studied at a very good university.

He woke at four every morning and drew until breakfast. He drew on everything: paper, cardboard, the back of Sasha's ledger pages, which caused an incident that I will not describe. He drew walls, towers, gates, staircases, drainage channels, firing positions, and an extraordinary number of decorative arches that nobody had asked for.

"We don't need arches," I told him.

"Nobody needs arches. That's why they're beautiful."

"We need walls before spring."

"You'll have walls before spring. You'll also have arches. Civilization is the difference between the two."

He redesigned Maksim's eastern foundation, as he had promised, and sent a long letter to Raven Dam explaining why. Maksim's reply arrived a week later, carried by one of the young men from the village. It was a single sheet of paper with one word on it, which I will not repeat.

Luis read it and laughed until he had to sit down.

"He agrees," he said, wiping his eyes. "He's furious, but he agrees."

He moved the gate to the north side, where the road had to climb along a narrow ledge between the river and the hill, visible from three towers. He designed the towers: four of them at the corners, round, with conical roofs, and a fifth, taller one in the center, beside the flagpole.

"A watchtower," he said. "With a bell."

"We don't have a bell."

"Then find one."

Kennet found one in a church in a village twenty kilometers away. It took four men and the truck to bring it back.

And Luis designed two things that he showed to nobody except Robert and me.

The first was a room.

It was hidden inside the thickness of the wall beside the central tower, behind a section of stonework that could be opened by pressing a particular tile. It was small — four meters by three — with a ventilation shaft, a water cistern, and space for perhaps six people.

"Every fortress has one," said Luis. "For the moment when everything goes wrong."

"What could go wrong?"

He looked at me with genuine pity.

"Vasya. Everything."

The second was a tunnel.

It began behind the clinic and ran down through the rock of the hill to the old quarry, where Luis planned to excavate a series of chambers in the limestone, deep enough to survive anything a catapult could throw.

"A shelter," he said. "For everybody. Women, children, the old, the sick. If the walls fall, they go down. And if the quarry entrance is closed, nobody above will ever know it's there."

"That's months of work," I said.

"Years," Luis agreed. "But we'll start now. Because one day, someone will come with more than catapults."

Robert studied the plans in silence.

"Who else knows about this?" he asked.

"You. Vasya. And me."

"The council should know."

Luis raised an eyebrow.

"Robert. A secret that seven people know is not a secret. It's a rumor with a waiting list."

Robert hesitated. Then he nodded slowly.

"The room stays between us," he said. "The shelter, the council will hear about. When it's ready."

"As you wish." Luis rolled up the drawings. "You know, you're a very strange leader."

"I'm not a leader."

"That," said Luis, smiling, "is exactly what I mean."

***

By the end of February, the walls had risen two meters above the foundations.

They weren't finished. They weren't even half finished. There were gaps on the western side where the stone hadn't arrived yet, and the northern gate was a hole in the wall with two tree trunks across it. But they were walls. Grey limestone, thick as a man is tall, faced on the outside with turquoise tiles that Luis had arranged in long, flowing patterns like waves.

When the sun came out, the whole hill shone.

People from Brookfield, who had lived their whole lives in a grey town on a grey river, would stop on their way to the fields and simply look at it.

"Magnificently absurd," said Luis, standing beside me on the half-built rampart one morning.

"You said that before."

"It bears repeating."

He looked east, toward the hills where the snow was beginning to melt.

"Six weeks," he said. "Perhaps eight. Then the passes will be open."

"Will the walls be ready?"

"No."

"Then what do we do?"

Luis was silent for a long time.

"Grom is not stupid," he said. "He's cruel, and proud, and he enjoys being feared. But he's not stupid. Before he attacks anywhere, he always sends someone to talk. To see whether the people inside will surrender without a fight." He turned to me. "Your friend Robert should be ready for that conversation."

"What should he say?"

Luis smiled, but there was no amusement in it.

"Something Grom has never heard before," he said.
