# Chapter 8: Pinefield

The third name took me west, alone.

Kennet stayed in the valley. Robert needed him. Forty-three people were now living beside the river, in tents and lean-tos and the back of Kennet's truck, and every one of them had arrived with a different idea of what Our Utopia was supposed to be. Some thought it was a refugee camp. Some thought it was a farm. One family had arrived under the impression that Robert was a prophet, and were disappointed to discover that he was mostly a man with a notebook who asked a lot of questions.

Martin was walking again, slowly, with a stick. He had not yet decided whether he was staying. In the meantime, he had taken it upon himself to organize night watches, because, as he said, he would rather be shot sitting up than lying down.

"Go," Robert told me. "Find Daria. We'll be planting potatoes until you come back."

"You'll be planting potatoes forever if I don't."

"That," he said, "is exactly why you're going."

***

The Pinefield Agricultural Research Station stood on a low plateau surrounded by forest, two days' ride west of the valley.

I smelled it before I saw it.

Smoke. Not wood smoke, but the sharp, oily smell of burning tires. When I came out of the trees, I saw a column of it rising from the plateau ahead, black against the grey sky.

I stopped the motorcycle and took out the binoculars.

The station consisted of a long, low laboratory building, a cluster of glass greenhouses — most of them still intact, glittering dully in the overcast light — and a square concrete structure half-buried in the hillside behind them, with a heavy steel door.

The seed vault.

In front of the vault, a barricade had been built from overturned tables, filing cabinets and sheets of greenhouse glass. Behind the barricade stood perhaps a dozen people. Most of them were children or teenagers. One of them, in the center, was an old woman in a long grey coat, holding what looked like a garden hoe.

In front of the barricade stood a crowd.

I counted fifty people before I gave up. Men, women, more children. They were thin, ragged, quiet. Some held sticks or knives. Most held nothing at all. They weren't shouting. That was what frightened me most. They stood facing the barricade in silence, the way people stand at a funeral.

The tires were burning in an oil drum between the two groups.

I put the binoculars away and sat for a while with my hands on the handlebars.

Then I started the engine and rode toward them.

***

Nobody tried to stop me.

The crowd parted to let me through, watching me with dull, incurious eyes. I stopped the motorcycle beside the burning drum and switched off the engine. In the silence, I could hear the tires crackling.

"Who are you?" shouted the old woman from behind the barricade.

"My name is Vasya. I'm looking for Daria Sokolova."

"Why?"

"A man called Robert sent me."

"I don't know any Robert."

"He knows you. He heard your message on the radio last winter. You were looking for fuel for your greenhouses."

The old woman lowered her hoe slightly.

"He's a little late."

"He's been busy."

"So have we."

She was about sixty, tall and bony, with white hair pulled back in a bun and a face that had spent most of its life outdoors. She looked past me at the crowd.

"If you've come to help them," she said, "you can turn around."

"Help them do what?"

"Eat my seeds."

***

It took me an hour to understand what was happening, and I understood it mostly thanks to a red-haired woman named Sasha.

She found me while I was trying to talk to the crowd. She was perhaps thirty, small and quick, with freckles and green eyes and red hair cut short with what looked like garden shears. She wore a man's coat several sizes too large. She was the only person in the crowd who looked angry rather than defeated.

"You're wasting your time," she said. "They won't talk to you. They don't talk to anybody anymore."

"Who are they?"

"Brookfield. The town down the valley." She pointed. "What's left of it. The crops failed last year, the year before, and the year before that. The Clear Path took the last of the grain in the winter. Half the town is dead. The other half is here."

"And you?"

"I used to run the warehouse at the Brookfield cooperative." She laughed shortly. "Logistics manager. Thirty-two thousand tons of grain a year. Now I can't find one sack."

"Why are they here?"

"Because there are seeds in that vault." She nodded toward the steel door. "Wheat. Barley. Rye. Beans. Potatoes. Four hundred varieties, she says, in sealed jars, collected from all over the world. Enough to feed Brookfield for a month."

"And then?"

"And then nothing. Because they'll have eaten the seeds." Sasha looked at me. "Do you understand? She's right. I know she's right. If we eat them, there's nothing to plant next spring. Ever again. But you can't tell a mother with a dying child that she should wait for next spring."

I looked at the crowd. At the children.

At the old woman behind the barricade, with her hoe.

"How long have they been standing here?"

"Three days."

"Has anybody tried to break through?"

"Twice. She hit a man with the hoe. Broke his arm." Sasha shrugged. "Nobody's tried since. But they're getting weaker. And hungrier. And sooner or later, somebody will decide that it's better to die fighting than to die standing still."

***

I went back to the barricade.

"Dr. Sokolova."

"I'm not a doctor. I'm an agronomist. I taught biology to fourteen-year-olds for twenty years before I came here, and they were less trouble than this."

"Can I come in?"

"Are you armed?"

"No."

She looked at me suspiciously.

"Nobody travels unarmed anymore."

"I'm not very popular," I admitted. "But I'm still alive."

She thought about that. Then she nodded to a teenage boy beside her, who moved a filing cabinet aside to let me through.

The boy was about seventeen, thin, with a long neck and enormous glasses mended with wire. He had a pair of headphones around his neck and a battered radio receiver clipped to his belt.

"You're the one on the motorcycle," he said. "From the east."

"How do you know?"

"I heard you." He tapped the radio. "Two days ago. There's a crew near the crossroads that talks about everybody on the road. They said a drifter on a green motorcycle was heading west, and that he was the one who stole the train."

"I didn't steal it. I borrowed it."

"They said you stole seven children too."

"I borrowed them as well."

The boy grinned.

"I'm Dima," he said.

***

Daria Sokolova took me into the vault.

It was cold and dry inside, lit by a single electric lamp powered by a bank of car batteries that Dima had connected to a solar panel on the roof. Steel shelves lined the walls from floor to ceiling. On the shelves stood jars. Thousands of them. Each sealed with wax, each labeled in small, careful handwriting.

*Triticum aestivum. Winter wheat, Krasnodar, 1987.*

*Phaseolus vulgaris. Climbing bean, Oaxaca, 2004.*

*Solanum tuberosum. Potato, 43 varieties, seed form.*

"My husband started this collection," said Daria. "Forty years ago. He went to every agricultural station in the country. He traded with collectors in Mexico, India, Ethiopia, Peru. He believed that one day, some catastrophe would destroy the crops we depended on, and someone would need to start again."

"Was he right?"

"He was always right. It was extremely irritating." She touched one of the jars. "He died the first winter. Pneumonia. I've been keeping them since."

"Alone?"

"With the children. Dima's from Brookfield. So are most of the others. Their parents sent them up here when the crops failed, because I had greenhouses and they thought I could feed them." She laughed bitterly. "I can. For now. Potatoes and cabbage and tomatoes. A little wheat. But not for fifty more. Not for a whole town."

"And if they break in?"

"Then they'll eat the seeds and die next year instead of this year." She looked at me. "And then there will be nothing. For anybody. Forever."

I looked at the shelves.

Four hundred varieties. Forty years of a man's life. The whole future of agriculture for a hundred kilometers in every direction, sitting in wax-sealed jars in a cold room on a hill.

And outside, fifty starving people.

"How much land do you need?" I asked.

She frowned.

"What?"

"To plant them. Not all of them. Enough to feed the people outside. How much land? How much water? How many people working?"

"Why?"

"Because I know a valley," I said. "With a river, and a quarry, and a great deal of empty land. And a man who's been planting potatoes and waiting for somebody who knows what she's doing."

***

It took the rest of the day.

Daria didn't trust me. That was reasonable. The people of Brookfield didn't trust anybody. That was also reasonable. And Sasha trusted nothing at all, including herself, which turned out to be the most useful quality of all.

Because Sasha could count.

She sat on the barricade with a notebook and a stub of pencil, and asked Daria questions. How many kilos of seed potatoes for one hectare. How long until the first harvest. How many people to work a field. How much water.

Then she asked me questions. How far the valley was. What kind of soil. How many people already lived there. How much food they had stored.

"Not much," I admitted. "Potatoes. Some dried fish. One chicken."

"One chicken."

"Her name is Eleanor."

Sasha closed her eyes briefly.

Then she went back to her notebook.

When she finished, she stood up and turned to face the crowd. She raised her voice.

"Listen to me!"

The crowd stirred. A few faces turned toward her.

"There's food in the greenhouses. Not much. Enough for three days, if we're careful. She'll give it to us." She looked at Daria. "Won't you?"

Daria hesitated.

Then she nodded.

"And then," said Sasha, "we walk. All of us. To a valley in the east, where there's land and water and people who'll let us plant. We take the seeds with us. We don't eat them. We plant them. And in four months, we'll have potatoes, and beans, and wheat. And next year, more."

A man in the crowd laughed harshly.

"And who's going to feed us for four months?"

Sasha looked at me.

I opened my mouth. Then I closed it again. I didn't know what Robert would say. I didn't know whether he had enough food. I didn't even know whether the valley would still be there when we arrived.

Then I remembered the second rule.

*No one goes hungry while food can be shared.*

"We will," I said.

It was the most reckless thing I had ever promised.

The crowd was silent.

Then a woman at the front, holding a child, began to cry.

***

We left Pinefield five days later.

Seventy-one people. Daria, with the jars of seeds packed in straw in three wheelbarrows and a handcart, which she refused to let out of her sight. Dima, with his radio and a car battery strapped to his back. Fourteen children from the greenhouses. Fifty-four people from Brookfield.

And Sasha.

She organized everything. The order of march. The rations. The water stops. The rotation of who pushed the carts and who carried the smallest children. Every evening she counted everybody twice. Every morning she counted them again.

"Why twice?" I asked her.

"Because the first time I'm usually wrong."

"Are you?"

"No. But I like to be sure."

On the fourth day, Dima picked up a voice on his radio.

It was Robert.

He had found an old military transmitter in the cab of the locomotive, which Kennet had dragged back to the valley piece by piece. It took Dima twenty minutes to establish a stable connection. When he did, he handed me the headphones with a grin.

"Vasya?" The voice was distant and crackling, but unmistakable. "Vasya, is that you?"

"It's me."

"Where are you?"

"About a day away."

"Did you find her?"

"I found her."

"Is she coming?"

I looked back at the column stretched out along the road behind me. Seventy-one people, three wheelbarrows of seeds, a handcart, a radio and a car battery.

"Robert," I said. "How many potatoes do we have?"

There was a long pause.

"Why?"

"Because I made a promise."

***

We came down the road into the valley the next afternoon.

Robert was waiting at the bottom of the hill. Kennet was beside him, and Irina, and Martin, leaning on his stick. Behind them stood the forty-three people who already lived beside the river, and the tents, and the trench at the foot of the hill, and the pole on the summit, still without a flag.

They watched us come.

Robert didn't say anything. He looked at the column for a long time. At the children. At the wheelbarrows. At Sasha, counting everybody for the third time.

Then he walked up to Daria Sokolova and held out his hand.

"Welcome to Our Utopia," he said. "We've been waiting for you."

Daria looked at him, at the valley, at the river and the bare hillside.

"You have no fields," she said.

"Not yet."

"You have no irrigation."

"Not yet."

"You have no walls."

Robert smiled.

"I'm told," he said, "that's what engineers are for."
