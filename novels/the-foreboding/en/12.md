# Chapter 12: Catapults

The first stone came over the wall at six minutes past five in the morning.

I know the time exactly because I was looking at my watch. Not the broken one that had stopped at 3:17. Robert had given me another the week before, an old Soviet watch with a cracked crystal, and told me that a man who repaired generators should at least know how long they ran.

I heard the trebuchet before I saw anything. A long, groaning creak from the dark slope, like a tree falling very slowly. Then a whistle. Then nothing, for a second that lasted much longer than a second.

Then the stone hit.

It struck the western rampart thirty meters to my left, where the wall was still incomplete. The sound was not like an explosion. It was deeper and duller, the sound of something enormous meeting something that refused to move. Turquoise tiles burst from the wall in a glittering cloud and rained down into the courtyard like broken sky.

The wall held.

I stood on the rampart with my mouth open, watching the dust settle, and the wall held.

"Maksim," I said aloud. "You beautiful, paranoid man."

***

The second stone fell short. The third went over the wall entirely and landed in Daria's potato field, where it made a crater large enough to bury a car.

Daria came out of the farmhouse in her long grey coat and stood at the edge of the crater, looking down into it.

Then she turned toward the eastern slope and shouted something in a language I didn't know. It was probably Latin. It was certainly not a blessing.

"Get her inside!" Martin yelled from the central tower.

Two Brookfield men took her by the arms. She went, still shouting.

The Wild didn't attack the walls.

That surprised me. I had expected them to come charging up the slope with ladders and axes, the way they did in the old films. Instead, they stayed among the trees at the bottom of the hill, just out of rifle range, and waited. Their fires burned. Their motorcycles growled. And every three or four minutes, the trebuchets creaked and groaned, and another stone came over the ridge.

"He's not trying to break the walls," said Luis. He stood beside me on the rampart, very pale, watching the black shapes on the slope. "He's trying to break us."

"How?"

"Fear. Noise. Exhaustion. He'll keep throwing stones until midday. Then he'll stop. Then he'll send a man to the gate to ask whether we've changed our minds." Luis closed his eyes. "And if we haven't, tonight he'll start throwing fire."

"The oil."

"The oil."

I looked down into the courtyard. Three hundred people. Tents. Wooden sheds. The clinic. The greenhouses, with their glass glittering in the dawn.

"How much can a trebuchet throw?" I asked.

"Mine? A hundred kilos. Comfortably."

"And a barrel of oil?"

Luis turned to look at me.

"Two hundred liters. With the barrel, about a hundred and eighty kilos." He frowned. "Why?"

"Because those aren't your trebuchets," I said. "Not exactly."

I told him about the pins.

***

Luis listened without interrupting. When I finished, he stood very still for a long time.

"Wood," he said.

"Pine, I think. Painted black so nobody would notice."

"The axle pins."

"Both ends. On both machines."

Luis gripped the edge of the rampart. When he spoke again, his voice was quiet and very precise, the voice of a man doing arithmetic in his head.

"The pin carries the shear load of the whole arm at the moment of release. With a hundred kilos, a good pine pin might survive. Might. Twenty throws, thirty. With a hundred and eighty..."

"It breaks."

"It breaks on the first throw, or the second, or the fifth. And when it breaks..." He stopped. "The arm is twelve meters long. When the pin fails, it doesn't just fall. It whips sideways. Anyone standing near the base..."

He didn't finish.

"How many people crew a trebuchet?" I asked.

"Eight. Ten. More, to load it."

We looked at each other.

"We have to tell them," I said.

"Tell who? Grom?"

"Somebody. Anybody."

"Vasya." Luis's voice was gentle. "If we tell them, he'll fix the pins, and then he'll throw fire at the clinic."

"And if we don't tell them, ten people die."

"Ten of his people."

"They're still people."

Luis looked away, at the slope, at the black shapes among the trees.

"You know who built those machines?" he said. "Without me? Boys. Sixteen, seventeen. The ones I taught to use a saw. Grom must have given them my drawings and told them to finish the job." He laughed, a terrible small laugh. "They couldn't afford steel. So they used what they had."

He turned and walked toward the central tower.

"Where are you going?"

"To talk to Robert," he said. "This isn't my decision. Thank God."

***

The council met in the central tower while the stones kept falling.

They met standing up, because there was no room to sit, and because every few minutes somebody had to go to the window to see where the last stone had landed. Robert. Irina. Daria, still furious. Sasha, with her ledger. Anna Petrovna. Kennet, with his rifle. Martin.

Luis explained the pins.

Nobody spoke for a while after he finished.

"So," said Martin finally. "If they throw fire, the machines break, and their own people die. And we don't have to do anything."

"Yes," said Luis.

"That's the best news I've heard in years."

"Martin," said Irina.

"What? It's war. In war, the enemy makes mistakes. You don't send him a letter explaining his mistakes."

"It isn't war," said Robert quietly.

Martin turned to him.

"Then what is it? Three hundred men with motorcycles and catapults, throwing rocks at our children. If that isn't war, I'd like to know what is."

"It's what happens," said Robert, "when people have forgotten that there's any other way to get what they need."

"That's very poetic."

"It's also true." Robert looked around the room. "If we let those boys die under their own machines, we win today. Grom loses ten people and both his trebuchets. He goes back to the hills. He tells everybody that the blue fortress cursed him. And in a year, he comes back with something worse, and so do all the others, because they'll know that we let men die when we could have warned them."

"And if we warn them?"

"Then they'll know something else about us."

"That we're fools."

"That we don't want them dead," said Robert. "Even now."

Martin stared at him.

"You're asking us to vote on whether to save the people who are attacking us."

"Yes."

"That's insane."

"Possibly." Robert didn't look away. "Vote."

***

They voted four to three.

Robert, Irina, Anna Petrovna and Kennet voted to warn them. Martin, Daria and Sasha voted against.

Daria said, "They destroyed my field. Let God decide."

Sasha said, "I can count. Ten of theirs dying means fewer of ours dying. I'm sorry. I can count."

Martin said nothing at all. He raised his hand against, and looked at the floor.

When the vote was finished, he raised his head.

"All right," he said. "The council has decided. How do we tell them?"

"Somebody goes out under a white flag," said Robert. "At midday. When Grom sends his messenger to the gate."

"Who?"

"Me," said Luis.

Everybody turned toward him.

"They're my machines," he said. "My drawings. My boys. And Grom will listen to me before he kills me. That's more than he'll do for anybody else."

***

He never got the chance.

At eleven o'clock, the stones stopped falling.

For a few minutes, the valley was completely silent. No creaking, no whistling, no thunder of stone on stone. Birds began to sing in the trees on the western slope. Somebody in the courtyard laughed nervously.

Then I saw the smoke.

At the base of the nearer trebuchet, figures were moving around a fire. They were rolling something toward the machine. Something round and black and heavy.

A barrel.

"He's not waiting for night," said Martin. "He's not waiting for the messenger. He's going to throw fire now."

"Why?" said Kennet.

"Because he's angry. Because the walls didn't fall."

Luis was already running for the stairs.

"Luis!"

"Open the gate!" he shouted. "Open the gate!"

I don't know who opened it. I only know that a moment later I saw him below, running out through the half-finished north gate in his long black coat, waving his arms over his head and shouting in a voice that carried across the whole valley.

"Stop! *Stop!* The pins! The pins are wood! Get away from the machine!"

They were too far away. They couldn't hear him.

Or they heard him and didn't understand.

The barrel was lifted into the sling.

The counterweight was released.

The arm began to rise.

I heard the crack from the rampart, three hundred meters away. It was a small sound, almost delicate, like a dry branch snapping under a boot.

Then the arm of the trebuchet tore free of its axle and swung sideways through the air, twelve meters of pine trunk moving faster than anything that size should ever move, and the barrel of burning oil flew loose from the sling and burst against the trunk of a tree, and the whole slope erupted in flame.

People were screaming.

Luis stopped running. He stood in the middle of the field below the walls and stared at the fire.

Then he started running again.

Toward it.

***

I don't remember deciding to follow him.

I remember being on the stairs. I remember the gate. I remember Robert's voice behind me, and Irina's, and the sound of my own boots on the ground. And then I was in the field, running toward the burning trees, and Kennet was beside me with a fire extinguisher from the workshop in each hand, and Irina was behind us with her medical bag and Lev, carrying the other bag, who had evidently decided that he was coming whether anybody liked it or not.

The Wild didn't shoot at us.

I think they were too shocked.

When we reached the trebuchet, the fire had spread through the dry undergrowth and into the lower branches of the pines. The arm of the machine lay across the ground, broken, smoking. Beneath it, around it, people lay where it had struck them.

Eight. I counted them later. Eight boys and two men.

Luis was on his knees beside one of them, trying to lift the end of the arm with his bare hands.

"Help me! He's under it!"

Kennet dropped the extinguishers and took the other side of the trunk. Together, groaning, they lifted it twenty centimeters. I pulled the boy out.

He was about sixteen. His leg was crushed. He was conscious, and he looked up at Luis with an expression of total bewilderment.

"Master Luis?" he said.

"Hello, Tolya." Luis's voice broke. "I told you to use steel."

"We didn't have any."

"I know. I know."

Irina pushed past me and knelt beside the boy.

"Out of the way. All of you. Vasily, the extinguishers. Kennet, start carrying. Anyone who can't walk goes up the hill to the clinic. Anyone who can walk helps." She looked up at the ring of shocked Wild faces around us. "*All* of you. Do you understand? You want them to live, you carry."

Nobody moved.

Then a young woman pushed through the crowd.

Black hair braided on top, shaved at the sides. A scar through her left eyebrow. She knelt beside Irina without a word, took one of the wounded men under the arms, and looked up at the others.

"You heard her," said Zhenya. "Carry."

***

We carried them up the hill.

Eight boys and two men, and then the four who had been burned trying to put out the fire, and then a man who had broken his arm falling from the second trebuchet when he climbed it to get a better view.

Fifteen people. Wild people, in their leather and furs, with their beaded hair and their filed teeth, carried through the gate of Our Utopia on blankets and doors and the backs of Brookfield farmers.

Inside the walls, nobody said anything.

They just watched. Three hundred people standing in silence while the enemy was brought into their clinic.

Irina worked for fourteen hours.

I didn't see most of it. I was outside with Kennet and the Brookfield men, cutting firebreaks in the forest until the fire on the eastern slope burned itself out against the river. When I came back, it was dark.

The Wild were still at the bottom of the hill.

Their fires were burning. Their motorcycles were silent.

Nobody had thrown a single stone since the trebuchet broke.

***

Grom came to the gate at midnight.

He came alone, on foot, without a white flag, without his dogs, without his coat of skins. He stood at the bottom of the north ramp and looked up at the gate.

Robert went down to meet him.

Martin went with him, and Luis, and I followed at a distance, because by then I had decided that Robert should never be allowed to go anywhere without someone who could describe it afterward.

Grom looked at Luis for a long time.

"You," he said.

"Me," said Luis.

"You told them. About the pins."

"I tried. I was too late."

"Why?"

Luis looked at him.

"Because those were my boys, Grom. I taught them. You gave them my drawings and sent them to build something they couldn't build properly, and then you put them under it and told them to throw fire at a hospital." His voice was shaking. "What did you think would happen?"

Grom didn't answer. He turned to Robert.

"My son is in your house."

Robert nodded slowly.

"Tolya," he said. "With the crushed leg. Irina says he'll keep it. He'll limp for the rest of his life, but he'll keep it."

Grom's face did not change. But something in his shoulders did.

"And the others?"

"Two have died. The rest will live."

"You carried them."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Robert looked at him for a long moment.

"No one goes hungry while food can be shared," he said. "No one is refused medical care. We didn't make an exception for you, because if we made an exception for you, it wouldn't be a rule. It would just be a mood."

Grom stood at the bottom of the ramp for a long time.

Then he laughed.

It wasn't the deep, rumbling laugh from his hall. It was smaller, rougher, almost helpless.

"A mood," he said. "A mood."

He turned around and walked back down into the dark.

***

In the morning, the Wild were gone.

They had taken the second trebuchet apart in the night and carried it away. They had left nothing behind except the broken arm of the first one, lying across the burned ground at the foot of the eastern slope, and a single motorcycle parked at the foot of the north ramp.

Its rider was sitting on the ramp, waiting.

It was Zhenya.

Behind her stood thirty-one young men and women from the Wild clans. They carried their belongings in bags and bundles. Some had weapons. Most didn't.

Martin went down to meet them.

"What do you want?" he asked.

Zhenya stood up.

"You said nobody owns anybody here," she said. "Not even women. Not even the ones who were slaves before."

"That's right."

"Grom owned my mother. Then he owned me." She lifted her chin. "Now he doesn't."

Martin looked at her. At the thirty-one behind her.

"You'll have to follow the rules," he said.

"I know the rules. I heard them in the hall."

"You'll have to work."

"I can work."

"You'll have to vote."

Zhenya smiled for the first time. It was a fierce, startled smile, as if she had just discovered a word she hadn't known existed.

"Show me how," she said.

Martin turned and looked up at the walls. At Robert, standing on the rampart beside the turquoise flag.

Robert nodded.

Martin stepped aside.

And the Wild came into Our Utopia, not over the walls, but through the gate.
