# Chapter 18: The Concert

The fever came at midnight, exactly as Irina had said.

I was sitting outside the clinic, because I couldn't think of anywhere else to sit. Through the window I could see the lamp burning on the table beside Robert's bed, and the shape of Irina moving back and forth, and another shape, motionless, in the chair by the wall.

Natalia had not left since they carried him in.

At midnight, Robert began to talk.

I couldn't hear the words through the glass. Only the sound of his voice, rising and falling, sometimes urgent, sometimes almost laughing. Irina came to the door once and stood on the steps with her cigarette, listening to it with her eyes closed.

"What's he saying?" I asked.

"Spanish, mostly. Some English. Some words I don't know." She took a drag. "He's in the bunker, I think. Counting days on the wall. Then he's somewhere else, on a road. Then he's talking to someone."

"Who?"

Irina didn't answer.

She didn't have to. Through the window, I could see Natalia lean forward in her chair and take his hand, and Robert's voice changed, softened, and said one word, very clearly, over and over.

The name was the same.

The woman holding his hand was not.

***

I went in at three in the morning to bring Irina coffee.

Robert was burning. Even from the doorway I could see it: the flush on his face, the sweat soaking the pillow, the way his whole body shook under the blankets. Irina had packed cloths soaked in river water around his neck and under his arms. She was boiling willow bark on the stove, which Daria had stripped from the trees by the river in the dark.

"It's aspirin," Daria had said. "The original kind. Before the chemists stole the idea."

It wasn't enough. Everybody knew it wasn't enough.

Natalia was still in her chair. She had wrapped his hand in both of hers.

"He thinks I'm her," she said quietly, without looking up.

"I know."

"He keeps telling me about the bunker. How he counted the days. Seven hundred and thirty. He scratched them on the wall with a nail." Her voice was very steady. "He says he talked to her every night. Out loud. So he wouldn't forget the sound of his own voice saying her name."

I didn't know what to say.

"And then he says, *You came back. You came back. I built you a fortress.*" She looked at me for the first time. Her eyes were red. "What do I say to that, Vasya? What do I say?"

"I don't know."

"I said yes," said Natalia. "I said, yes, I came back. Because he was so happy. And because..."

She stopped.

Robert stirred and murmured something. She bent over him and smoothed the wet hair off his forehead, the way you would with a child.

"Because I wanted to," she whispered.

***

In the morning, the fever was worse.

Irina came out of the clinic at seven o'clock and stood on the steps for a long time without lighting her cigarette.

"Infection," she said. "In the wound. I was afraid of it."

"What do we do?"

"Antibiotics. And we have none." She looked at me. "Without them, three days. Maybe four."

I went to Dima.

Dima spent the whole morning on the radio. He called every frequency he knew. The Kurgan traders. Raven Dam. The settlements on the main road. A woman in a village forty kilometers south who had once traded medicine for repairs.

Nobody had antibiotics. Or if they had them, they weren't saying.

At noon, Luis came to the workshop.

He looked as though he hadn't slept either. His long hair was loose, his beard was untrimmed for the first time since I had known him, and there were dark circles under his eyes.

"Dima told me," he said. "About the antibiotics."

"Nobody has any."

"Somebody does." Luis looked out of the workshop door toward the guardhouse, where Martin and Zhenya were being held. "The Arbiter does."

I stared at him.

"Martin's agreement?"

"Martin made a deal with the Clear Path. Diesel and medicine every month, in exchange for peace and rifles. The rifles are in our cabinet now. The deal is still open." Luis's voice was very quiet. "Somebody has to go to the ford tonight. And somebody has to bring back antibiotics."

"The council would have to vote."

"The council voted yesterday that I speak for them until the assembly meets." Luis held my gaze. "Robert will be dead before they finish arguing about it."

"Luis—"

"Do you want him to live?"

I didn't answer.

"Then help me load the diesel," said Luis.

***

I helped him load the diesel.

I've thought about that many times as well.

He took forty liters from the stores, signed for by Sasha with a face like a wall, and four jars of Irina's salves that Irina gave him without a word. He went down to the ford alone at midnight with a lantern. I watched him from the wall.

He was gone for an hour.

When he came back, he was carrying a small cardboard box.

He went straight to the clinic and gave it to Irina. She opened it under the lamp. Inside, wrapped in plastic, were twelve vials of a broad-spectrum antibiotic and a packet of sterile syringes, with labels from a pharmaceutical company that hadn't existed for four years.

She looked at Luis for a long time.

"What did you promise him?" she asked.

"Enough," said Luis.

He walked out of the clinic before she could ask anything else.

***

The concert was the next evening.

I didn't think anyone would come. The courtyard had been silent all day. People went to the fields and came back and went into their houses. Children were kept indoors. The Brookfield men guarded the guardhouse in shifts. Nobody spoke much.

But at sunset, they came.

All of them. Three hundred people, sitting on the ground in front of the central tower, in the same place where two days earlier they had stood in a ring around two men with sabres. Somebody had raked the earth smooth, so there was no blood. Somebody had lit torches. Anna Petrovna's children had made a banner from strips of turquoise cloth: *FOR ROBERT*.

Natalia and Oxana sat on two chairs in the torchlight.

Natalia hadn't slept in three days. Her face was pale and thin, and she held her cello as though it were the only thing keeping her upright. Oxana was beside her, very quiet, without her usual grin. She kept looking toward the guardhouse.

Natalia closed her eyes and began to play.

It was something I had never heard before. Not the sad song from the ice cream van, not the dances. Something new. It began very low, very slowly, a single long note that seemed to go on forever. Then a second. Then a melody, climbing slowly upward, faltering, falling back, climbing again.

I didn't understand what it was at first.

Then I did.

It was a man getting up off his knees.

Again and again. Falling and rising. Falling and rising. The melody kept trying to climb, and kept being knocked back, and kept trying again, and every time it rose a little higher, until at the end, very quietly, almost too quietly to hear, it reached a note that it had been trying to reach the whole time, and held it.

And stopped.

Nobody applauded.

Three hundred people sat in complete silence in the torchlight.

Then, from the guardhouse, through the barred window, a single voice said, "Again."

It was Martin.

Oxana stood up, very quickly, and walked away from the light. I saw her standing alone by the wall with her face in her hands.

***

Luis came out after the music.

He came out of the shadows by the central tower and stood in the torchlight beside Natalia's empty chair. He had tied his hair back again and trimmed his beard. He was wearing his long black coat. In the firelight, he looked like a figure from a painting.

He didn't say anything for a long moment.

He let them look at him.

Then he spoke.

"I want to tell you about Robert," he said.

His voice was soft. Not the hurricane voice. Not the voice that explained arches. The voice he had used on the steps of the ice cream van, at three in the morning.

"Two years ago, I came to this valley in a snowstorm. I'd been living with the Wild. I'd built their catapults. I'd built a hall for a man who owned three hundred people." He paused. "And Robert let me in. He didn't ask what I'd done. He asked what I could do. And then he gave me two kilometers of empty hillside and said, *Build something for people who own themselves.*"

He looked around the courtyard.

"I've built bridges in six countries. I've never built anything like these walls. Not because of the stone. Not because of the tiles. Because of what's inside them. You. All of you. Robert's idea, made real." He took a breath. "And two days ago, a man who was supposed to protect it turned guns on you."

He didn't look toward the guardhouse. He didn't have to.

"He hurt eleven of you. He broke Anna Petrovna's wrist. He made a secret deal with the Clear Path — the people who once tried to sell Vasya, and who held Dr. Morozova's children as debt. And then, when Robert stood in front of him with nothing but an old sword and the rules, he cut him down." Luis's voice didn't rise. It got quieter. "Robert is in that clinic tonight, fighting for his life, because he refused to yield. Because he believed that if he yielded, you would learn that the man with the sharpest blade decides."

Somebody in the crowd was crying.

"So I ask you," said Luis. "What did Robert fight for? For us to stand here tonight and argue for a week about whether the man who did this deserves another chance? For us to vote, and debate, and deliberate, while the Clear Path counts our diesel and the Wild count our rifles, exactly as Martin said they would?" He shook his head. "No. Robert fought so that we would never again let anybody turn this place into something it isn't. Not Martin. Not Zhenya. Not anyone."

"What are you asking?" called Daria. Her voice was sharp.

Luis turned to her.

"Two things," he said. "First, that the people who took part in this — Martin, Zhenya, and those who held rifles against you — leave Our Utopia. Tomorrow. With food for a week and their own belongings. Not as punishment. Robert would never want punishment. As protection. For everyone else."

There was a murmur. Some people were nodding.

"And second?" said Daria.

"That until Robert is well enough to return to the council, someone takes responsibility for keeping this place safe. Not a Defense Council. Not five people with rifles. One person, chosen by you, answerable to you, who will hand everything back the day Robert walks out of that clinic." Luis spread his hands. "I'm not asking for myself. I'm asking for someone. Anyone you trust."

"And who would that be?" said Irina from the clinic steps.

Luis looked at her.

"I'm not a member of the council," he said. "I've never been elected. I've never asked to be. But if you want me, I'll serve. For as long as you need me. Not a day longer."

***

They voted by show of hands.

It was the first time in Our Utopia that a vote was taken that way. Before, it had always been pebbles in jars, or ballots on telephone-directory paper, counted twice by Sasha. But it was dark, and everyone was exhausted, and Luis said that a show of hands was quicker, and nobody argued.

Expulsion of Martin, Zhenya, and the others who took up arms.

A sea of hands went up.

Luis as Steward of Our Utopia until Robert's recovery.

A sea of hands again.

Daria didn't raise her hand. Neither did Irina. Neither did Anna Petrovna, nor Kennet, nor Sasha.

Nor did I.

But we were twelve, perhaps fifteen, in a crowd of three hundred.

Luis looked out over the hands for a long moment.

"Thank you," he said quietly. "I'll try to deserve this."

He walked back into the shadows.

Behind him, in the torchlight, Natalia's cello stood alone on its chair.

***

They left at dawn.

Thirty-four of them. Martin. Zhenya. Twenty-one of the young Wild who had come through the gate a year earlier. Eleven Brookfield men, including Fyodor, whose wife and four children stood at the north gate and watched him go and didn't follow.

Each of them carried a bag of food. Potatoes, beans, bread. Sasha had packed it herself, and counted it twice, and given each of them exactly the same amount.

Martin came last.

He stopped at the gate, where I was standing.

"How is he?" he asked.

"Irina says the fever broke an hour ago. The antibiotics are working."

Martin closed his eyes briefly.

"Good," he said.

"Luis got them," I said. "From the Arbiter. At the ford."

Martin opened his eyes.

He looked at me for a long time. Then he looked up at the central tower, where, in the council room window, I could see a tall figure in a long black coat, watching us.

"Of course he did," said Martin softly.

He turned to go. Then he stopped.

"Vasya."

"Yes?"

"I was wrong. About a lot of things." He hesitated. "But I was right about one."

"What?"

"Some decisions get made by one person, quickly, in the dark." He looked at the window. "Watch him. Watch him very carefully."

Then he walked out of the gate.

Oxana was standing on the wall above.

She had her violin. She didn't play it. She just stood there, watching Martin walk down the north ramp and along the narrow road between the river and the hill, until he was very small, and then gone.

Then she sat down on the rampart with her back against the parapet and put the violin across her knees and didn't move for a very long time.

***

Robert woke on the fourth day.

I was the first person he saw, because Natalia had finally fallen asleep in her chair and Irina had gone out to smoke. He opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling, and then at me.

"Vasya," he said. His voice was a cracked whisper.

"You're alive."

"Apparently." He tried to move and winced. "Martin?"

I told him.

I told him about the concert, and the vote, and the show of hands. About the thirty-four at the gate. About Luis, and the ford, and the twelve vials in the cardboard box.

Robert listened without interrupting.

When I finished, he lay for a long time looking at the ceiling.

"The assembly decided," he said finally.

"By show of hands. In the dark. After a funeral march on a cello."

"It's still the assembly."

"Robert—"

"It's still the assembly, Vasya." He closed his eyes. "If I say it isn't, because I don't like the result, then I'm Martin. Then I'm the one with the sharpest blade."

"And Luis?"

"Luis saved my life."

"Luis made a deal with the Clear Path in the dark, the same deal Martin was expelled for, and then stood in front of three hundred people and asked for power."

"For a time," said Robert. "Until I'm well."

"And when you're well?"

Robert was silent.

Then he turned his head on the pillow and looked at Natalia, asleep in her chair, with her blonde hair falling over her face and her hand still resting on the edge of his bed, where it had been for four days.

He looked at her for a long time.

"She stayed," he said.

"The whole time."

"I remember her voice. In the fever. I thought..." He stopped.

"I know what you thought."

Robert closed his eyes.

"Four years," he said, very quietly, so quietly I almost didn't hear it. "Four years, I've been faithful to a ghost."

He didn't say anything else.

But when Irina came back in, he was asleep, with his face turned away from Natalia toward the wall. And on his face, even in sleep, was the expression of a man who had done something unforgivable.

Not to Martin. Not to the assembly.

To someone who wasn't there.
