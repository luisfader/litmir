# Chapter 19: The Amber Ring

Luis was a very good Steward.

That was the problem.

In the first week, he repaired the leaking roof of the farmhouse school, which had been dripping on Anna Petrovna's children for two years while the council discussed whether to use the tiles for the roof or the walls. He simply decided, and it was done, in two days.

In the second week, he reorganized the night watch. He replaced the Brookfield men who didn't know how to hold their rifles with a rotation of the most reliable people in the valley, and he gave each of them a whistle and a lantern and a schedule written in his elegant handwriting and nailed to the guardhouse door.

In the third week, he finished the western wall.

It had been incomplete since the siege. The council had been arguing about the stone, the labor, the priority. Luis walked out to the quarry one morning with Tolya and forty volunteers and a bottle of wine he had been saving since Valencia, and by sunset, the gap was closed.

People cheered.

They cheered him every day now. When he walked across the courtyard in his long black coat, children ran after him. Brookfield women brought him bread. Men who had never spoken to him before stopped him to shake his hand.

"You see?" said Luis to me one evening, standing on the finished western rampart, looking out over the valley. "This is how things should be done. Decide. Do. Move on."

"Robert would say that's how kings are made."

"Robert," said Luis, "is still in bed."

***

Robert was still in bed.

The antibiotics had saved his life, but the wound was slow to heal. Irina wouldn't let him walk more than a few steps. He lay in a small room at the back of the clinic that had been Irina's office, with the window open onto the courtyard, and listened.

He listened to everything.

He listened to the cheering. He listened to the hammers on the western wall. He listened to the assembly bell, which rang less often now, because Luis said that people were tired of meetings, and that the important decisions could be made by the council.

The council met every week, as before.

But it met in the council room, with two of Luis's new guards outside the door. Luis chaired it. He listened to everybody, and he was charming and patient and reasonable, and then he decided.

"He's not breaking any rules," Daria told Robert. "That's what makes me so angry. He asks our opinion. He lets us vote. And then somehow, the vote always goes his way."

"How?"

"Because he talks to everybody beforehand. Separately. In the corridor. In the fields." She shook her head. "By the time we sit down, it's already decided."

"That's called politics," said Robert.

"It's called manipulation."

"Sometimes," said Robert, "it's hard to tell the difference."

***

At the end of the second month, the term of the council expired.

The rule had been simple, from the beginning: every three months, the assembly votes again. Every seat. No exceptions. Robert had insisted on it, two years earlier, beside the river.

Sasha brought the ledger to the council meeting and opened it to the page where the rule was written.

"The election is due," she said. "Next Sunday."

Luis smiled at her.

"Of course. I've been thinking about that."

"Thinking what?"

"That perhaps this isn't the best moment." He spread his hands. "Robert is still recovering. The Clear Path agreement is delicate. Martin and Zhenya are somewhere out there in the hills, with thirty people, and we don't know what they're planning. If we hold an election now, in the middle of all this uncertainty..." He shook his head. "People are frightened, Sasha. Frightened people make bad decisions."

"Frightened people elected you," said Sasha.

Luis's smile didn't change.

"Yes. And I'm grateful. But I think it would be wiser to wait. A month. Perhaps two. Until things are settled."

"The rule doesn't say *when things are settled*. It says every three months."

"The rule was written for peaceful times."

"So was the rule about not owning people," said Sasha. "Should we suspend that too?"

There was a short, cold silence.

"I think," said Luis gently, "that we should put it to a vote."

They voted.

Four to three, to postpone the election.

Luis. Anna Petrovna, who was frightened. Two new members who had been elected the night of the concert, to replace Martin and Sasha, who had resigned and then been reinstated by Daria, and then resigned again.

Against: Daria. Irina. Kennet.

Sasha wasn't allowed to vote. She had resigned.

***

The carving appeared a week later.

It was above the north gate, in the stone lintel beneath the three rules. The rules had been there since the spring: deep, clean letters, cut by Tolya under Luis's direction, exactly as Luis had promised a year earlier. *Carve them deep. People forget words that aren't carved.*

*No one owns another person.*

*No one goes hungry while food can be shared.*

*Every adult has a voice in the decisions of the community.*

Below them, now, in smaller letters, slightly more ornate, there was a fourth line.

*Designed and built by Luis Herrera, Architect.*

I stood in front of the gate and looked at it for a long time.

"It's true," said Tolya, who was standing beside me with his chisel. He sounded uncomfortable. "He did design it."

"Did he ask the council?"

Tolya didn't answer.

"Did he ask anybody?"

"He said it was traditional," said Tolya. "On cathedrals. Bridges. The architect always signs his work."

"This isn't a cathedral."

"No," said Tolya, looking up at it. "It's bigger."

***

The harvest festival was at the end of September.

It was Daria's idea, originally. The fields had produced more that year than anyone had believed possible — potatoes, wheat, beans, squash, corn, cabbages the size of a man's head. The storehouse was full for the first time since the valley had been settled. Sasha had counted everything three times and then, for the first time I could remember, closed her ledger and laughed.

So there was a festival.

It filled the whole courtyard. There were tables made of doors and planks, covered with food. There were lanterns strung along the walls. Children ran between the tables with faces painted turquoise. The Raven Dam villagers came, with Granny Zoya at their head and a cart full of smoked fish. Maksim came with them and spent the first hour walking around the western wall, examining Luis's work with a frown, and the second hour drinking with Luis and arguing about it.

Robert came.

He walked out of the clinic for the first time in two months, leaning on a stick, with Irina on one side and Kennet on the other. When the people in the courtyard saw him, they stopped what they were doing.

Then they cheered.

It was a different cheer from the one they gave Luis. Quieter. Warmer. People came up to him one by one and touched his arm, or shook his hand, or simply said his name. Anna Petrovna's children brought him a crown made of wheat. He put it on, gravely, and wore it for the rest of the evening.

Natalia and Oxana played.

Oxana's playing had changed since Martin left. It was still fast, still brilliant, but there was something underneath it now that hadn't been there before, something that made the dancers dance harder, as if they were trying to outrun it.

Natalia played the piece she had written. The man getting up off his knees.

This time, the courtyard applauded for five minutes.

And Robert, sitting at the end of a table with a crown of wheat on his head, looked at her across the courtyard the way a man looks at the first light after a very long night.

I saw Luis see it.

***

He did it at midnight.

The music had stopped. The lanterns were burning low. People were sitting at the tables, tired and happy, finishing the last of Granny Zoya's fish. Natalia was packing her cello into its case.

Luis climbed onto the stone platform at the foot of the central tower, where the council sat during assemblies.

"Friends," he called. "Before we go to bed, I'd like to say something."

People turned toward him. The conversation faded.

"Two years ago," said Luis, "I came here with nothing. A motorcycle and some drawings. And tonight, look at what we have." He gestured at the walls, the tables, the full storehouse, the lanterns, the turquoise tiles glowing in the light. "I've built many things in my life. None of them mattered to me as much as this."

Applause.

"But a man can't live inside walls alone." He turned. "Natalia."

She looked up from her cello case.

The whole courtyard turned to look at her.

Luis stepped down from the platform and walked across the courtyard toward her. He walked slowly, in his long black coat, through the lantern light, and people moved aside to let him pass.

He stopped in front of her.

He took something from his pocket.

It was a ring. Gold, very old, set with a large stone that glowed in the lantern light like honey, like a small trapped sun.

Amber.

"The Wild trade it in the hills," said Luis. "From the old mines in the north. Grom gave me this one the day I finished his hall. He said it had belonged to his grandmother." He smiled. "I've carried it for three years. I never knew why. Now I do."

He knelt.

Three hundred people held their breath.

"Natalia," he said. "Will you marry me?"

***

Natalia stood absolutely still.

She looked down at Luis, kneeling in front of her with the amber ring in his outstretched hand. She looked at the faces around her, every one of them turned toward her. Children. Brookfield farmers. Granny Zoya. Maksim, with his wine cup halfway to his mouth.

She looked at Robert.

He was sitting at the end of the table with the crown of wheat on his head. He wasn't moving. His face was very pale.

Natalia looked back at Luis.

"Luis," she said softly. "Please stand up."

He didn't move.

"Please," she said.

He stood up slowly. He was still holding out the ring.

"You're a remarkable man," said Natalia. Her voice was quiet, but in the silence of the courtyard, everybody could hear it. "I've never known anyone like you. You've built something here that I didn't think was possible." She swallowed. "But I told you once that I didn't know what I felt. And I still don't know. And you shouldn't have asked me here. Like this. In front of everybody."

"Is that a no?"

"It's a not like this," said Natalia. "It's a not now. It's a—" She stopped. "I'm sorry. Yes. It's a no."

Luis stood very still.

For a moment, his face showed nothing at all. Then, slowly, it showed everything. Hurt, and disbelief, and humiliation, and something underneath them that I had seen once before, in the starlight, the night of the secret room.

He closed his hand around the ring.

"I see," he said.

He turned and looked across the courtyard at Robert.

Robert looked back at him. Neither of them said anything.

Then Luis turned back to Natalia.

"You'll regret this," he said.

His voice was perfectly quiet. That was what made it frightening.

He walked away through the crowd, and up the steps of the central tower, and into the council room, and closed the door.

***

The festival ended.

People went home in silence. Granny Zoya gathered the Raven Dam villagers and their empty cart. Maksim stood by the gate for a long time, looking up at the carving above it, and then at the council room window, where a lamp was burning.

"I told you," he said to me. "He's the best architect I've ever met." He shook his head. "And the worst loser."

***

At two in the morning, there was a knock on the door of Robert's room at the back of the clinic.

I was there, because I had helped him walk back from the courtyard and then sat down and hadn't left. Robert was sitting up in bed, still wearing the crown of wheat. He had forgotten to take it off.

Sasha came in. Then Kennet, walking now, slowly, with a stick of his own. Then Irina. Then Daria, in her grey coat.

Then, after a moment, Natalia.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Sasha opened her ledger.

"I've been counting," she said.

"Counting what?" said Robert.

"Everything." She turned the pages. "The diesel. Every month since Luis became Steward, forty liters have gone out of the stores to the ford. Signed for by Luis. Four jars of Irina's salves. And last week, two hundred kilos of seed potatoes."

Daria made a sound.

"My seed potatoes."

"Your seed potatoes." Sasha turned another page. "The new guards outside the council room. Six of them. All volunteers, all chosen by Luis. Every one of them on extra rations." She turned another page. "The election. Postponed. Not cancelled. Postponed. Until things are settled." She looked up. "Nobody has said when things will be settled."

"And the carving above the gate," I said.

"And the carving above the gate." Sasha closed the ledger. "He hasn't broken a single rule, Robert. Not one. Every decision, he's taken to the council. Every vote, he's won. The Clear Path deal, the guards, the election." She took a breath. "But I've counted, and I can see where the numbers are going. In six months, there won't be an election. In a year, there won't be a council. And in two years, those six guards will be twenty, and they'll be outside his door, not the council's."

Robert was silent.

"And tonight," said Irina, very quietly, "he told a woman she'd regret saying no."

Everybody looked at Natalia.

She was standing by the door with her arms folded, very pale.

"I'm not afraid of him," she said.

"I am," said Irina. "A little. And I've treated men from the Clear Path."

Robert took the crown of wheat off his head, finally, and looked at it.

"What do you want me to do?" he asked.

"Stop him," said Kennet.

"How? With a sabre?"

"No," said Sasha. She opened the ledger again, to the very first page. *NO ONE GOES HUNGRY WHILE FOOD CAN BE SHARED*. And below that, in her small handwriting, the rules of the assembly, copied out on the night of the first council vote, two years ago.

She pointed to a line.

*Any thirty adults may call an assembly at any time.*

"We don't need Luis to call the election," she said. "We don't need the council. We need thirty signatures." She looked around the room. "There are six of us. We need twenty-four more."

"And then?"

"And then we do it properly. Pebbles in jars. Every adult. No show of hands in the dark. And I count them." Her voice was steady. "Twice."

Robert looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked at Natalia.

"Will you sign?" he asked.

Natalia unfolded her arms.

"I was going to leave in the spring," she said. "I told Luis that. I've been telling myself that for months." She came across the room and sat down on the edge of the bed. "I'm not leaving."

"That isn't an answer."

"Yes," said Natalia. "It is."

Robert held out his hand to Sasha.

She gave him a pencil.

He wrote his name at the top of a blank page in the ledger, slowly, in his careful handwriting. Then he handed the pencil to Natalia.

Then Irina. Then Daria. Then Kennet.

Then me.

Six names.

"Twenty-four more," said Sasha.

"By when?"

She closed the ledger.

"By Sunday," she said. "When the election should have been."
