# Chapter 5: North Station

The generator in the hospital basement was even worse than I had imagined.

Somebody had been running it on a mixture of diesel and used cooking oil. The injectors were clogged with something that resembled black honey, the crankshaft bearing knocked like a prisoner asking to be released, and the exhaust pipe had been extended through a hole in the wall with a length of drainpipe and a great deal of optimism.

"Can you fix it?" asked the crewman who had brought me down.

I was beginning to hate that question.

"Maybe," I said, trying to look stupid. "If I have time. And tools. And diesel. Clean diesel."

"How much diesel?"

"Lots."

He frowned, as if the word "lots" were a complicated technical term.

"I'll ask the sergeant."

***

The sergeant came that afternoon.

I heard him before I saw him: heavy boots on the concrete stairs, a slow, measured step, a voice telling somebody upstairs to put out his damned cigarette before he set the oxygen tanks on fire.

Then he appeared in the doorway.

Narrow face. Close-cropped grey hair. A military jacket without insignia. And a large scar across his chin that I had last seen in the market, at a distance of about half a meter, while its owner was explaining to me that I owed a debt for food that didn't exist.

I kept my eyes on the injector in my hands.

I had a disguise. I was a stupid drifter, good with his hands. I had a cap pulled low over my forehead and four days of beard and a smear of engine grease across one cheek, which Irina had added personally with the expression of an artist finishing a portrait.

It was a terrible disguise.

The sergeant looked at the generator. Then he looked at me.

He looked at me for a long time.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Kolya."

"Kolya." He repeated it slowly, as though tasting it. "Where are you from, Kolya?"

"Around."

"Around," he said. "Of course."

He walked over to the generator and examined the bearing housing I had already dismantled. He didn't touch anything. He simply looked, with the careful attention of a man who had served in an army where things that were broken got people killed.

"How long?"

"Three days. Maybe four."

"You'll need diesel."

"Lots," I said.

The corner of his mouth moved.

"I'll get you your diesel, Kolya." He turned toward the stairs. Then he stopped. Without looking back, he said, "Your friend on the motorcycle. Did he make it home?"

My hands stopped.

The basement was very quiet. The generator wasn't running. I could hear water dripping somewhere behind the wall, and my own heart, which seemed to be trying to leave my body through my ears.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No," said the sergeant. "Of course you don't."

He climbed the stairs.

I sat on the concrete floor for several minutes, waiting for the crewmen to come down and drag me outside.

They didn't come.

***

"He knows," I told Irina that night.

We were in the children's ward. Sonya was asleep with a damp cloth on her forehead. Lev was sitting up in bed, pretending not to listen, which meant he was listening to every word.

"Martin knows a great many things," said Irina.

"Martin?"

"The sergeant. His name is Martin. He was a sergeant in the army before the Devastation. Now he's a sergeant in the Clear Path. He says it's the same job with worse paperwork."

"He recognized me."

"Then why are you still alive?"

"That's what worries me."

She rolled one of her mint cigarettes without lighting it.

"Martin is the reason the children are still here," she said quietly. "When Kostya wanted to put Lev to work in the chemical warehouse, Martin said the boy was sick and would only drop the barrels. When the others wanted to move the children out to make room for wounded crewmen, Martin said a hospital without a doctor was useless and a doctor without her children would be even more useless."

"So he's protecting them."

"He's protecting his doctor. He believes in strength, Vasily. He believes the strong survive and the weak belong to them. But he also believes that a man who breaks his tools is an idiot."

"That's not exactly kindness."

"No. But it's not nothing."

***

For three days I repaired the generator.

I did it slowly and well, because I wanted the crewmen to trust me, and because I had found that I couldn't bring myself to repair a machine badly, even for the Clear Path. I cleaned the injectors one by one in a tin of diesel. I replaced the bearing shell with one I cut and filed from a piece of brass pipe. I rebuilt the exhaust so that it would stop pumping fumes into the children's ward above.

Every afternoon, Martin came down and watched me for a few minutes. He never said anything about the motorcycle again.

Every night, after the card players in the first-floor ward had fallen asleep over their crates, I went out through the loading platform to the depot.

The shunting engine was a three-hundred-horsepower diesel built forty years earlier for a railway that no longer existed. Its batteries were dead. Its fuel tank contained a centimeter of water and a family of mice. Its brakes had seized, its cab door had rusted shut, and somebody had stolen the driver's seat.

I loved it immediately.

I took the batteries from one of the armored vehicles in the courtyard. That was the riskiest thing I did, and I did it at three in the morning while the coughing guard was relieving himself behind the gate. I carried them across the tracks one at a time, wrapped in a blanket, and I have never in my life been so grateful for a man's weak bladder.

I drained the water from the fuel tank. I filled it with the diesel Martin had brought me for the generator — "lots" of it, as promised, in six large cans that the crewmen carried down to the basement with a great deal of complaining.

I unseized the brakes with penetrating oil, patience and a hammer.

I didn't find a seat. I decided I would stand.

On the third night, I connected the batteries, primed the fuel system, opened the throttle a few millimeters and pressed the starter.

The engine turned.

It turned again, slowly, groaning.

Then it caught, coughed, belched a cloud of black smoke into the depot, and settled into a deep, uneven rumble that I could feel through the soles of my boots.

I shut it off immediately.

In the silence afterward, I stood in the dark cab with my hands on the controls and laughed until my eyes watered.

***

We left on the fourth night.

Irina had prepared everything. The children were dressed in every piece of clothing they owned. Sonya, who was too weak to walk, was wrapped in two blankets and tied to Irina's back with a sheet. Misha, the smallest, was assigned to me. The twins held hands and refused to let go. Lev carried a bag of medical supplies that was almost as large as he was, and refused to let anyone else carry it.

"He's eleven," Irina said. "Let him be useful. It's the only thing they've taught him that's worth keeping."

At midnight I finished my work on the generator.

I started it. It ran beautifully — smooth, quiet, steady. The crewmen in the first-floor ward cheered. One of them gave me a cigarette, which I put in my pocket for Irina.

At one in the morning, the card players fell asleep.

At one-thirty, we went down the service staircase.

We almost made it.

***

Kostya was standing on the loading platform.

He had the sawed-off shotgun in one hand and a flashlight in the other, and he was examining the door I had cut open. He must have come down to relieve himself in the nettles, like everyone else in the building, and found the weld broken.

The beam of his flashlight swung up the corridor and found us: a doctor with a sick child on her back, a mechanic holding a three-year-old, five children in a frightened line.

For a moment, nobody moved.

Then Kostya smiled.

"The drifter," he said. "I knew it. I told the sergeant. I knew I'd seen your face."

"Kostya," said Irina. "Put that down. You'll frighten the children."

"Shut up, doctor. You're not the one in charge anymore."

He raised the shotgun.

I didn't think. I turned so that Misha was behind me, which is the only brave thing I did that night and probably the only brave thing I have ever done.

"Kostya."

The voice came from the staircase behind us.

Martin stood on the bottom step. He wasn't holding a weapon. His hands were in the pockets of his military jacket.

"Put it down," he said.

"Sergeant, they're running! The doctor and the drifter, they're stealing the kids—"

"I can see what they're doing."

"Then let me—"

"Put it down, Kostya. You'll hit a child."

"They're not children, they're debt! You said it yourself, every man pays what he owes—"

"I said put it down."

Martin stepped off the staircase and walked toward the platform. He walked slowly, the way he walked everywhere, as if he had decided long ago that hurrying made people stupid.

He walked past me. Past Irina. He stopped between the children and the shotgun.

"Go to bed, Kostya. Tomorrow you can report me to whoever you like."

For a second, I thought it would work.

Then Kostya's face changed. It was the face of a man who has been told what to do once too often and has suddenly discovered that he is holding the gun.

"No," he said.

He fired.

The noise was enormous in the narrow corridor. Martin was thrown backward against the wall. He slid down it and sat on the floor, looking surprised, with both hands pressed to his side.

Polina screamed.

Kostya stared at what he had done. For a fraction of a second, he looked very young.

Then Lev hit him in the knee with the bag of medical supplies.

It was not a strong blow. But it was unexpected, and Kostya stumbled, and his foot slipped on the edge of the platform among the wet nettles, and he fell backward onto the rails with a howl of pain and rage. The shotgun skittered away into the dark.

"Go!" I shouted. "Go, go, go!"

Upstairs, somebody was yelling. Lights were coming on.

We ran.

Irina reached the engine first. I lifted the children into the cab one after another. Lev climbed in by himself, still clutching his bag.

Then Irina did something I didn't expect.

She turned around and ran back toward the platform.

"Irina!"

"Help me!" she shouted. "Vasily, help me, damn you!"

She was kneeling beside Martin.

He was still conscious. His face was grey. He looked at her with an expression of mild irritation.

"Leave me, doctor."

"Be quiet."

"They'll be here in a minute."

"Then stop wasting it."

I ran back. Together we got him to his feet. He was heavy and he was bleeding onto both of us, and every step he took, he swore in a low, steady, professional voice, as if he were reading a list.

We heaved him into the cab.

I took the controls. There was no seat. I didn't need one.

I opened the throttle.

The engine roared. Black smoke filled the depot. The wheels slipped, caught, slipped again, and then the old locomotive lurched forward, dragging itself out of the weeds and onto the spur line beneath the hospital wall.

A rifle cracked behind us. The bullet rang off the hood.

We passed the loading platform. Kostya was lying on the tracks, clutching his knee and screaming at us. I saw his face in the glow of the cab light for a moment. Then he was gone, and the hospital was behind us, and the depot, and the white arrow on its black circle.

We joined the main line a kilometer later.

I opened the throttle all the way.

***

We traveled south for three hours.

The engine ran rough, and the rails were in terrible condition, and twice I had to stop and clear fallen branches from the line while Irina stood guard in the cab with Kostya's shotgun, which Lev had picked up from the platform without anybody noticing.

"You're keeping that?" I asked him.

"Somebody has to," said Lev.

In the back of the cab, Irina worked on Martin by the light of my flashlight. She had cut his jacket open. The shot had torn through his side above the hip. She packed the wound with gauze from Lev's bag and held it there with both hands.

"Will he live?" I asked.

"If he stops talking."

"I'm not talking," Martin said.

"You're breathing loudly. It's distracting."

He laughed and then groaned.

"Why did you do it?" I asked him. "In the corridor."

He was silent for a while.

"Kostya was going to hit the children," he said finally. "Bad for business."

"That's not why."

He opened his eyes and looked at me.

"No," he agreed, and closed them again.

***

Just before dawn, the line came to an end.

We saw it from a long way off. A river crossed the valley ahead of us, and the railway bridge that had once spanned it now ended halfway across, in a jagged edge of concrete and twisted steel. The rest of it lay in the water below.

I brought the engine to a stop thirty meters from the edge.

For a while, nobody spoke.

Sonya was coughing again. The twins had fallen asleep against each other. Misha was asleep on my jacket. Martin's breathing had become shallow and fast.

"How far are we from the valley?" asked Irina.

I checked the map.

"Thirty kilometers. Maybe a little more."

"Can we walk it?"

I looked at the children. At Martin. At Irina, who had not slept in two days.

"No," I said.

She nodded, as if she had expected nothing else, and began rolling a cigarette with bloody fingers.

I climbed down from the cab and walked to the end of the broken bridge. The river was wide and brown and fast. On the far bank, a dirt road wound up through the trees.

I sat on a block of concrete and put my head in my hands.

Then I heard an engine.

Not one engine. Two.

The first I recognized immediately: an old touring motorcycle with an uneven idle and a fuel line that I had fixed, and which had apparently started to block again. The second was deeper, a heavy pickup truck with a diesel engine that needed a new muffler.

They came down the road on the far bank, stopped at the water's edge, and switched off.

A man in a black leather jacket climbed off the motorcycle and stood looking at the broken bridge, at the locomotive, and at me.

Even from across the river, I could see that he was smiling.

"Vasya!" Robert shouted. "You were supposed to bring back a doctor! Not a train!"

"I brought both!"

"I can see that!"

The driver of the pickup got out.

He was enormous. Taller than Robert by a head, broad, slow-moving, with a rifle slung across his back and a baseball cap that had once been red. He looked at the river, then at the locomotive, then at me.

"Huh," he said. His voice carried easily across the water, deep and calm, with an accent I had only heard in old American films. "Never seen that before."

"Who's that?" I called.

"This is Kennet," Robert called back. "He found me while you were away. He has a truck, a rope and a boat."

"A boat?"

"A small one," said Kennet.

"Is that enough?"

The big man considered the river.

"Well," he said. "I guess we'll find out."
