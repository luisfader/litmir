# Chapter 20: Visitors

The journalists arrived on Thursday, which nobody could have planned, and which Luis treated as though he had planned it himself.

Kennet saw them first from the central tower: a small convoy on the north road, two cars and a minibus with its windows intact, which in itself was remarkable. On the side of the minibus, somebody had painted in neat white letters: *THE SURFACE HERALD — TRUTH FROM ABOVE*.

Behind it came a horse-drawn cart full of people from the settlements on the main road, and a man on a bicycle carrying a typewriter.

"Who are they?" I asked.

"Writers," said Dima, who had been listening to the radio all morning. "From everywhere. They heard about the walls. And the train. And the siege." He grinned. "We're famous, Vasya."

"I don't want to be famous."

"Too late."

***

There were eleven of them.

Five came from settlements within two hundred kilometers: a schoolteacher who printed a weekly newsletter on a hand-cranked press, a former radio presenter who now traveled between camps reading the news aloud, two young women who collected stories for what they called *the archive of the new world*, and the man with the typewriter, who said he was writing a history and wouldn't tell anybody of what.

The other six came from Necrologia.

They stepped out of the minibus in clean clothes and polished shoes, blinking in the sunlight as though they hadn't seen it in some time, which was probably true. There was an editor, a writer, two photographers with cameras that actually worked, a woman with a tape recorder, and a young man whose only job, as far as I could tell, was to carry an umbrella over the editor's head.

The editor was a soft, smiling man of about fifty in a cream-colored suit. He looked up at the turquoise walls and took off his sunglasses.

"Extraordinary," he said. "Absolutely extraordinary. Who is responsible for this?"

Luis was already walking down the north ramp toward him.

"I am," he said.

***

He gave them the tour himself.

He showed them the walls, the towers, the bell. He showed them the north gate and the three rules carved above it, and the fourth line beneath, and he stood in front of it while the photographers took his picture. He showed them the western wall, the one he had finished in a single day. He showed them the clinic from the outside, and the greenhouses from the outside, and the school from the outside.

He talked the whole time.

He talked about the snowstorm in which he had arrived, and the catapults he had designed for the Wild, and how he had come to the valley to atone for them. He talked about the siege, and how he had run out through the gate to warn the boys about the pins. He talked about the coup, and the secret room, and the concert, and how the assembly had asked him to serve as Steward in the dark hour.

He didn't lie. Not once. Everything he said was true.

He simply arranged it so that everything had happened because of him.

"And Robert?" asked the woman with the tape recorder. "We heard there was a man called Robert. A foreigner. The founder."

Luis smiled sadly.

"Robert," he said. "Yes. A dreamer. A good man. He had the idea, years ago. But he's been very ill. He was wounded in the duel. He's still recovering." He paused, as if choosing his words with great care. "Between ourselves, I'm not sure he'll ever be quite the same."

The woman wrote it down.

I was standing three meters away.

I turned around and walked to the clinic.

***

Robert was sitting on the bench outside Irina's office in the sun, with his stick across his knees. Natalia was beside him. Irina was standing in the doorway with her arms folded.

"He's telling them you're finished," I said.

Robert didn't seem surprised.

"Is he?"

"Not in those words. In nicer words." I sat down heavily on the step. "They're writing down every one of them. They'll print it in Necrologia. *The founder is broken. The architect is the real genius of Our Utopia.*"

"Is that untrue?" asked Robert mildly.

"Robert!"

"The architect *is* a genius. You've said so yourself."

"And the founder isn't broken."

Robert looked at his stick.

"I'm a little broken," he said.

Natalia stood up.

"Get up," she said.

Robert looked at her.

"Natalia—"

"Get up, Robert. You can walk. Irina says you can walk if you go slowly." She held out her hand. "They want to see Our Utopia. Luis is showing them a building. Let's show them the rest."

***

We did it the slow way.

Robert walked with his stick, and Natalia walked beside him, and I walked behind them, because by then it had become my job to walk behind Robert. We didn't go to the walls. We didn't go to the gate. We went to the people.

We started in the clinic.

Irina showed the woman with the tape recorder her two rooms, the stove, the shelves of jars, the table where she had operated on Martin and Robert and fifteen of the Wild. She showed them the willow bark drying by the window and the little garden of medicinal herbs Daria had planted for her outside. She showed them Sonya, who was now eleven and helping her sort bandages, and who told the journalists, with great seriousness, that she was going to be a surgeon.

"Where did you learn medicine?" asked the woman with the tape recorder.

"From her," said Sonya, pointing at Irina. "And from being dead. Almost."

The woman stopped writing.

We went to the fields.

Daria showed them the beans growing up the corn, the squash spreading underneath, the wheat on the flat land near the river. She showed them the seed vault Tolya had dug into the hillside, cool and dry, with four hundred jars on its shelves. She told them about her husband, who had collected the seeds for forty years and been extremely irritating about being right.

"And now they're growing," she said. "Every one of them. In the soil, where they belong. Not in jars."

One of the photographers took her picture. She glared at the camera until he lowered it.

We went to the school.

Anna Petrovna's children recited the three rules for the journalists, in unison, and then argued with each other about what the third rule meant, loudly, until Anna Petrovna made them stop.

"They argue about everything," she said apologetically.

"Is that a problem?" asked the editor, who had wandered over from Luis's tour.

Anna Petrovna considered this.

"No," she said. "I think that's the point."

We went to the storehouse.

Sasha showed them her ledger. Two years of entries. Every potato, every nail, every liter of diesel. On the first page, the rule about food. On the last page, a column of figures that showed the valley had produced, that summer, three times as much food as it needed.

"So you're rich," said the editor.

Sasha looked at him.

"We're fed," she said. "That's different."

And then, at the end, we went to the river.

Kennet was there, sitting on a rock beside the ford with Eleanor the chicken in his lap. Eleanor was quite old by then, and slept most of the day.

"Who's this?" asked the woman with the tape recorder.

"Eleanor," said Kennet. "She's our first citizen."

"A chicken?"

"Got here before most of the humans." He scratched the chicken's head gently. "She's never voted. But she's never complained about it, either."

The woman laughed. It was the first time I had heard any of the Necrologia journalists laugh at something that wasn't Luis's joke.

Robert sat down on a rock beside Kennet. He was grey with exhaustion. Natalia sat beside him.

The woman with the tape recorder sat on the grass in front of them.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Of course."

"Mr. Herrera says that you had the idea, and he built it. Is that true?"

Robert thought about it.

"Partly," he said.

"Which part?"

"I had an idea," said Robert. "A very small one. That nobody should own another person. That nobody should go hungry while there's food. That everybody should have a voice." He looked out over the valley. "Luis built the walls. Maksim built the foundations. Vasya fixed the generator and stole a train. Irina built the clinic. Daria planted the fields. Sasha counted everything. Kennet carried whatever needed carrying." He smiled. "And three hundred people built the rest. Every day. By arguing about it."

"So who built Our Utopia?"

"Nobody," said Robert. "Everybody. That's the only way it works."

The woman looked at him for a long time.

"That's not a very good headline," she said.

"No," Robert agreed. "That's why it's true."

The photographer raised his camera. Robert didn't notice. He was looking at the river.

The shutter clicked.

***

The assembly met on Sunday, as it should have.

We had the signatures. We had more than the signatures. By Saturday night, Sasha had collected a hundred and sixty-two names in her ledger, more than half the adults in the valley. Some signed because they wanted Robert back. Some signed because they didn't like the carving over the gate. Some signed because they had been at the concert, and had raised their hands in the dark, and had felt, the next morning, a vague unease they couldn't explain.

And some signed simply because Sasha asked them, and nobody had ever successfully refused Sasha anything.

Luis didn't try to stop it.

That surprised me. I had expected him to argue, to call it illegal, to send his six guards to the storehouse. Instead, when Sasha brought the ledger to the council room on Saturday afternoon and laid it on the table in front of him, he read every name, slowly. Then he closed it and handed it back.

"Of course," he said. "The rules are the rules."

He even smiled.

The journalists stayed to watch.

It was done properly. Every adult in the valley came to the courtyard. Each of them took a pebble from a bucket. Each of them dropped it into one of the clay jars that Tolya had made, one jar for each name, in front of everybody. Sasha counted the pebbles, jar by jar, aloud, with Dima writing down the totals. Then Irina counted them again. Then Sasha counted them a third time, because she said she liked to be sure.

It took four hours.

When it was over, Sasha stood up on the stone platform and read the results.

The council of Our Utopia: Robert. Irina. Daria. Kennet. Anna Petrovna. Sasha.

And Luis.

He had come seventh. Seventh of fourteen candidates. Enough to keep his seat, but only just.

"And the office of Steward?" asked somebody in the crowd.

Sasha looked at her notes.

"Abolished," she said. "Two hundred and nineteen to ninety-three."

The courtyard was very quiet.

Then Robert stood up.

He stood up slowly, with his stick, from the bench at the end of the platform where Irina had insisted he sit. He looked out over the faces. Then he looked at Luis, standing at the other end of the platform in his long black coat.

"Thank you," he said. "To everybody. To Sasha, for counting. To all of you, for voting." He paused. "And to Luis, for keeping this place standing while I couldn't."

He held out his hand.

Luis looked at it for a long moment.

Then he took it.

They shook hands in front of the whole valley. The photographers' shutters clicked. Somebody started clapping. Then everybody.

Luis was smiling.

He was still smiling when he let go of Robert's hand and walked down the steps from the platform, through the applauding crowd, and across the courtyard to where the journalists from Necrologia were packing their cameras into the minibus.

***

Most of the journalists left that evening.

The five from the settlements on the main road went first, on their cart, with their newsletters and their archives and the man with the typewriter pedaling behind them. Then the editor in the cream suit, with the young man and his umbrella, in one of the cars.

But the woman with the tape recorder and one of the photographers stayed.

They stayed for two more days, as Luis's guests, in the guest room he had built in the western tower. They walked with him every morning along the walls. They ate with him every evening in the council room. And they listened to him for hours.

I know, because on the second night I went up to the western tower to fix a broken lamp, and I stood on the staircase outside the guest room door, and I heard him talking.

"...of course, the walls are only the beginning," he was saying. "There's a shelter under the quarry. Seven chambers, carved out of the limestone. Enough room for the whole population. Water. Ventilation. Food stores. Nothing above ground could reach it."

"And the seeds?" asked the woman.

"Four hundred varieties. The only collection of its kind left on the surface. And the fields — you saw them. Three times what we need. We could feed a city."

"And Robert?"

There was a pause.

"Robert," said Luis gently, "is a saint. A dreamer. We all love him. But you saw him on Sunday. He can barely stand." Another pause. "And he refuses to defend what we've built. He refuses to buy weapons. He refuses to make alliances. He believes that if we're kind enough, the world will leave us alone." He laughed softly. "The world has never left anything alone that was worth taking."

"So what does Our Utopia need?" asked the woman.

"Protection," said Luis. "A friend. Someone powerful. Someone who understands that a place like this is too valuable to be left defended by votes."

"Someone like the Directorate?"

Luis didn't answer. I could imagine him smiling.

"I've said too much," he said. "Please don't print that."

I heard the click of the tape recorder.

The woman said, "Of course not."

***

They left on the third morning.

Luis walked them to the gate. He stood on the north ramp in his long black coat, under the carving with his name, and waved as the minibus drove away along the narrow road between the river and the hill.

Then he turned around and saw me watching him from the wall.

He looked up at me for a long moment.

"Vasya," he said pleasantly. "Did you fix the lamp?"

"Yes."

"Good." He climbed the ramp. When he reached the top, he stopped beside me and looked out at the road, where the dust from the minibus was still settling. "They'll print a wonderful story. You'll see. Everybody in Necrologia will be talking about Our Utopia."

"About you."

"About us." He smiled. "Isn't that what we wanted? To be seen?"

"Robert never wanted to be seen."

"No," said Luis. "That's why nobody will ever remember him."

He walked away along the wall.

I stood there for a long time, watching the empty road.

I thought about the tape recorder. About the words *too valuable to be left defended by votes*. About a woman in Necrologia, in clean clothes and polished shoes, typing them up for the next edition of a newspaper read by people who ate oysters grown in tanks.

And I thought about the photographer by the river.

About the click of his shutter, and the picture he had taken that nobody had asked for. A tired man on a rock beside a ford, with a stick across his knees and a chicken asleep beside him, looking out at a valley he had built with three hundred other people.

I wondered who would see it.
