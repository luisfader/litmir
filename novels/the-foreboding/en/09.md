# Chapter 9: Turquoise Walls

That summer, the valley learned to feed itself.

It didn't happen gracefully. It happened in mud and argument and blisters, and in Daria Sokolova walking along the riverbank every morning at five o'clock with her long grey coat and her hoe, shouting at people who were planting things in the wrong place.

"Beans with corn! Not beside it! *With* it! The beans climb the corn. The corn shades the beans. Did nobody listen to anything I said yesterday?"

"We listened," said one of the Brookfield men wearily. "We just didn't understand."

"Then ask! Asking is free! Ignorance is extremely expensive!"

She divided the land along the river into fields. She knew which soil was good and which was sour, where the water would collect and where it would drain away. She planted potatoes on the slope, wheat on the flat ground near the river, beans and squash and corn together in long rows that looked chaotic and turned out to be perfectly organized. She built the first greenhouse herself, out of window frames salvaged from the ruined farmhouse, and refused all help until Kennet simply picked up the frame she was struggling with and held it in place while she nailed it.

"I didn't ask for assistance," she said.

"I know," said Kennet. "I'm just standing here."

By the end of June, the first potatoes were in flower.

By the end of July, we ate them.

***

Sasha ran the stores.

I don't know how else to describe it. Within a week of our arrival, she had taken over the old workshop's storage shed, built shelves out of pallets, and begun recording everything that came into the valley and everything that went out. Every sack of flour. Every tin of fish. Every potato, every nail, every liter of diesel.

She kept the records in a ledger she had found in the ruins of a bank. On the first page she had written, in capital letters, *NO ONE GOES HUNGRY WHILE FOOD CAN BE SHARED*, and underneath, in smaller letters, *but no one takes more than they need, either.*

"Who decides what people need?" Robert asked her.

"I do."

"Is that fair?"

"No. But it's accurate."

Robert considered this.

"Perhaps," he said, "we should have a council decide."

"Perhaps the council should learn to count first."

***

The council happened anyway.

There were a hundred and twenty people in the valley by August, and they had stopped fitting around a single fire. Robert proposed that every adult should vote to choose seven people who would meet once a week, hear complaints, settle disputes, and make the decisions that couldn't wait for everybody.

"Seven people with power," said Martin. "That's how it starts."

"Seven people who can be replaced every three months," said Robert. "That's how it continues."

"And if they don't want to be replaced?"

"Then the other hundred and thirteen replace them anyway."

Martin snorted.

"You have a great deal of faith in a hundred and thirteen people."

"I have a great deal of faith in rules that nobody can change alone."

They voted.

It took a whole afternoon. Dima made ballots out of pages from an old telephone directory, and Sasha counted them twice. The results were announced at sunset beside the river.

Robert. Irina. Daria. Sasha. Kennet. A Brookfield schoolteacher named Anna Petrovna.

And Martin.

He stood very still when he heard his name. Then he looked around at the faces turned toward him — Brookfield people, refugees from the forest, the family who had thought Robert was a prophet.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because you're the only one who stands guard every night," said Anna Petrovna. "We thought you should have a say in what you're guarding."

Martin opened his mouth. Then he closed it again.

That night, for the first time, he didn't take his stick to the watch. He took the rifle Kennet had given him.

"So you're staying," I said when I found him on the hillside.

"Somebody has to keep your prophet alive."

"He isn't a prophet."

"No," said Martin, looking down at the tents and the fires and the river. "That's what worries me."

***

Maksim arrived at the end of August, on a horse borrowed from Granny Zoya.

He spent a week walking. He walked every meter of the valley. He walked up and down the quarry, along the river, around the hill, and through the forest on the eastern slope. He carried a notebook, a tape measure, a level, and a long steel rod that he kept pushing into the ground and pulling out again, frowning at whatever it told him.

Robert followed him around like a student.

"Well?" he asked at the end of the week.

"Your hill is good," said Maksim. "Solid rock under a meter of soil. You can build on it. Your river is good. Clean water, steady flow. Your quarry is excellent. Limestone. Easy to cut, very hard once it cures."

"So we can build the walls."

"You can build foundations." Maksim opened his notebook. "Here. And here. A circuit of two kilometers around the hilltop, following the contour. Four meters deep on the eastern side, because the ground is weaker there. Two meters everywhere else."

"And then?"

"And then you find an architect."

"Why can't you design the walls?"

"Because I build dams," said Maksim. "A dam has one job. It stands still and holds back water. Water doesn't climb, doesn't think, doesn't bring ladders or explosives or catapults. Your walls will need towers, gates, firing positions, drainage, roads. They'll need to be built so that people can live inside them for a hundred years and not go mad." He closed the notebook. "That's not engineering. That's architecture."

"Luis Herrera," said Robert.

"Luis Herrera."

They looked east, toward the hills.

***

We dug the foundations in September.

The whole valley worked on them. Men, women, teenagers. Even the children carried water and small stones. Maksim stood at the top of the trench with his level and his notebook and shouted at people, more quietly than Daria but with exactly the same effect.

We cut stone from the quarry. We hauled it up the hill on sledges pulled by Granny Zoya's horse and Kennet's truck and, on one memorable afternoon, by forty people on a single rope while Anna Petrovna sang to keep the rhythm.

The two young men from Raven Dam turned out to be very good at mixing lime mortar.

By the end of the month, a ring of grey stone surrounded the hilltop, rising half a meter above the ground.

It wasn't a wall. It wasn't even close.

But you could stand inside it and see where the wall would be.

***

The tiles were my fault.

In October, Kennet and I drove south to look for fuel. We didn't find any. What we found instead, in a valley thirty kilometers away, was an abandoned ceramics factory with a faded sign over its gate: *LAZURIT. ARCHITECTURAL CERAMICS. EST. 1962.*

Inside, in a warehouse whose roof had partially collapsed, stood pallets of tiles.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Glazed, thick, heavy, the size of a man's hand. Some were broken. Most were perfect, still wrapped in brittle paper and stacked in crates.

They were all the same color.

Turquoise. Not blue, not green. The color of shallow water over white sand. The color of a sky in a picture painted by a child who has never seen a real one.

Kennet picked one up and turned it over in his hand.

"Huh," he said.

"A hotel chain ordered them," I said, reading a delivery note I found pinned to a crate. "For a resort on the coast. Five hundred square meters. The order was cancelled two weeks before the Devastation."

"So nobody wants them."

"Nobody wants them."

Kennet looked at the tile. Then at me.

"Robert's walls," he said.

"They're for bathrooms. Swimming pools."

"They're waterproof. Frostproof. Hard as hell." He knocked the tile against a steel beam. It rang like a bell. "You put these on the outside of a stone wall, the rain runs off. The ice doesn't crack it. It'll last forever."

"It'll look ridiculous."

"It'll look," said Kennet, "like nothing anybody's ever seen."

We brought back one truckload. Then another. By November, there was a mountain of turquoise tiles at the foot of the hill, wrapped in tarpaulins against the rain.

Robert came down to look at them.

He picked one up. He held it to the light.

He didn't say anything for a long time.

"My wife," he said finally, "always said she wanted to live by the sea."

It was the first time he had ever mentioned a wife.

None of us asked.

***

The flag was Anna Petrovna's idea.

The schoolchildren made it: thirty children from Brookfield and Pinefield and the forest, working for two weeks under her supervision in the farmhouse. They cut it from a turquoise bedsheet that Sasha released from the stores after a heated argument with herself. They sewed three white stripes across it, one for each rule.

On the first day of winter, they carried it up the hill.

Everybody came. Daria and her fields. Maksim, who had stayed three weeks longer than the agreed month and was pretending he hadn't noticed. Irina and her clinic, which now had glass in its windows and a stove. Sasha and her ledger. Dima and his radio. Martin, at the top of the hill, with his rifle.

And Robert, standing beside the pole, with the notebook in his pocket that held seven drawings of a wall that didn't exist yet.

Lev raised the flag.

He was twelve now. He had grown seven centimeters since the hospital, and he had insisted, with great seriousness, that he should be the one, because he had been on the train.

Nobody argued.

The wind caught the turquoise sheet and spread it out above the valley. Three white stripes. One for each rule.

Somebody started clapping. Then everybody.

I looked at Robert.

He wasn't clapping. He was looking east.

I followed his gaze.

On the ridge of the eastern hills, about two kilometers away, three figures sat motionless on motorcycles, black against the white winter sky.

They were watching us.

When I looked again, they were gone.

***

That night, the council met in the farmhouse.

"The Wild," said Martin. "Scouts. They've been watching us for weeks."

"How do you know?" asked Irina.

"Tracks on the eastern slope. Fires on the ridge at night. Somebody took two sheep from the Brookfield flock last Tuesday and left the gate open behind them, very politely." He leaned forward. "They're counting us. Counting our food, our fuel, our guns. And when they've finished counting, they'll come."

"When?" asked Robert.

"Spring. When the roads dry out. They won't attack in winter. They'll wait until we've planted, so they can take the harvest as well."

"Then we have until spring."

"To do what? Build two kilometers of wall?"

"No," said Robert.

He opened his notebook. He turned past the seven drawings of the wall, past the plans for the clinic and the school and the gardens, past the page with the three rules and the page with Robert's list.

On the last page, in pencil, he had written a single name.

*Luis Herrera.*

"To find the man," he said, "who knows how to build one."

Martin stared at him.

"He lives with the Wild."

"Yes."

"In the eastern hills."

"Yes."

"With the people who are planning to attack us in the spring."

"Exactly," said Robert. "So he'll know what they're planning, as well."

Martin looked at Kennet. Kennet looked at me.

I looked at the ceiling.

"I'll get my motorcycle," I said.

But it turned out we didn't need it.

Because three days later, before any of us had left the valley, Luis Herrera came to us.
