# Chapter 16: The Secret Room

Martin didn't shout. He never shouted.

He waited until the courtyard was full, until three hundred people were standing in front of the central tower, looking at the rifles in the hands of the men behind him. Then he climbed three steps of the tower staircase, so that everybody could see him, and spoke in the same calm, measured voice he used to assign the night watches.

"Nobody is going to be hurt," he said. "I want to say that first. Nobody is going to be hurt. Please stay where you are."

Nobody moved.

"Last night," said Martin, "I made an agreement with the Clear Path. Diesel and medicine, every month, in exchange for peace and eight rifles. I made it alone, without asking the council, because if I had asked the council, they would still be discussing it next spring." He paused. "I know that this breaks the rules. I know what the assembly decided three days ago. I was there."

"Then why?" called Anna Petrovna from the crowd.

"Because the rules were written for a world that doesn't exist yet." Martin looked out over the faces. "Robert's world. A world where everybody is reasonable, and everybody shares, and nobody comes over the hill with a gun. I want that world. I want it more than you'll ever know. But we don't live in it. We live in this one. And in this one, the Clear Path is counting our diesel, the Wild are counting our rifles, and somebody is always counting how long it takes us to agree."

He took a breath.

"So from today, until the threat has passed, there will be a Defense Council. Five people. Myself, Zhenya, Fyodor, and two others chosen by them. The Defense Council will make all decisions concerning weapons, guards, supplies and the security of Our Utopia. The ordinary council will continue to meet about everything else. Schools. Fields. The clinic." He looked at Irina. "Nobody will touch the clinic."

"And when does the threat pass?" called Daria.

Martin was silent for a moment.

"When it passes," he said.

***

Robert walked out of the crowd.

He walked slowly, the way he always walked, with his hands in the pockets of his black leather jacket. He walked past Zhenya's people, past the rifles, until he was standing at the foot of the staircase, looking up at Martin.

The courtyard was so silent I could hear the river.

"Martin," he said.

"Robert."

"You don't have to do this."

"Somebody does."

"No. Nobody does. That's the whole point." Robert looked up at him. "You're right about almost everything you said. We're slow. We argue. We make mistakes. Kennet nearly died because of a vote. I'll carry that for the rest of my life." He paused. "But you're wrong about one thing."

"Tell me."

"You think the threat will pass. It won't. There will always be a Clear Path. There will always be a Grom. And there will always be a reason why five people with rifles should decide for three hundred people without them. And every time, it will feel temporary. And every time, it won't be."

"So what's your solution? Vote while they shoot at us?"

"Yes," said Robert. "If that's what it takes."

Martin looked down at him for a long time.

"Then you'll die," he said quietly. "And so will they."

"Maybe."

"I won't let that happen."

"It isn't yours to let."

Something changed in Martin's face. It wasn't anger. It was closer to grief.

"I'm sorry, Robert," he said.

He nodded to Zhenya.

"Take him to his house. Keep him there. Gently."

***

It wasn't gentle.

I don't think Martin meant it to be anything else. But Zhenya's people were young, and frightened, and holding new rifles, and when two of them took Robert by the arms, three Brookfield men stepped forward to stop them. One of Zhenya's boys swung his rifle butt. A Brookfield man went down with blood on his face.

Then everything happened at once.

Somebody screamed. Somebody fired a shot into the air. The crowd surged forward, then backward. Children were crying in the farmhouse windows. Daria was shouting in Latin. Anna Petrovna was knocked to the ground, and Irina was running toward her with her bag, and Fyodor was standing in front of the storehouse door with his rifle raised, shouting that nobody was touching the food.

In the middle of it all, Martin stood on the staircase with his arms raised, shouting for calm.

Nobody was listening to him anymore.

I was trying to reach Robert when a hand closed on my arm.

"This way," said Luis. "Now. Quickly."

He had Natalia by the other hand. Her face was white.

"Where's Robert?"

"Here."

Robert was behind him. Somehow, in the chaos, he had pulled free of Zhenya's boys. There was a cut on his forehead, bleeding down into one eyebrow. He was looking back at the courtyard.

"I can't leave them."

"You can and you will," said Luis. "If you stay, Zhenya's boys will take you, and if anybody tries to stop them, someone will die. Do you want that?"

"No."

"Then come with me."

He pulled us along the base of the wall, behind the stacks of building stone, to the foot of the central tower. He stopped in front of a section of stonework that looked exactly like every other section of stonework. Grey limestone, turquoise tiles in a pattern of waves.

He pressed one of the tiles.

Nothing happened.

He swore in Spanish, pressed a different tile, and the wall moved.

***

The room was exactly as he had described it a year earlier.

Four meters by three, hidden inside the thickness of the wall. A ventilation shaft that let in a faint grey light from somewhere above. A cistern of water. Four folded cots, a box of candles, a box of tinned food, a first aid kit.

Luis pulled the door closed behind us. It slid into place with a soft click.

The noise from the courtyard disappeared.

For a moment, nobody spoke. We stood in the dim light, breathing hard. Natalia was shaking. Robert had his hand pressed against the cut on his forehead.

Luis lit a candle.

"Every fortress has one," he said. His voice was trembling slightly. "For the moment when everything goes wrong."

"You said that before," I said.

"I'm a man of few ideas. I like to repeat them."

He opened the first aid kit and handed Robert a bandage.

Robert took it without looking at it.

"I have to go back," he said.

"Not yet."

"Luis—"

"Not *yet*." Luis turned to face him. In the candlelight, his face was hard. "Listen to me. Right now, outside, there are forty frightened people with rifles who think they've just started a revolution, and three hundred frightened people without rifles who think they're about to be shot. If you walk out there now, one of the first group will panic and shoot you, or one of the second group will try to save you and get shot. Either way, Martin loses control. And then it's not a coup. It's a massacre."

"So what do we do?"

"We wait. An hour. Two. Until the shouting stops. Until Martin has calmed his children down and realized what he's done." Luis sat down on one of the cots. "And then you go out. Alone. And you talk to him."

Robert stood still in the middle of the tiny room.

Then, slowly, he sat down on the cot opposite Luis.

"How did you know?" he asked. "That we'd need this room."

Luis laughed tiredly.

"I didn't. I hoped we wouldn't." He looked at the candle. "I built a hall for Grom once. With a fireplace big enough for a pig. And behind the fireplace, a room exactly like this one. He never knew it was there. I used it twice. Once when Grom's brother tried to kill him. Once when Grom tried to kill me." He shrugged. "Architecture is mostly about anticipating disappointment."

***

We waited.

The candle burned down slowly. Through the ventilation shaft, very faintly, we could hear the sounds of the courtyard. Shouting at first. Then less shouting. Then voices. Then, after a long time, almost nothing.

Natalia sat on the floor with her back against the wall and her arms around her knees.

She hadn't said anything since we came in.

Robert was sitting on his cot, staring at the floor. The bandage was still in his hand. He hadn't used it. Blood had dried in a dark line down the side of his face.

Natalia looked at him for a long time.

Then she got up, took the bandage from his hand, and knelt in front of him.

"Hold still," she said.

He looked at her, startled.

She cleaned the cut with water from the cistern and a corner of her dress. She did it carefully, without hurrying, the way she tuned her cello. Then she wrapped the bandage around his forehead and tied it.

"There," she said.

"Thank you."

She didn't move away. She stayed kneeling in front of him, looking at his face.

"Why do you stand on the wall every night?" she asked.

Robert didn't answer.

"Every night. Looking at the hills. I've watched you. Since the first evening." Her voice was very quiet. "What are you looking for?"

Luis stood up abruptly.

"I'll check the ventilation shaft," he said, and went to the far corner of the room, where he stood with his back to us, looking up at the grey light.

I wished very much that I could check something too.

Robert looked at Natalia for a long time.

"My wife," he said finally.

"Your wife?"

"She went south in the first week. To the army. She was a doctor." He looked down at his hands. "She said she'd come back. I've been waiting for four years."

Natalia was silent.

"I know she's dead," said Robert. "I know. Every rational part of me knows. There were no lists. No graves. No one who saw her. Four years." He laughed, very softly. "But every night I go up to the wall and I look south, and I think — maybe tonight. Maybe tonight there'll be a light on the road."

"That's why you built a fortress," said Natalia slowly. "So that she'd have somewhere to come back to."

Robert didn't answer.

"And her name," said Natalia. "It's Natalia. Isn't it?"

He looked up at her.

"How did you know?"

"Because the first day I came here, you looked at me as though you'd seen a ghost." She smiled sadly. "I'm not a ghost, Robert."

"I know."

"I'm not her."

"I know that too."

They looked at each other in the candlelight.

I studied the ceiling with great concentration.

In the corner, Luis had stopped pretending to look at the ventilation shaft. He was standing perfectly still, with his back to us.

***

After two hours, the courtyard was silent.

Robert stood up.

"I'm going out."

Luis turned around. His face was calm again, composed, the face of an architect explaining a staircase.

"Alone?"

"Alone."

"They might shoot you."

"They might." Robert touched the bandage on his forehead. "But I don't think Martin wants that. I think Martin wants somebody to tell him how to stop."

"And if you can't?"

Robert looked at him.

"Then you'll be in charge," he said. "You and Irina and Daria. Whatever's left of the council. Get people into the shelter if you have to. Keep them alive." He paused. "And keep the rules, Luis. Whatever happens to me. Keep the rules."

Luis held his gaze for a long moment.

"Of course," he said.

Robert turned to the door.

Natalia stood up.

"Robert."

He stopped.

She didn't say anything else. She just looked at him.

He nodded, as though she had said something, and pressed the tile, and the wall slid open.

Daylight flooded in.

***

I followed him.

I don't know why. He had said alone. Luis grabbed for my sleeve as I went past and missed. But I had followed Robert out of a market with a cable on my wrists, and across a broken bridge on a motorcycle, and I wasn't going to let him walk into a courtyard full of rifles without somebody who could describe it afterward.

The courtyard was nearly empty.

Most of the people had gone back to their houses, their tents, the farmhouse. The children had been taken inside. A few Brookfield men sat on the steps of the storehouse with bandaged heads, guarded by two of Zhenya's boys. Irina was in the clinic doorway with blood on her apron.

Martin was sitting on the central tower staircase, alone, with a rifle across his knees.

Zhenya was standing beside him.

When Robert came out from behind the stacks of stone, they both stood up.

Zhenya raised Kostya's shotgun.

"Don't," said Martin.

She lowered it a few centimeters.

Robert walked across the courtyard toward them. He stopped at the foot of the staircase, exactly where he had stood two hours earlier.

"Well," he said. "Here I am."

Martin looked at the bandage on his forehead.

"I'm sorry about that."

"So am I."

"I told them gently."

"I know. They didn't listen." Robert looked around at the empty courtyard. At the Brookfield men on the storehouse steps. At the clinic door. "How many hurt?"

"Eleven. Nobody badly. Anna Petrovna broke her wrist."

"Anybody dead?"

"No."

"Good." Robert looked back at him. "You've broken the foundations of Our Utopia, Martin. You've turned guns on the people who elected you. You've brought violence into the one place in this whole broken world that was built to be without it." His voice was very quiet. "Why?"

Martin didn't answer.

Zhenya did.

"Because your foundations are made of sand," she said. "Your rules are beautiful. In peace. But this isn't peace. This is war. It's always war. In the hills, we knew that. Here, you pretend." She lifted her chin. "Martin doesn't pretend."

"And who decides when it's war?" asked Robert. "You? Martin? The person holding the shotgun?"

"Someone has to."

"No," said Robert. "Everyone has to. Or no one does."

Zhenya stared at him.

Then she laughed, the way she had laughed on the steps in Grom's village a year earlier, startled and fierce.

"You're a stubborn man," she said. "Grom would have liked you. Before he killed you."

"I've been told."

Zhenya looked at Martin. Something passed between them. Some agreement, made long before this morning.

"In the hills," she said, turning back to Robert, "when two men disagree about who should lead, there's no vote. There's no assembly. They fight. One against one, in front of everybody. Whoever wins, leads. Whoever loses, accepts. Nobody else is hurt."

Robert looked at Martin.

"Is that what you want?"

Martin was silent for a long time.

"I don't want to kill you, Robert," he said finally.

"I don't want to kill you either."

"Then we won't." Martin looked out over the courtyard, at the faces beginning to appear again at the windows. "But somebody has to decide. And you're right — it can't be me, just because I have more rifles. And it can't be you, just because you wrote the rules." He took a breath. "So let them watch. Let them see. Not who's right. Who's willing."

"Willing to do what?"

Martin looked at him.

"To stand up," he said, "when it costs something."

Robert didn't answer for a long time.

Then he looked up at the council room window, high in the central tower. Through the glass, I could see the wall where Luis had hung two old cavalry sabres that Kennet had found the previous summer in the ruins of a regional museum. Luis had said they gave the room dignity. Daria had said they were dusty.

"No guns," said Robert.

"No guns," Martin agreed.

"Nobody else gets hurt. Whatever happens."

"Nobody else."

"And when it's finished," said Robert, "whoever wins, the assembly meets. And votes. On everything. On the Defense Council. On the Clear Path. On you. On me."

Martin hesitated.

Then he nodded.

"Agreed."

Robert turned around and looked at me.

"Vasya," he said. "Ring the bell."
