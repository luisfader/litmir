# Chapter 6: The First Vote

It took four trips to cross the river.

Kennet's boat was an aluminum dinghy with a dent in the bow and a small outboard motor that ran, as far as I could tell, on prayer. He tied one end of a rope to the pickup's tow hook, waded into the water up to his chest with the other end over his shoulder, and swam the rope across to me as calmly as if he were carrying groceries.

I tied it to the bridge pier.

Then we ferried them over, two or three at a time, hand over hand along the rope while the current dragged at the dinghy and tried to turn it sideways. The twins went first, because they refused to be separated. Then Polina, Tamara and Misha. Then Lev, who insisted on sitting in the bow with Kostya's shotgun across his knees like a small, serious admiral.

Irina and Sonya went fourth.

Martin went last.

He was barely conscious by then. Kennet carried him down from the cab in his arms, the way you would carry a sleeping child, and laid him in the bottom of the dinghy.

"Who's this?" he asked.

"A sergeant of the Clear Path," I said.

Kennet looked at the wound, the military jacket, and the scar.

"Friend of yours?"

"He was trying to sell me to a chemical warehouse a week ago."

"Huh," said Kennet.

"Then he got shot protecting the children."

Kennet thought about this.

"People are complicated," he said, and pushed the boat into the river.

***

We reached the valley in the middle of the afternoon.

I almost didn't recognize it.

In the two weeks I had been away, the workshop had acquired a roof — a patchwork of corrugated iron and plywood that leaked in at least three places, but a roof. The ruined farmhouse had a door. A fence of stakes and wire surrounded the vegetable plot Robert had started beside the river. There was a chicken. One chicken, brown and indignant, which Kennet had won in a card game at a settlement on the main road and which he had named Eleanor.

"After my aunt," he explained. "Same temperament."

Robert had cleared the ground floor of the farmhouse and set up four beds, salvaged from God knows where. Irina walked into the room, looked at the beds, the dusty windows, the cracked plaster and the bucket of river water in the corner, and turned to Robert.

"This is your clinic?"

"It's the beginning of one."

"It's a barn."

"It was a farmhouse."

"It's a barn with ambitions."

Robert smiled.

"So am I."

Irina stared at him for a moment. Then she put Sonya down on the nearest bed and held out her hand without looking at him.

"The antibiotics."

He gave her all three packets.

***

Sonya's fever broke on the third day.

I was sitting by the river, trying to work out how to build a water wheel for the workshop, when Lev came running down the slope, shouting. I thought something terrible had happened. I ran back with him, my heart pounding, and burst into the farmhouse to find Sonya sitting up in bed, pale and thin and furious, demanding to know why everybody kept crying.

Irina was standing by the window with her back to the room.

She didn't turn around for a long time.

When she finally did, her eyes were dry and her voice was perfectly steady.

"She needs soup," she said. "Real soup. Not water with a potato in it. Who here can cook?"

We all looked at each other.

"I can make chili," said Kennet.

"With what?"

"Usually beans and meat."

"We don't have any meat."

Kennet looked thoughtfully out of the window, at Eleanor.

"No," said Robert firmly.

***

Martin woke properly on the fifth day.

He had been drifting in and out of fever since the train, mumbling orders to people who weren't there. Irina had removed most of the shot from his side by the light of a car headlamp I connected to the generator. She had sewn him up with thread she sterilized in boiling water. She had sat with him through two nights, and when Robert offered to take her place, she told him to go and build something.

When Martin opened his eyes, the first thing he saw was Kennet, sitting beside the bed, cleaning his rifle.

"Morning," said Kennet.

Martin looked at him. Then at the room. Then at the bandages around his own waist.

"Where am I?"

"Our Utopia," said Kennet.

Martin closed his eyes again.

"God help me," he said.

***

That evening, Robert called a meeting.

It took place around the fire by the river, because the farmhouse was full of children and the workshop was full of tools. There were five of us: Robert, Irina, Kennet, myself, and Martin, who had insisted on being carried out on a stretcher because, as he said, he wasn't going to let people discuss him behind his back like a broken vehicle.

"We have to decide something," said Robert.

"What to do with me," said Martin.

"Yes."

"You can shoot me. That would be the sensible thing."

"We're not going to shoot you."

"Then you're not sensible."

"We've been accused of that before."

Robert picked up a stick and poked the fire.

"I'll tell you how I see it. Martin is wounded. While he's wounded, he stays here and Irina treats him, because nobody in Our Utopia is refused medical care. That part isn't a question. When he can walk, he decides whether he wants to stay or leave. If he leaves, we give him food and show him the road. If he stays, he lives by the same rules as everybody else."

"What rules?" asked Martin.

Robert told him.

No one owns another person. No one goes hungry while food can be shared. Every adult has a voice in the decisions of the community.

Martin listened with an expression of growing disbelief.

"That's it?"

"For now."

"Who enforces them?"

"We do."

"With what? Your big American?"

Kennet looked up from his rifle.

"I'm from Ohio," he said mildly.

"Is that a different thing?"

"Very."

Martin shook his head.

"You'll last a month. The first crew that comes down that road will take everything. Your food, your fuel, your doctor. Your children."

"Then help us stop them," said Robert.

Martin didn't answer.

"I'm not saying this is decided," Robert continued. "I'm saying this is what I think. Now I want to hear what everybody else thinks. Irina?"

Irina was smoking one of her mint cigarettes. She looked at Martin through the smoke.

"He's my patient," she said. "He stays until I discharge him. After that, I don't care whether he stays or goes, as long as he doesn't touch the children."

"I never touched the children," Martin said.

"No," said Irina. "You didn't. That's why you're still breathing."

"Kennet?"

The big man finished cleaning the bolt of his rifle and slid it back into place.

"Where I come from," he said slowly, "we had a saying. You judge a man by what he does when nobody's making him do anything." He looked at Martin. "Nobody made you stand in front of that shotgun."

Martin looked away.

"Vasya?"

I thought about the market. About the cable on the counter. About the look on Martin's face when he said, *When your debt is settled.*

Then I thought about the corridor, and the sound of the shotgun, and his voice saying, *You'll hit a child.*

"I don't trust him," I said. "But I don't think we can build a place like this if we only let in the people we trust."

Robert nodded.

"Martin?"

The sergeant stared at the fire for a long time.

"You're asking me?"

"You're an adult. You have a voice."

"I'm a prisoner."

"No. You're a patient. There's a difference."

"A considerable one," I said.

Robert gave me a look. I shrugged.

Martin's jaw tightened. When he spoke again, his voice was rough.

"Four years," he said. "Four years I've been listening to men explain freedom to me. Kostya. The others. The men above them, and the men above those. Every man owns himself. Nobody can take what is yours. And every one of them was holding a gun to somebody's head while he said it." He laughed, without humor. "And now you. No guns. No guards. Just a fire and a vote."

"Is that worse?" asked Robert.

"I don't know yet."

He was silent for a moment.

"I'll stay until I can walk," he said finally. "Then I'll decide."

"That's fair."

"And Robert?"

"Yes?"

"When they come — and they will come — you'll need somebody who's actually been in a fight. Your American can shoot. Your mechanic can build trains. Your doctor can sew. None of you know how to hold a line."

"Do you?"

"I held one for eleven years," said Martin. "Before all this."

Robert looked at him across the fire.

"Then perhaps," he said, "you'll teach us."

***

So that was the first vote of Our Utopia.

It wasn't really a vote, because nobody raised their hands and nobody counted anything. But five adults had sat around a fire and said what they thought, and one of them had been a prisoner an hour earlier and was now something else, although none of us knew exactly what.

Afterward, I sat with Kennet by the water while Robert helped Irina carry Martin back to the farmhouse.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

"Sure."

"What is a man from Ohio doing here?"

Kennet picked up a flat stone and skipped it across the river. It bounced five times.

"National Guard," he said. "Joint exercise. Six weeks, they told us. Some training, a couple of parades, a lot of bad coffee." He picked up another stone. "We'd been here eleven days when it happened."

"And you couldn't get home."

"No planes. No ships. No phones. We waited for orders for about a month. Then we stopped waiting." The second stone bounced seven times. "Most of the guys headed west, toward the coast. Thought they'd find a boat. I went with them for a while."

"Why did you stop?"

"Met a family on the road. Grandmother, mother, two kids. Their car had broken down." He shrugged. "Couldn't leave them there. By the time I got them where they were going, my unit was long gone."

"And then?"

"Then I just kept finding people who needed something carried." He smiled slightly. "Robert says that's a skill."

"Robert says that about everything."

"Yeah. I noticed."

We watched the river.

"Do you think it'll work?" I asked. "This place."

Kennet didn't answer for a while.

"Back home," he said, "my grandfather built a barn. Took him two summers, by himself, with his hands. Everybody said he was crazy. Said he should buy one of those metal ones you put together in a weekend." He skipped a third stone. "That barn's still standing. The metal ones blew down in the first big storm."

"So you think it'll work."

"I think," said Kennet, "that it depends on what we build it out of."

***

The next morning, Robert came to the workshop with his notebook under his arm.

"Sonya's eating," he said. "Martin's complaining. Irina says she needs a proper clinic before winter, with glass in the windows and a stove. And people are coming."

"What people?"

He pointed toward the road.

Two figures were walking along it toward the valley: a man and a woman, carrying everything they owned in a wheelbarrow. Behind them, at a distance, came a third, an old man leaning on a stick.

"They heard about the train," Robert said. "Apparently a locomotive stopping at a broken bridge with a doctor and seven children on board makes quite a good story."

"How many more?"

"I don't know. Kennet saw a family at the crossroads yesterday. Martin says there are at least thirty people living in the forest north of the main road."

I put down my wrench.

"We don't have room."

"No."

"We don't have food."

"No."

"We don't have walls."

"That," said Robert, opening his notebook to the page with the second name, "is why you're going to Raven Dam."

*Maksim Orlov. Civil engineer and water systems specialist.*

"Alone?"

"With Kennet. And his truck."

"What about Eleanor?"

"Eleanor stays here," said Robert. "Under Irina's protection."

I looked at the road, at the three tired figures coming down it, and at the field where Robert believed a fortress would one day stand.

For the first time, I could almost see it.

"All right," I said. "Let's go and find an engineer."
